A research guide · Church of the Firstborn, 1861 –

The Morrisites and their records

An open guide to the sources, archives, places, and scholarship on the movement Joseph Morris organized at Kington Fort on the Weber River in 1861, and on the congregations that carried it from Utah to Idaho, Nevada, California, Nebraska, Iowa, and Montana.

This is an independent scholarly resource. It is not affiliated with any church, descendant organization, or repository. Entries are being added and checked; corrections are welcome.

Essential works

The movement's own scripture, a later compilation from within the movement, and the only book-length history of it.

Primary text · 1886

The Spirit Prevails

Joseph Morris's revelations, articles, and letters, recorded from 1857 to 1862 and published after his death by his follower George S. Dove. It holds more than 300 revelations, making Morris one of the most prolific revelators in Mormon history. For his followers it was scripture.

Read online: Internet Archive · Google Books. Both are free scans of the University of California copy (684 pages).

Primary text · 1899

Gems of Inspiration

A Collection of Sublime Thoughts by Modern Prophets, compiled by John R. Eardley, who later directed the Montana church, and printed in San Francisco by Joseph A. Dove in 1899. It shows how the movement kept and passed on its prophetic writings a generation after Morris.

Read online: Internet Archive.

The standard history · three editions

C. LeRoy Anderson's history of the Morrisites

  1. 1981 For Christ Will Come Tomorrow: The Saga of the Morrisites
  2. 1988 Joseph Morris and the Saga of the Morrisites, 2nd ed.
  3. 2010 Joseph Morris and the Saga of the Morrisites (Revisited), the final edition. Digital edition

Anderson, a sociologist at the University of Montana, built the book on Morrisite papers found in a Deer Lodge attic and on the records of the Hendrikson family, among the last Morrisite leaders in Deer Lodge. His research files are the core of the Morrisite collection at USU.

Forthcoming edition

Mark H. Forscutt on Joseph Morris

Forscutt (1834–1903) was clerk of the Morrisite First Council and an apostle in Morris's church, and later a minister in the Reorganized Church. He wrote about the movement at least twice.

“Sketch of Joseph Morris,” undated

CHL MS 702 · edition in preparation
Original
Church History Library, Salt Lake City, MS 702. Manuscript, 54 leaves, in Forscutt’s hand. Catalog record and images
Copy
Morrisite collection, Utah State University, USU CAINE COLL MSS 10, box 3, folder 1
Date
Written some years after Forscutt joined the RLDS Church
Contents
An insider’s history of the movement: the setting in Utah after Joseph Smith’s death, Morris’s call and claims, the organization of the church in April 1861, its theology and revelations, its relations with the Reorganized Church, the mounting conflict with the territorial authorities, and the opening of the siege of Kington Fort on June 13, 1862, which Forscutt witnessed. The narrative ends with the first cannon shot. Utah State University’s description says the last page refers to an earlier history Forscutt wrote; this is being checked against the original.
Edition
A documentary edition, with an introductory essay and annotation, is in preparation by Eric Paul Rogers. The text is not published here in the meantime; short passages are quoted and cited elsewhere in this guide.

Series 1

Chronology

The main events, from Morris’s revelations through the Montana congregations to later claimants and the present.

  1. 1857–1862Joseph Morris, an English convert to the Latter-day Saint church, records the revelations later gathered as The Spirit Prevails. He writes repeatedly to Brigham Young. No written reply is known; Anderson reports that the first letter, dated December 7, 1857, bears a penciled note on its margin, “He’s weak minded.” The church’s answer comes in February 1861, when Wilford Woodruff and another apostle visit South Weber and Morris and his followers are excommunicated.
  2. 6 Apr 1861Morris organizes the Church of the Firstborn and calls his followers to gather at Kington Fort, an abandoned 1853 settler fort at South Weber.
  3. 1861–1862The community holds its goods in common and awaits the Second Coming. As appointed days pass, some members grow discontented and try to leave.
  4. 13–15 Jun 1862A territorial posse under Robert T. Burton besieges the fort. Morris and John Banks are killed during the surrender; eleven people die in all, including a young posse member, Jared Smith.
  5. Mar 1863In Salt Lake City, seven Morrisites are convicted of second-degree murder and sixty-six of resisting arrest. The new territorial governor, Stephen S. Harding, pardons them all three days later.
  6. May 1863Many Morrisites leave Utah under the protection of Patrick Edward Connor, commanding the District of Utah. On May 5 a party of 160 (53 families, seven single men, and four widows) sets out with a company of California Volunteers; it reaches Soda Springs on May 20, where Connor has a town site surveyed on the north bank of the Bear River beside a new military post, Camp Connor (established by order of May 23). The Morrisites call their town Morristown. Connor also gives 150 Morrisites passage to Carson City, Nevada, in an empty supply train. Others settle in California. (Connor’s report, June 2, 1863, Official Records, ser. 1, vol. 50, pt. 1, 226–29.)
  7. 1860s–Some Morrisites return to the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, in Utah and elsewhere. The 2006 monument at South Weber notes that a few were rebaptized and remained there. Others find a home in the Reorganized Church (RLDS): the former Morrisite apostle Mark Hill Forscutt joins it in Salt Lake City in 1865, and John L. Bear (Johann Lebrecht Bär, 1838–1919), a Swiss convert from Affoltern am Albis who emigrated from Zürich in 1860 and lost his wife and child at Kington Fort, later serves as an RLDS missionary to Switzerland and Germany, dies in Independence, Missouri, and publishes his autobiography in the RLDS Journal of History.
  8. 1867–1881William W. Davies leads a Morrisite offshoot, the Kingdom of Heaven, on Mill Creek near Walla Walla, Washington. He declares his infant son Arthur to be Christ returned, the “Walla Walla Jesus.” Lawsuits by former members end the community.
  9. 1869Danish-language congregations keep membership and account books in Omaha, Nebraska, and Council Bluffs, Iowa.
  10. 1870sMany members regather in the Deer Lodge Valley, Montana, under George Williams, the “Prophet Cainan,” who claims to be Morris's successor. In 1874 the Montana church is divided into two stakes.
  11. 1877Montana Morrisites begin building a wood-frame church a mile or so below Racetrack, south of Deer Lodge.
  12. 1879Robert T. Burton, indicted in 1870 for the killing of Isabella Bowman during the surrender, is tried and acquitted.
  13. Jan 1879George Williams prophesies that Deer Lodge County will be the site of the Second Coming.
  14. 1880In Chicago, Mark Hill Forscutt, a former Morrisite apostle now serving as an RLDS minister, baptizes James Brighouse (August 5) and confirms him (August 8), having baptized Mrs. Brighouse in July and preached the funeral of their infant child. In December Forscutt nominates Brighouse as a teacher in the branch. (Forscutt journal, 1880–1881, Vault MSS 811.) Four years later Brighouse founds the Order of Enoch.
  15. 1882Williams dies, and the movement splits among rival successors. The Montana church, later directed by John R. Eardley, is reorganized as the Church of Jesus Christ of Saints of the Most High; George Dove leads the California congregation.
  16. 1884A later claimant. James Brighouse, breaking from the RLDS Church, organizes the Order of Enoch in Independence, Missouri. The group rejects Brigham Young but honors Joseph Morris as a prophet and martyr. Brighouse claims to be the “One Mighty and Strong” and a reincarnation of Christ and earlier prophets, a reincarnation teaching like that of the Davies group at Walla Walla. Wikipedia describes the Order as the one Morrisite-related body that survived 1969; its later activity has not been confirmed for this guide. Brighouse had been brought into the RLDS Church in 1880 by the former Morrisite apostle Mark Hill Forscutt (see 1880). The Order’s regard for Morris is reported in secondary accounts (Lost Mormonism; Shields, Divergent Paths, 106, as cited by Wikipedia) and still needs checking against Brighouse’s own writings; whether Forscutt shaped that regard is not known.
  17. 1886The Spirit Prevails, a collection of Morris's revelations, articles, and letters, is published by George S. Dove & Company.
  18. 1892In San Francisco, James Dove publishes A Few Items in the History of the Morrisites for the California congregation.
  19. 1969The Church of Jesus Christ of the Saints of the Most High, the last organized Morrisite body in Montana, formally disbands. According to Anderson, August 6, 1969, was the last annual Morrisite gathering. Its records, its descendants, and the Racetrack church remain.
  20. 1981–1988C. LeRoy Anderson publishes For Christ Will Come Tomorrow (1981) and Joseph Morris and the Saga of the Morrisites (1988), the first book-length histories.
  21. 1990A later claimant. Neal Chase, interpreter for Leland Jensen’s Missoula-based sect, the Bahá’ís Under the Provisions of the Covenant, publishes Ezekiel’s Temple in Montana. Drawing on Anderson’s book, he cites George Williams’s prophecy that Christ would come in the Deer Lodge Valley, identifies the Montana State Prison at Deer Lodge (where Jensen was held from 1969) as Ezekiel’s temple, and dates the fulfillment to August 9, 1969, Jensen’s first full day in prison. Anderson rejected this reading, noting that Williams named no date. The sect was not Morrisite in origin; it adopted Morrisite prophecies as confirmation of its leader’s mission.
  22. 1996–2010After Jensen’s death in 1996 the sect divides over leadership, with Chase leading one faction; lawsuits continue to about 2010.
  23. 2006A monument to the Kington Fort–Morrisite War site is dedicated at South Weber.
  24. 2010Anderson’s final edition, Joseph Morris and the Saga of the Morrisites (Revisited), is published.
  25. July 2012Descendants of Morrisite families hold a reunion in Deer Lodge; the property owners allow them to visit the Racetrack church.
  26. 2026Kirk Watson publishes The Morrisites: Writings and Revelations, collecting writings of Morris, Williams, Davies, and the Doves. The Racetrack church still stands, in declining condition (see the condition record in Series 5).
Series 2

Places

Sites connected with the movement, and what can be seen at each today.

Kington Fort

South Weber, Utah

Built in 1853 and abandoned in 1858, then reoccupied by the Morrisites. Nothing of the fort survives; a monument to the Kington Fort–Morrisite War site was dedicated in 2006. The fort was named for Bishop Thomas Kington, and “Kington” is the correct spelling. The monument itself notes that the earliest sources (the notebook kept by Kington’s wife, his gravestone, Wilford Woodruff’s journals, and early church records) all spell it that way; recent scholarship such as Holley (2008) follows them. Many nineteenth- and twentieth-century accounts, and some modern references, including the Utah History Encyclopedia and Wikipedia, write “Kingston Fort.” Insiders used both: Forscutt, who lived in the fort, wrote “Kingston’s Fort” and called it “the proper name” of the place (“Sketch of Joseph Morris,” CHL MS 702). Search under both spellings. A log cabin nearby, which local resident Ed Johnson believed was the building Morris used to hold the three departing members whose detention led to the siege, is shown in MO-IMG-046–049; the identification rests on local tradition. About 200 yards south, on the west side of the road, in the South Weber Pioneer Cemetery, a marker reads “Joseph Werner, ‘Morrisite,’ about 1870” (MO-IMG-021). The name lingered locally. In the early 2000s, while the editor was going door to door in South Weber, a woman then in her sixties said that elementary-school classmates had called her “Morrisite” as a taunt because of where in South Weber she lived; she said her family had no Morrisite connection. (Editor’s memorandum of recollection, written November 1, 2026; the conversation was not recorded at the time.) A cannonball from the siege was kept by the family of Al Peek, together with a portrait of Mary Christoffersen Anderson (MO-IMG-051–053; the portrait is MO-IMG-062).

VisitThe monument is public. The Pioneer Cemetery appears to lie on private land but is reached by a public easement; stay on the access route. The cabin is on private property.

Morristown

Soda Springs, Idaho

The Morrisites were among the founders of Soda Springs, one of the oldest towns in Idaho. Connor reported bringing 160 settlers (53 families, seven single men, and four widows), who arrived May 20, 1863, and were given building lots on a town site surveyed on the north bank of the Bear River, beside the military reservation of Camp Connor (Connor’s report, June 2, 1863, OR). A later marker describes a 200-acre survey west of Soda Creek, lots of 25 by 130 feet, and one-room log homes with dirt floors. The Morrisite “Lower Town” stood below the military “Upper Town”; Soda Springs was the first seat of Oneida County, 1864–66. After the troops left in 1865, hard winters and failed crops thinned the settlement. Many Morrisites moved on, some north to the Blackfoot area, and others sold to miners and to Latter-day Saint settlers, whose new town site dates from Brigham Young’s visit of 1870–71. Most of the old town now lies under Alexander Reservoir. Those who stayed, led by Niels Anderson, carried their quarrel with the Mormon hierarchy into territorial politics; the Niels Anderson Homestead marker credits his anti-polygamy campaign with helping deny Mormons the vote in Idaho. Accounts differ on some details: most give 160 settlers (one popular account says about 325), Camp Connor’s founding is dated May 20 or May 23, and the National Park Service marker wrongly calls Morris Welsh-born. Markers stand in town, including the Park Service’s Morrisite settlement marker in the parking lot of a Latter-day Saint meetinghouse (HMDB), the state’s Camp Connor marker (HMDB), and “Law & Order on the Frontier” on Camp Connor’s probable site near the hospital (HMDB). Descendants held a reunion in Soda Springs on August 3, 2013.

Niels and Mary Christoffersen Anderson, married at Morristown on July 20, 1863 (the date on their gravestone; some accounts give July 30), are buried in Fairview Cemetery at Soda Springs. Their gravestone carries a long bronze inscription giving a Morrisite account of the 1862 siege, in which Mary, then fourteen, lost her jaw to the first cannon shot fired into the fort. Forscutt, who was present, described her lower jaw “hanging only by a narrow strip of skin,” with two women dead beside her (“Sketch of Joseph Morris,” CHL MS 702; MO-IMG-061, with transcription; MO-IMG-062; HMDB; see also the Niels Anderson Homestead marker).

VisitThe markers and the Anderson gravestone in Fairview Cemetery are publicly accessible. The townsite is largely under Alexander Reservoir.

Carson City & California

Nevada · California

The western congregations, where George Dove published The Spirit Prevails and led the California church after 1882. In May 1863 Connor gave 150 Morrisites passage to Carson City in an empty quartermaster’s train bound there for stores (Connor’s report, June 2, 1863, OR).

VisitNo surviving sites identified for this guide. Information welcome.

Omaha & Council Bluffs

Nebraska · Iowa

Danish-speaking congregations whose 1869 membership and account books survive in translation.

VisitNo sites identified; the record survives in documents only.

Deer Lodge Valley

Deer Lodge County, Montana

The longest-lived center of the movement. The wood-frame Morrisite church, begun in 1877, stood on private land at Racetrack, visible from the west side of Interstate 90 south of Deer Lodge, when last photographed for this guide in November 2018. It is the only Morrisite church building known to survive, and its condition has worsened markedly since 2009. See the condition record in Series 5.

View onlyThe church is on private land and unsafe. View it from the public road; do not enter or trespass.

Kingdom of Heaven

Walla Walla, Washington

William W. Davies's commune on Mill Creek, 1867–1881. A road called the Mormon Grade, an interpretive sign at Scenic Loop Road and Mormon Grade, and Davies's grave in Lyons Cemetery remain.

VisitThe interpretive sign and Lyons Cemetery are public.

Morrisite scepters

LDS 65-3058 · 3059 · 3060
Held by
Church History Museum collection, The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, Salt Lake City
Description
Three painted wooden staffs, each with a printed inscription mounted on the shaft. The editor saw them in the museum vault and transcribed the inscriptions. Photographs were requested in 2010 for Anderson’s revised history; they are not reproduced here.
Inscriptions
Joseph Morris’s scepter declares him sent “to RULE over all ADAM’S posterity” and is signed “Joseph Morris, PRESIDENT of the Melchisidec Priesthood.” The other two belonged to Hugh Park (“number five,” his name written over an earlier one) and Daniel Smith (“number seven”), each presenting himself as a ruler “over all Abraham’s seed.”

Transcription of the Anderson plaque (MO-IMG-061)

NIELS ANDERSON  ·  OCTOBER 3 [?], 1835 – MARCH 4 [?], 1926
MARY CHRISTOFFERSEN ANDERSON  ·  JULY 26, 1847 – JULY 13 [?], 1928

THEY WERE BORN IN DENMARK; JOINED THE MORMONS THERE. IN 1857 MRS. ANDERSON ARRIVED AT BURLINGTON, IOWA; DETAINED THERE UNTIL 1859 ON ACCOUNT OF JOHNSTON’S ARMY HAVING TO BE SENT TO UTAH. THAT YEAR SHE WALKED NEARLY ALL THE WAY TO SALT LAKE CITY. IN 1860 MR. ANDERSON CAME; HE PULLED A HAND-CART FROM FLORENCE, NEBRASKA. THEY SOON SAW THE DECEPTION IN “BRIGHAMISM,” JOINED MORRIS A SECEDER FROM BRIGHAM. MAY 22 [?], 1863, THEY CAME TO SODA SPRINGS WITH THE FIRST SETTLERS; THEY WERE ESCORTED AND PROTECTED BY GEN. CONNOR AND CALIFORNIA VOLUNTEERS. JULY 20, 1863, THEY WERE MARRIED HERE; THEIR MARRIAGE WAS THE FIRST IN IDAHO TERRITORY AS FAR AS THEY KNEW. LATER MR. ANDERSON BECAME “THE FATHER OF THE ANTI-MORMON PARTY” IN IDAHO.

AT SOUTH WEBER, UTAH, JUNE 13–15, 1862, MR. AND MRS. ANDERSON WERE IN “THE MORRISITE MASSACRE.” MRS. ANDERSON’S CHIN WAS SHOT AWAY WITH A CANNON BALL, THAT KILLED MRS. BARBARA DE[…]THELM [?] AND MRS. JOSEPH (?) MARSH, FIRED BY GEN. ROBERT T. BURTON’S MORMON MILITIA INTO A BOWERY OF UNARMED “MORRISITES,” JUNE 13, BECAUSE THEY REFUSED TO OBEY “BRIGHAM’S” DECREES, AFTER JOHNSTON’S ARMY HAD LEFT UTAH ON ACCOUNT OF THE CIVIL WAR. MRS. J. L. BEAR AND HER BABY WERE KILLED WITH ANOTHER CANNON BALL. THEN MANY MORRISITES WITH THEIR OLD GUNS TRIED RESISTANCE. ON THE THIRD DAY ALL SURRENDERED UNDER A WHITE FLAG.

MRS. ANDERSON SAYS: “WHEN MORRIS CAME BEFORE GEN. BURTON, THE LATTER USED ABUSIVE LANGUAGE AND TRIED TO RIDE OVER MORRIS ON HIS HORSE. THIS ABUSE WAS ON ACCOUNT OF HIS RELIGIOUS TEACHING. I SAW HIM … SHOOT MORRIS.”

DOW’S AFFIDAVIT SAYS: BURTON SHOT MORRIS, BANKS, MRS. JAMES BOWMAN AND ANOTHER WOMAN, AFTER THEY SURRENDERED. TEN “MORRISITES” WERE KILLED, AND ONE “MORMON.” THE PRISONERS WERE TO BE TRIED AT THE NEXT TERM OF COURT HELD BY CHIEF JUSTICE KINNEY, A MORMON. IN THE MEANTIME GOV. HARDING, ASSOCIATE JUSTICES WAITE, DRAKE, COL. CONNOR AND VOLUNTEERS ARRIVED.

“SEVEN MORRISITES” WERE SENTENCED TO IMPRISONMENT FROM “TEN TO FIFTEEN YEARS.” “SIXTY-SIX” HEAVILY FINED.

“WE,” WROTE GOV. HARDING IN 1874, “ATTENDED THROUGH THE TRIAL, WHICH WAS NOTHING BUT A MOCKERY. BURTON ADMITTED HIS SHOOTING THE PRISONERS. … HAD I BEEN ON THE BENCH I SHOULD HAVE HAD HIM ARRESTED … ; BUT IT WOULD HAVE BEEN USELESS. THE JURORS WOULD ALL BE MORMONS, AND RECOGNIZE NO LAW BUT ‘THE COMMANDS OF AUTHORITY.’

“PETITIONS WERE GOTTEN UP FOR THE UNCONDITIONAL PARDON OF THE MORRISITES, WHICH WERE SIGNED BY ALL THE GENTILES, … NOT A MORMON SIGNED THEM. …

“I ISSUED THE PARDON; … SOME MOUNTED MORTARS … COMMANDING THE BEE-HIVE HOUSE AND LION HOUSE, MADE THINGS TOLERABLY SMOOTH ON THE SURFACE.”

BRIGHAM YOUNG JUNE 15, AS BY H. P. McLANE, SAID, “THAT MORRIS AND BANKS WILL BE TAKEN I HAVE NO DOUBT. … BUT IF I HAD MY OWN WAY ABOUT IT, I WOULD NOT SPARE NEITHER MAN, WOMAN NOR CHILD; BUT AS IT IS A UNITED STATES AFFAIR, THEY CAN HAVE IT AS THEY PLEASE. BUT AS REGARDS THIS YOUNG MAN, HE HAS DIED A MARTYR TO THE CAUSE AND KINGDOM OF OUR GOD. … THIS WAS A MATTER OF NECESSITY. … THE WORST … I [?] DISLIKE … , IS THAT AFTER THEY ARE ROUTED, THE … ACCURSED DEVILS … WILL BE TRYING TO SNEAK BACK INTO THE CHURCH AGAIN. …”

R. W. YOUNG WRITING FOR THE “CONTRIBUTOR,” OF 1890, FOR THE MORMON MUTUAL IMPROVEMENT ASSOCIATION SAYS: “THE … ACTION OF THE GOVERNOR WAS FOLLOWED … IN COURSE OF TIME, BY PERSECUTION OF GEN. BURTON. … A GRAND JURY WAS LED IN … 1870, TO BRING IN AN INDICTMENT FOR MURDER … , AND … (HE) THOUGHT HIMSELF COMPELLED BY CIRCUMSTANCES TO EVADE ARREST … LATER, ANOTHER INDICTMENT WAS FOUND AGAINST GEN. BURTON … , HE MADE NO ATTEMPT TO ESCAPE. …”

BETWEEN THESE TWO INDICTMENTS A RULING HAD “PROVIDED IN SUBSTANCE FOR JURIES HALF MORMON, AND HALF GENTILE.”

THIS YOUNG SAYS, “THE PROSECUTION LABORED LONG, AND,” “CLOUDS OF WITNESSES WERE BROUGHT FORTH,” “TO PERSUADE THE JURY,” “ONE-HALF BEING NON-MORMONS,” THAT BURTON KILLED MRS. BOWMAN.

THIS CASE WAS NO. 3109, U.S. VS. BURTON FOR “MURDER 1ST DEGREE,” SET FOR TRIAL 1ST DAY OF FEB. TERM 1879, MARCH 8 — INDICTMENT DISMISSED. THIS WAS ONE OF THE THREE INDICTMENTS AGAINST HIM.

IT WAS ASKED: WHAT ABOUT THE CASE FOR KILLING MORRIS? U.S. DISTRICT ATTORNEY VANZILE ANSWERED “WHAT IS THE USE, IF THEY WILL NOT CONVICT FOR KILLING AN INNOCENT WOMAN?”

TO FATHER AND MOTHER, AND HISTORIC TO MAN, WE DEDICATE THIS MEMORIAL.

ABRAHAM CHRISTOPHER ANDERSON, I[D]A COLLETT, ANNA CHRISTINA ROGERS,
DAVID ANDREW ANDERSON, ABELONE LARSEN, PETER WILLIAM ANDERSON,
NIELS CHRISTIAN ANDERSON, MARY AMELIA VERHAM.

Open questions

Questions for research

Questions the guide has not been able to settle, for students, local historians, and other researchers. Each can be advanced with records that are digitized or held in Utah, Idaho, Montana, or California.

If you take one up, or can answer one, write to contact@morrisite.org. Answers will be credited here. The edition of Forscutt’s “Sketch of Joseph Morris” is in progress by the editor.

Where is Joseph Morris buried?

Open
Known
The Salt Lake City Cemetery sexton’s record lists Morris under June 15, 1862, as no. 1469. He lies in the pauper ground, in an unmarked grave. A tradition holds that he was buried beneath a street so that he would be trodden underfoot; no published source for it has been found.
Where to look
Salt Lake City Cemetery office (plat maps of the pauper sections); sexton’s records on FamilySearch; Salt Lake newspapers for June 1862 in Utah Digital Newspapers; where the tradition first appears in print or family accounts.

Did Brigham Young ever answer Morris?

Open
Known
About a dozen of Morris’s letters survive in Young’s office files (CR 1234 1). Anderson found no reply, and reports a penciled note on the first letter, “He’s weak minded.” The letters have not been listed by date.
Where to look
Young’s letterpress copybooks and office journals, 1857–1861, in the same collection; the Journal of Wilford Woodruff for February 1861. A dated list of the letters, with catalog links, would be useful in itself.

What did Joseph F. Smith write to Niels Anderson?

Open
Known
In 1903 Niels Anderson asked the church to help his wife, Mary Christoffersen Anderson, whose jaw was shot away in 1862. His letters survive (CR 1 191, digitized), and they answer a letter from Smith dated February 25, 1903, which is not in the file. Whether any help followed is unknown.
Where to look
Joseph F. Smith and First Presidency letterpress copybooks for 1903; Bannock Stake and Soda Springs ward records.

Who were the Morrisites?

Open
Known
The 1886 roll of membership lists about 430 people baptized at South Weber, but the lives of ordinary members have never been traced. Most Morrisite names appear in no readily available source.
Where to look
The roll; Slaterville and other ward records; the 1860 and 1870 censuses; Soda Springs, Carson Valley, and Deer Lodge records. Findings would be cited here, not published as a list, and living people are excluded.

Who died at Kington Fort?

Open
Known
Accounts differ on the number of dead and on some of their names and dates. No list checked against all the sources exists.
Where to look
Contemporary newspapers; Woodruff’s journal; the Journal History; Forscutt’s account; the Anderson gravestone; Salt Lake and Weber County burial records; testimony from the 1879 trial of Robert T. Burton.

What became of the California congregation?

Open
Known
George and James Dove led the church in San Francisco after 1882 and published its books into the 1890s. How and when the congregation ended is not known.
Where to look
San Francisco directories and newspapers in the California Digital Newspaper Collection; probate and cemetery records for the Dove family; the imprints of later Morrisite publications.

What do the Danish revelations say?

Open
Known
The USU collection holds a bound volume of Morris’s revelations in Danish that does not seem to exist anywhere else. It has not been translated or compared with The Spirit Prevails.
Where to look
USU Special Collections, Caine MSS 10. A reader of nineteenth-century Danish handwriting is needed.

Where are the records of the 1863 trials?

Open
Known
Seven Morrisites were convicted of second-degree murder and others fined in March 1863, before Governor Harding pardoned them. Accounts come mainly from newspapers and later histories.
Where to look
Utah territorial court case files at the Utah State Archives; federal records for the territory; Harding’s papers and pardon records.

About

About this guide

Where the material comes from, how it is checked, and how to cite it.

How the guide was compiled

The guide brings together work done over more than two decades. Its foundation is the research of C. LeRoy Anderson, whose files at Utah State University (Caine Coll. MSS 10) remain the largest body of Morrisite records. The editor assisted Anderson with the 2010 edition of his history and has studied the Morrisite apostle and later RLDS minister Mark Hill Forscutt since at least 2001, when his first article on Forscutt appeared, including transcribing Forscutt’s journals. Photographs of the sites were taken by the editor from the early 2000s onward. Kirk Watson’s 2026 edition of Morrisite writings, published scholarship, and records shared by descendants fill out the rest.

Standards

  • Citations follow The Chicago Manual of Style. Entries marked Not yet checked are known only from another source’s citation and have not been examined by the editor.
  • Facts are drawn from published works with citations; their text is not reproduced. Videos and photographs by others are linked, not copied.
  • Dates marked provisional, approximate, or “ca.” are flagged as such and revised when better evidence turns up.
  • Membership rolls and other records naming private individuals are cited but not republished. The 1861–62 South Weber roll, for example, lists about 430 names (Anderson, Revisited, 214–18).
  • Changes are listed under What’s new.
