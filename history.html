History

Chronology

The main events, from Morris’s revelations through the Montana congregations to later claimants and the present.

  1. 1842–1853Joseph Morris’s early life, as his elder brother George tells it. Joseph joined the church in England, “I think in 1842,” received the gift of tongues and used it unwisely, and was badly burned by fire damp in the coal mines. He married Mary Thorp and emigrated to St. Louis, where he worked on a river steamboat; after a serious illness he became, George writes, “very flighty and Visionary,” and in Pittsburgh he made himself president of a small branch. He reached Salt Lake City in the fall of 1853 with his wife and one child and lived for a time with George, who rejected his claims (George Morris reminiscences, Church History Library, MS 1614, pp. 115–20, 179; see Documents).
  2. 1857–1862Joseph Morris, an English convert to the Latter-day Saint church, records the revelations later gathered as The Spirit Prevails. He writes repeatedly to Brigham Young. No written reply is known; Anderson reports that the first letter, dated December 7, 1857, bears a penciled note on its margin, “He’s weak minded.” The church’s answer comes in February 1861 (next entry).
  3. 11 Feb 1861The apostles John Taylor and Wilford Woodruff hold a meeting at South Weber to “regulate” the branch. On Taylor’s motion, seconded by Woodruff, sixteen of Morris’s followers are cut off from the church, among them Bishop Richard Cook, his brother John Cook, John Parson and Robert Farley (Woodruff’s journal, February 11, 1861). Morris’s name is not on the list Woodruff recorded.
  4. 6 Apr 1861Morris organizes his church at South Weber with six members and calls his followers to gather at Kington Fort, an abandoned 1853 settler fort there (Anderson, Revisited, p. 66). Two days earlier a revelation named Richard Cook and John Parson as the first men called, Parson to preside over those who would hold the apostleship, because it was necessary to call these two “in order to commence an organization” (The Spirit Prevails, No. 56, pp. 78–79). Josiah D. Price’s journal names the six whom Morris baptized on April 6: Richard Cook, John Cook, Nathan Brooks, John Firth, William Kendell and Moses Bryne (written “Burnes”), all of them high priests except Bryne (Josiah D. Price journal, 1858–1873, photocopy, P26-22 f1, Community of Christ Library Archives). On its name, see the Glossary.
  5. May–Jun 1861An informer’s report sent to Brigham Young describes Morris’s Sunday meetings. On May 12 six people are confirmed by three men laying on hands together, Morris, Richard Cook and John Parson, and Cook gives “an explanation of the diagram.” Morris says: “I say now I profess to be the Seventh Angel. The seventh thousand years is now ushered in. Joseph held the 6th seal of the 6th dispensation.” Parson reads a revelation of May 11 commanding “those who believed in him (Morris) to gather forthwith to South Weber.” On May 26 Morris says that “one month would not elapse before the judgments of God would be poured out” (“Doctrines of the Morrisites, May 12, 1861,” informer’s report, “Morrisite papers, 1861–1862,” Brigham Young office files, CR 1234 1, Church History Library).
  6. Jun–Dec 1861Further reports to Brigham Young, many through Chauncey W. West of Ogden, follow the camp. John Banks, arriving on June 30, says that he and Cook “were friends in the old country.” A revelation of November 1861 names Richard Cook to the office of first counselor in place of Brigham Young, and that month Parson preaches while “pointing at the diagram of the planets.” On December 4 West reports that Morris has told his men “to scour up their guns and have ammunition ready” (“Morrisite papers, 1861–1862,” Brigham Young office files, CR 1234 1, Church History Library).
  7. 1861–1862The community holds its goods in common and awaits the Second Coming. On December 30, 1861, a revelation promises that Christ will come “to-morrow morning” if those present consecrate all their property; when he does not, later revelations blame those who gave unwillingly and decline to set another time (The Spirit Prevails, Nos. 188–199, pp. 338–59). As appointed days pass, some members grow discontented and try to leave, and in April 1862 a revelation forbids letting them take back what they consecrated (No. 254, p. 490). See Teachings.
  8. 1 May 1862Six captains are chosen at Kington Fort, each to drill ten men. That day, by Josiah D. Price’s account, Morris, Richard Cook, John Banks and John Parson rode before the company as the four horsemen of Revelation 6, on white, red, black and pale horses, with a sword, a pair of balances and the book of revelations, which were explained to the people as judgment, justice and mercy (Josiah D. Price journal, 1858–1873, photocopy, P26-22 f1, Community of Christ Library Archives).
  9. 26 May 1862The men are reorganized into three companies of high priests and four of priests of Aaron, and each swears an oath as a soldier to defend the kingdom; Price swore it that day (Josiah D. Price journal, 1858–1873, photocopy, P26-22 f1, Community of Christ Library Archives). Morris’s revelation on the army of the kingdom provides for seven companies under captains (The Spirit Prevails, pp. 534–35).
  10. 30 May 1862Foreshadowing Day. Twelve generals ride around the fort, the first four on white, red, black and pale horses, followed by seven companies of armed infantry, with Morris as the representative of Jesus (The Spirit Prevails, “History of Events,” pp. 6–7; Anderson, Revisited, pp. 116–17). Price records the order of march, Morris first, then Cook, Banks and Parson and the rest in the order of their baptism, in black coats, white waistcoats, trousers and gloves and black hats, and copies the declaration made by William Harris in the bishop’s robe (Josiah D. Price journal, 1858–1873, photocopy, P26-22 f1, Community of Christ Library Archives). An onlooker, Eli Smith, wrote three days later that Morris rode “his old white horse,” its mane braided into seven braids, in a hat with “seven crowns upon the top of it” and a sword in his hand; Richard Cook, “his first Counsellor,” rode beside him with a sword, and John Banks carried “a pair of small balances”; Parson, “president of the twenty-four apostles,” carried “the Book of the Revelations.” They marched round seven times, shouting “Hosanna to God and the Lamb,” and in the afternoon the lesser priesthood marched with William Harris at their head. Smith also reported talk in the camp of a day appointed for “the slaying of the prisoners” (Eli Smith to Christopher Layton, June 2, 1862, “Morrisite papers, 1861–1862,” Brigham Young office files, CR 1234 1, Church History Library). See Where did the rituals come from?
  11. 11 Jun 1862Acting Governor Frank Fuller orders General Daniel H. Wells to furnish the territorial marshal, Henry W. Lawrence, and his deputies, Robert T. Burton and Theodore McKean, “a sufficient military force” to act as a posse in serving Third District Court writs for the arrest of “Joseph Morris and others residing in the northern part of Davis County.” The order survives in a copy Wells made in 1871 (Utah Territorial Militia Records, series 2210, document 1,215, Utah State Archives; page image on FamilySearch).
  12. 13 Jun 1862A territorial posse under Robert T. Burton arrives at Kington Fort about 10 a.m.; one of its members, John G. Chambers, puts it at “about 500 men,” with artillery. Howard Egan carries a white flag; when no notice is taken, the cannon open fire. “The first ball killed two women & wounded a third” in the bowery, where Morris was reading a revelation (Chambers; Forscutt also saw two women dead). Jared Smith of the posse is killed. Two men, one of them “Eardley,” leave the fort with their families under a white handkerchief (John G. Chambers, “Siege of Morris fort, 1862 June,” Historian’s Office collected historical documents, CR 100 397, Church History Library).
  13. 14 Jun 1862Rain falls all day. That evening the posse fires the Ogden cannon and “the ‘Old Sow,’” a large gun sent up from Salt Lake City (Chambers). The same day the acting governor, Frank Fuller, writes to Burton that “the shedding of blood in resistance to civil authority renders execution of the law imperative”: “Let your acts still be tempered with mercy, but see that the Laws are vindicated” (copy, Brigham Young office files, CR 1234 1, Church History Library).
  14. 15 Jun 1862A Sunday. About eighteen men of the posse take a log cabin near the entrance of the fort, and Peter Wahler is killed. A rolling battery of willows on wagon wheels is then brought forward; by Chambers’s account the Morrisites took it for a mine and panicked, while other accounts have it tipping over in the mud or pushed up to the wall (see Kington Fort). A white flag goes up, and the people are called together to the tune “In a cottage near a wood.” Morris is killed at the surrender. Most accounts say John Banks, wounded, died in custody that evening; Chambers writes that Burton “with his aides killed Morris & Banks, and two women” (see Who died at Kington Fort?). The siege ends that evening, and the men are listed as prisoners “Sunday, June 15, 1862 – 7 p.m.” (John G. Chambers, “Siege of Morris fort, 1862 June,” Historian’s Office collected historical documents, CR 100 397, Church History Library; “Morrisite prisoners, 1862 June,” Historian’s Office collected historical documents, CR 100 397, Church History Library).
  15. 16 Jun 1862Of 149 men taken, counting Morris and Banks, 49 are discharged at the fort: 41 mostly older men, among them Nathan Brooks, Erick Eliason and Ole Olsen, and eight who had “surrendered during the engagement on the 13, 14 & 15 June,” among them Mark H. Forscutt, John R. Eardley, John E. Jones, William James, Griffith Thomas, William Lloyd and Joseph Moss Jr. (“Morrisite prisoners, 1862 June,” Historian’s Office collected historical documents, CR 100 397, Church History Library; second copy in Brigham Young office files, CR 1234 1). That day Fuller ordered that only “all able bodied men among your prisoners, capable of bearing arms, and who have been found in resistance to civil authority” be held for the court, and congratulated Burton on “the small number of casualties to your force” (copy, Brigham Young office files, CR 1234 1, Church History Library).
  16. 17 Jun 1862The other 98 prisoners are brought to Salt Lake City (prisoner list, “Morrisite prisoners, 1862 June,” Historian’s Office collected historical documents, CR 100 397, Church History Library; Chambers dates the march to the day after the surrender).
  17. 18–19 Jun 1862Before Judge Kinney in Salt Lake City, Richard Cook is fined and jailed for contempt, Peter Klemgaard and Christian Neilson are held in prison, and ninety-five other men are bound over on $1,500 bail each for the March 1863 term (Deseret News, June 25, 1862).
  18. Mar 1863In Salt Lake City, seven Morrisites are convicted of second-degree murder: Peter Klemgard is sentenced to fifteen years, Christian Neilsen to twelve, and Abraham Taylor, Kadrup Neilsen, Jens Christiansen, Andrew Lee and Andrew M. Mason to ten; John Parson and John E. Jones are acquitted. The other prisoners are fined $100 each for resisting arrest (Deseret News, April 1, 1863). The new territorial governor, Stephen S. Harding, pardons them all a few days later.
  19. May 1863Many Morrisites leave Utah under the protection of Patrick Edward Connor, commanding the District of Utah. On May 5 a party of 160 (53 families, seven single men, and four widows) sets out with a company of California Volunteers; it reaches Soda Springs on May 20, where Connor has a town site surveyed on the north bank of the Bear River beside a new military post, Camp Connor (established by order of May 23). The Morrisites call their town Morristown. Connor also gives 150 Morrisites passage to Carson City, Nevada, in an empty supply train. Others settle in California. (Connor’s report, June 2, 1863, Official Records, ser. 1, vol. 50, pt. 1, 226–29.)
  20. 1860s–Some Morrisites return to the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, in Utah and elsewhere. The 2006 monument at South Weber notes that a few were rebaptized and remained there. Others find a home in the Reorganized Church (RLDS): the former Morrisite apostle Mark Hill Forscutt joins it in Salt Lake City in 1865, and John L. Bear (Johann Lebrecht Bär, 1838–1919), a Swiss convert from Affoltern am Albis who emigrated from Zürich in 1860 and lost his wife and child at Kington Fort, later serves as an RLDS missionary to Switzerland and Germany, dies in Independence, Missouri, and publishes his autobiography in the RLDS Journal of History. In 1865 RLDS missionaries at Malad, Idaho, baptize several people who appear on the roll. Others join only after years in the Morrisite groups: Erick and Caroline Eliason are baptized together at Soda Springs on June 9, 1870; Margaretta Cook leaves the Morrisites in Nevada in 1870 and is baptized RLDS in 1873; and Lena Hanson, at Soda Springs and then Deer Lodge, in 1895; in 1890 John R. Eardley reported that Brother Emmons of the Montana church had been baptized and that Mrs. Emmons meant to follow (see the roll).
  21. 1865–66Morrisites are meeting in the Deer Lodge Valley, Montana. On March 4, 1866, Sven Olson Hagg writes from Deer Lodge City that he has spent the winter with L. C. Larsen and his family, and helped Larsen build a new house “hvori vi nu holder forsamlinger” (“in which we now hold meetings”). God has begun “to pour out the power of his Spirit upon some of his children right here in Deer Lodge Valley, where the beginning (of the Kingdom) shall be founded.” The first to receive it, he says, was “his servant William D.”, president of the Aaronic priesthood, standing “in Abel’s place” by birthright. This was William W. Davies, whose community later dated its era from January 24, 1866. Hagg also notes that Williams, in an epistle of August 26, 1865, had told J. P. Lorentzen, S. P. Guhl and George Thompson to winter in the valley (Hagg to A. Hanssen, March 4, 1866, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Sven Olson Hagg, 1866, 1869; translation from the Danish generated by Claude).
  22. 1867–1881William W. Davies, who in an 1867 pamphlet declared that a third dispensation had been opened through him on January 24, 1866, setting baptism aside for a covenant vow (Watson, p. 124), leads a Morrisite offshoot, the Kingdom of Heaven, near Walla Walla, Washington. He declares his infant son Arthur to be Christ returned, the “Walla Walla Jesus.” Lawsuits by former members end the community: in 1881 a court awards three of them $3,225, and the sheriff sells the community’s livestock and its eighty acres to satisfy the judgment.
  23. 1868From Salt Lake City George Williams writes to “Bro. James & Saints of the Most High in Montana” (December 14, 1868): William James’s group is already in Montana and uses the name later borne by the Montana church. Williams wishes to visit “my beloved brethren & sisters in Montana” but says he is “called to travel to the States & Europe” (Church History Library, MS 31876).
  24. 1869George Williams leaves Utah for England, passing through Omaha from January 18 to February 5, where the Council Bluffs and Omaha congregations are organized on his authority (see Omaha & Council Bluffs under Places); a year later he writes from Loughborough that it was “now one year since I left the continent of America”; his wife and children stay in Salt Lake City, and he asks the Montana members to send help for “my little ones” (Williams to the Saints of the Most High in Montana, January 10 [?], 1870, Church History Library, MS 31876). By June he is at Ashwicken, near King’s Lynn, Norfolk, writing to the Saints at Omaha and Council Bluffs, where Rasmus Rasmussen is “the presiding Bishop.” George Thompson is with him at Ashwicken that summer, and William P. Thomas and a Brother James come to England too: Thompson writes on July 29, 1869, to “Beloved Brethren W. P. and D. J.,” one at Llanybyther in Wales and the other at Derby. The Omaha minutebook names him: on June 2, 1869, Guhl left for Denmark with “2 Welsh brethren, William P. Thomas, a High Priest, and an Aaronic Priest Daniel James,” called “to go to Wales on mission.” In March 1870 Williams writes that “Bro Thomas & Bro James,” having “attended the heavenly call,” are “now permitted to return to their families in Montana” by way of Omaha. In September 1870 he gives his own account of Almerin Grow, who had walked from California to Salt Lake on hearing of him, was told of “his mission to the Sultan & Priesthood of Mahommet,” and was later “sent to Constantinople & Jerusalem,” returning “without my consent” (Williams to the Omaha church and to Andrew Hendrikson, 1869–70; George Thompson to William P. Thomas, 1869; and the Omaha minutebook, 1869–1871, MS 31876). Danish-language congregations keep membership and account books in Omaha, Nebraska, and Council Bluffs, Iowa.
  25. 1870–71Williams’s followers in Denmark. S. P. Guhl, who had sailed for Denmark in June 1869, and J. P. Lorentzen found a congregation of “the Most High Saints” in Copenhagen, preaching publicly there from January 1, 1871, in a hall in Adelgade, and supporting themselves by their own work; Lorentzen, Guhl and C. B. Berg form a committee to publish tracts (letter from Copenhagen, February 10, 1871). By November 1871 Guhl has broken with Williams. Writing from Denmark on November 5 to Mikkel Christiansen and “Seth’s disciples,” he says they had organized the church in Scandinavia on Williams’s orders but have now “rejected him from our hearts and our assemblies,” objecting to Williams’s oaths (“God’s law says thou shalt not swear at all”), to his appointing one leader after another “to further his speculations to get money,” and to his claim to the Urim and Thummim, which Guhl says he “never had, or else lost” (Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to Andrew Hendrickson, 1869–1871; translation from the Danish generated by Claude). Guhl was back in Omaha, again under Williams, by 1874 (see the roll note).
  26. 1870sMany members regather in the Deer Lodge Valley, Montana, under George Williams, the “Prophet Cainan,” who claims to be Morris's successor. In July 1874, following a revelation in Williams’s epistle from Ashby of April 6, that “my Servant W. James take charge of those that are convenient unto him, and my Servant A. Henrickson take charge of those convenient unto him, and there shall be two Stakes under one Head, even my own chosen Servant Cainan,” the Montana church is divided into two stakes (Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1874–1875; William P. Thomas brought the epistle from the post office and read it to the Saints one Sunday): Stake No. 1 on Warm Spring Creek, which met at a meeting house at Willow Glen under William James, and Stake No. 2 at Race Track under Andrew Hendrikson (Church History Library, MS 32226; see Repositories). Stake No. 2’s own minutes record its founding general meeting on July 12, 1874. Hendrikson spoke to “the brothers and sisters who had chosen to be under his presidency”: they lived in two places, Warm Spring and Deer Lodge, about equally many in each, and Jens Jensen was appointed to preside over the meetings at Deer Lodge. From then on only members of “our organization” were to sit in its councils, not those of “the other organization” (general meeting, July 12, 1874, MS 31876; translation from the Danish generated by Claude). Hendrikson was living at Race Track by February 1878. A local history of Anaconda dates the first Morrisite settlers in the valley to 1863, among them William Price Thomas on Warm Springs Creek, east of the later townsite; has a group under William James arriving in 1867 as others left; and describes a caravan of thirteen covered wagons that came in June 1871, part of a larger migration from Iowa, Nebraska, Utah and Idaho: Scandinavian families, mostly Danes, with English, Scottish and Welsh believers. It places the two congregations in the upper valley near Lost Creek and near Race Track Creek, with about eighty members in all (Patrick F. Morris, Anaconda, Montana, 1997, pp. 15–17). Williams’s letters from England direct the move from Omaha: in September 1871 “Bro Merrick” sets out for Montana “with his cattle,” in the spring of 1872 Williams sends “instructions for gathering up to Montana,” and by December 1872 Andrew Hendrikson’s mail goes to the Warm Spring Creek post office (Williams to Hendrikson and the Omaha church, 1871–72, MS 31876). Not everyone left Nebraska at once. A branch at Fremont met in Andrew Peterson’s house in November 1871, and in March 1873 Peterson wrote to Hendrikson that the Fremont members hoped to come to Montana that summer and were trying to sell their place (MS 31876, Minutes, 1871, and Minutes, 1872–1873). Williams himself was in England, living by 1876 in George Thompson’s house at Walthamstow, Essex: “I am now writing in Bro Thompson’s house where I have been tarrying some weeks” (to William James, July 13, 1876, Church History Library, MS 31876).
  27. 22 Sep 1872At the house of William P. Thomas on Warm Spring Creek, the Montana Saints meet “for the purpose of Organizing the Church of Jesus Christ of the Saints of the Most high,” following a general epistle Williams sent from England in August. William James is appointed president, with W. P. Thomas and Andrew Hendrikson as counselors; Rasmus Rasmussen is ordained bishop, with Daniel James and Nels Damander as his counselors; Peter Staffenson is to preside over the second quorum of Seventies; and Margaret Thomas is reordained “as the Lady elect to preside over the Female Order.” A revelation through Williams of August 22 had called James “on a Mission to Nevada,” leaving his counselors in charge, and he left in haste “with a Teamster down to Corrine.” A second meeting on November 10 “complete[s] the organization” by ordaining six men to the Seventies. The minutebook’s title page dates the emigration of “the Saints of the Most high from the States of Iowa and Nebraska” from the summer of 1871 (minutebook, September 1872–January 1874, Church of the Firstborn (Morrisites) records, 1869–1890, MS 32226, Church History Library).
  28. 1873The Montana council minutes, kept in English this year, show the church at Warm Springs while its leaders are away. William James is on “his Mission in Nevada.” Williams calls William P. Thomas “on a Mission to Salt Lake,” and he appoints Andrew Hendrikson by letter “to take the Presidency, assisted by the Bishop and his Counselors,” while both are absent (May 5, 1873). In March a committee of four tries a quarrel between Thomas and Daniel James over the line between their lands and Daniel James’s cattle, set on by dogs as they passed Thomas’s place. It tells the two to forgive each other, but Thomas rejects the ruling as “Develish talke.” In July, Damander, who had hosted the Council Bluffs councils, turns against Williams, saying “Cainan was a Man like Brigham Young to lead the People astray.” James takes back the presidency on July 20. In September the council meets privately at Rasmus Rasmussen’s house to keep Thomas out and agrees to report him to Williams; Thomas, Williams’s appointed first counselor and “our second man in authority,” is to be left alone until Williams answers. In December the sisters in the town of Deer Lodge complain that Margaret Thomas did not invite them to the sisters’ feast at Warm Springs (council minutes, March–December 1873 and January 12, 1874, Church History Library, MS 31876). In California the same year, George Dove, who with his father James had followed Williams and then Davies, begins receiving communications and organizes a Church of the Firstborn of about thirty people (Watson, p. 198). James Dove’s own account places the Davies years in San Francisco: back there in January 1868, he “found a few who had accepted W. W. Davies,” joined them and preached among them; it does not say whether he accepted Davies’s claims himself, nothing so far places the Doves at Walla Walla, and by 1892 he wrote of Davies with open hostility (James Dove, A Few Items in the History of the Morrisites, [1892], pp. 3, 6, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to Andrew Hendrickson, 1892). James Dove’s own account puts the organizing a little later: George’s communications began the year before John Parson set up his “kingdom” in San Francisco on April 7, 1874, and only after that did “the word of the Lord” come “that we were to organize into a church called the Church of the Firstborn,” with five baptized first and about twenty-nine members (James Dove, A Few Items in the History of the Morrisites, [1892], pp. 4–5, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to Andrew Hendrickson, 1892).
  29. 1876The Danish council minutes for the year show the Montana church meeting monthly in members’ houses at Warm Springs, Deer Lodge and Dempsey Creek, with Andrew Hendrikson presiding and Jens Jensen reading the minutes. Williams, who in April wrote that he had been “very sorrowful … because of the strife, the unfriendliness, the unforgiveness, the disputings” in Montana (Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1876–1877), has sent William James away on a mission and directed that Daniel James look after James’s stake; when James suspected that the mission was meant “to get rid of you,” Williams wrote that “no such thought could emanate from me” (Williams to James, July 13, 1876, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to William James and Montana followers, 1876–1878). When James returns in August with a letter from Williams, he reads Williams’s word “convenient” to mean that he should preside over the members at Warm Springs and Hendrikson over those at Deer Lodge; the council holds that the two stakes must stay as they are until Williams sends further instructions. In November a letter from Williams asks Hendrikson to call together the brethren “of both priesthoods” to propose a form of government with officers; the council proposes no change. In December it excludes Peter Nelsen, who said he had no faith in Cainan’s mission, from “Jesu Christi Kirke af den Høiestes Hellige,” the Church of Jesus Christ of the Most High Saints (council minutes, January–December 1876, Church History Library, MS 31876, Minutes, 1876; translation from the Danish generated by Claude). That December a grand jury at Deer Lodge indicted “Wm. P. Thomas” for assault “with intent to do great bodily injury”; he was tried in the district court, and on December 21 “the jury returned verdict of not guilty and prisoner discharged” (New North-West, December 15, 1876, p. 3; December 22, 1876, p. 3; January 5, 1877, p. 3). This was very probably William P. Thomas of Warm Springs, Williams’s former first counselor; the reports do not name the person assaulted. Williams, told of it in England, wrote in August 1877 of “such a serious charge before a court where his life was in jeopardy” and of Thompson’s distress that Thomas “could be guilty of such hideous crimes,” and complained that a note of Thompson’s to Thomas had been used “to bring persecution on the faithful” (Williams to Hendrikson, August 8, 1877, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1876–1877).
  30. 1877Williams settles the dispute of 1876: the word “convenient” meant that each member “may choose the Stake convenient to their feelings & opportunities of attending,” under “two distinct Presidencies, not interfering one with the other.” In the same letter of January 11 he asks that February 14, his birthday, be kept as “a day of rejoicing,” and that Sister Rasmussen be ordained “High Priestess” for Stake No. 2. In April he sends William James instructions for a white linen robe and creates “the Court of the Lord’s House,” to meet every May 10; in August he adds a court every November 10. In October he asks for a building “to carry out Temple ordinances,” and moves that the court commit William Davies, S. P. Guhl, James and George Dove and one other “to outer darkness for one thousand years,” to be sanctioned “with both hands uplifted, saying Amen.” In August Williams writes of the “serious charge” that William P. Thomas had faced the winter before (see 1876). (Williams to Hendrikson, January 11 and August 8, 1877; to James, April 2, 1877; to James, Hendrikson and the Montana Saints, October 12, 1877; Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1876–1877, and Letters from Cainan to William James and Montana followers, 1876–1878).
  31. 1878–79Montana Morrisites build a wood-frame church, the Lord’s House, a mile or so below Racetrack, south of Deer Lodge. About 1877 Williams wrote from England that he was glad the church “is to have a House and the workmen all in the covenant” (MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to William James and Montana followers, 1876–1878), but the site was settled only on February 24, 1878. In September 1878 Williams sent the words of dedication and told James to walk round the building, opening and closing “standing on the South East Corner, from whence cometh the greatest light” (Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to William James and Montana followers, 1876–1878). That day a joint council of both stakes met in Andrew Hendrikson’s house at Race Track, with William James presiding. Some wanted the house on Warm Spring Creek or on Peder Jansen’s land, and Rasmus Rasmussen cited “Cainan’s letter that it was to be build on Land of some faithful Brother.” By ten votes to five the council chose “H. Land,” Hendrikson’s land (council minutes, February 24, 1878). Peder Jansen superintended the building (“havde forestaaet Bygningen”). In July 1879 the Race Track council moved its public Sunday meetings into the Lord’s House at 10:30 a.m., with the brethren to sit on the right and the sisters on the left (council minutes, July 24, 1879). The House was dedicated on August 9, 1879: in July 1882 the council planned to celebrate August 9, “som var 3de Aarsdagen siden at Herrens Huus blev indviet” (“which was the third anniversary of the dedication of the Lord’s House”; council minutes, July 29, 1882). The first council minuted “i Herrens Huus” met on September 27, 1879. A special council for both stakes on October 25, 1879, found that the brothers and sisters had given $634.70 in cash, with $76.73 still owing, to be paid by November 10 (council minutes, 1878–82, Church History Library, MS 31876; translation from the Danish generated by Claude).
  32. 1879Robert T. Burton, indicted for the killing of Isabella Bowman during the surrender (in 1870, an indictment voided in 1872, and again in 1877), is tried and acquitted. Morris’s brother George, a Latter-day Saint high priest who had rejected Joseph’s claims, attended the trial and wrote afterward that “there was no nesesity to shed one Drop of Blood” and “no attempt made to take them prisoners alive” (George Morris reminiscences, MS 1614, pp. 198–99).
  33. 6 Jan 1879Writing from Walthamstow to William James and the “Saints of the Most High in Stake No. 1 on the Sides of the North in Montana,” George Williams says that the region where they live is “the Sides of the North” (Psalm 48:2), where “the City of the great King” will come down before the Lord’s coming: “you are the pioneers.” He sets no date. The same letter calls the Lord’s House, then building, “my first House of Prayer on the Sides of the North” (Andrew Hendrickson’s copy of 1883, letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1878–1881, MS 31876, Church History Library). See Teachings.
  34. 1880In Chicago, Mark Hill Forscutt, a former Morrisite apostle now serving as an RLDS minister, baptizes James Brighouse (August 5) and confirms him (August 8), having baptized Mrs. Brighouse in July and preached the funeral of their infant child. In December Forscutt nominates Brighouse as a teacher in the branch. (Forscutt journal, 1880–1881, Vault MSS 811.) Four years later Brighouse founds the Order of Enoch.
  35. 1880–81The Race Track council meets in the Lord’s House. In March 1880 word comes through Rasmus Rasmussen that a son of Williams has died and that Williams hopes to send his family to Montana; the council votes to help them when they come. On May 10 a joint meeting appoints August 9, the anniversary of the dedication, as “a Social Meeting in the Lords House (Conference),” and William James, robed, administers the sacrament and looks for the coming of Christ in 1891 (see Teachings). On July 14, 1881, Williams appoints George Thompson in writing to “officiate in his stead for the present and until his recovery,” and Thompson asks the Montana church to lay the appointment “before the Saints for their approval or disapproval” (Thompson to the Montana brethren, August 21, 1881, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1878–1881). In September 1881 Hendrikson reads the council “an Epistle from Cainan which George Thompson had written,” and in November members object that John R. Eardley has preached against brandy and tobacco in a public meeting; Hendrikson says Eardley has “gone to extremes” (minutes, 1880–81, Church History Library, MS 31876, Minutes, 1880 and Minutes, 1881; translation from the Danish generated by Claude, except the meeting of May 10, 1880, which is in English).
  36. 1882–83Williams dies in England on April 25, 1882, “being in his 68 year,” after about two years of great pain from “an enlargement of the bone” in his shoulder joint; George Thompson, who had done his writing for him, still knew of no one “appointed to fill his place” in August 1883 (Thompson to William P. Thomas, August 9, 1883, MS 31876). The movement splits among rival successors. In Montana, where the church is known as the Church of Jesus Christ of Saints of the Most High, Williams had directed that William James, its local president, be put before the church a year after his death: “Let the organisation remain as it is, for twelve months after my decease, then let brother James be placed before the whole Church when assembled at the years end, and if sustained by uplifted hand, there let him remain, and so on, from year to year” (Williams to the Montana brethren, January 29, 1882, Church History Library, MS 31876). An envelope he addressed to James, postmarked at New York on March 26, bears the words “I leave you in charge after my decease.” In his last weeks George Thompson, who lived near him in Walthamstow, answered the Montana church’s questions for him: on March 23, 1882, he wrote that “Bro Cainan is no better but gradually getting weaker & weaker,” and that if Williams named no one to replace a Sister Rasmussen in her office, the church should choose someone “and we will confirm your choice” (MS 31876). On April 25, 1883, a general assembly in the Lord’s House sustains James by 19 votes to 10, and he chooses Andrew Hendrikson and John R. Eardley as counselors. Several of the minority, among them Erick Eliason and Lars Beck, refuse to accept the vote, and the Race Track bishop, Rasmus Rasmussen, who will not sustain James, keeps the church’s records and is suspended (Church History Library, MS 32226; see Repositories). (Eardley, later a stake president, leaves Montana about 1900.) In the Danish-speaking stake at Race Track, the council minutes record the other side of the vote: in December 1882 Andrew Hendrikson read Williams’s last instructions that, if no new servant of the Lord came before the anniversary, the church might “selv … vælge en Leder for Kirken” (“itself choose a leader for the church”); in February 1883 he said that since “Cainan havde recommenderet Wm. James” he would uphold him, while Lars Beck said he could not see that choosing a president would do them any good (Race Track council minutes, December 30, 1882, and February 24, 1883, MS 31876; translation from the Danish generated by Claude). George Dove leads the California congregation.
  37. 1884The yearly assembly of April 25, at which Williams had directed that William James be put before the church “from year to year,” becomes a dispute. James and Andrew Hendrikson set it aside; John R. Eardley holds that Williams’s words, “Let the organization remain as it is for twelve months after my decease; then let Bro. James be placed before the whole church,” meant a vote every year, not “I leave you in charge” for life, and George Thompson in England takes his side (Eardley to Hendrickson, April 24 and 25, 1884, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to Andrew Hendrickson, 1880–1889).
  38. 1884A later claimant. James Brighouse, breaking from the RLDS Church, organizes the Order of Enoch in Independence, Missouri, a small commune with Lars Peterson whose main tenet is celibacy. After breaking with the Petersons and returning to Utah he proclaims himself the embodiment of the Second Coming and the “One Mighty and Strong,” and only then, as far as Kirk Watson can tell, teaches reincarnation. Brighouse had been brought into the RLDS Church in 1880 by the former Morrisite apostle Mark Hill Forscutt (see 1880), but Forscutt opposed the doctrine, calling it Morris’s “strange theory of repeated embodiment” (“Sketch of Joseph Morris,” CHL MS 702, p. 12). Watson, who has researched Brighouse, has found no connection between him and the Morrisites beyond shared ideas and language, and notes that the two men meant different things by the “seventh angel”: for Morris a stage in the soul’s ascent through the angelic ranks, for Brighouse a figure taken from Revelation 11. Watson also points out that the title was in Latter-day Saint use before either man took it up. The 1832 revelation now Doctrine and Covenants 88:112 speaks of “Michael, the seventh angel, even the archangel,” and in his funeral sermon for Joseph and Hyrum Smith in 1844, W. W. Phelps cast Joseph as the angel who would pour out the “vials of wrath,” an allusion to the seventh angel of Revelation 16; Latter-day Saints of the time often saw their prophet as the Seventh Angel (Kirk Watson, correspondence with the editor, November 2026, following Christopher James Blythe, “‘Would to God, Brethren, I Could Tell You Who I Am!’” Nova Religio 18, no. 2 (2014): 18–19; Phelps’s sermon as reconstructed in 1855, in Van Wagoner and Walker, “The Joseph/Hyrum Smith Funeral Sermon,” BYU Studies 23, no. 1 (1983): 15–16). One online account (Lost Mormonism) says the Order honored Morris as a prophet and martyr, but the standard references do not: Shields’s entry on the Order (Divergent Paths, 5th ed., §5.4) and Kraut’s note on Brighouse both omit Morris. Wikipedia describes the Order as the one Morrisite-related body that survived 1969; its later activity has not been confirmed for this guide.
  39. 1885George S. and James Dove of the California congregation preach in Salt Lake City and the Cottonwoods, baptizing ten on September 14, then go on to Montana in October. At Willow Glen and Deer Lodge they hold meetings, baptize W. P. Thomas and L. C. Larson on October 26, and speak in the Deer Lodge courthouse; John R. Eardley orders James Dove out of his store, and at Race Track the members refuse them the Lord’s House and agree not to hear them. By the Doves’ account, “a number of brethren” in Montana pressed George S. Dove to publish Morris’s revelations “just as they were written”; it took him about seven months, “as the revelations were in a very disordered condition,” and The Spirit Prevails appeared in 1886 (James Dove, A Few Items in the History of the Morrisites, [1892], pp. 6–13, in Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to Andrew Hendrickson, 1892). In November the Montana council disowned the Doves as “pretenders” in the New North-West.
  40. 1886At the yearly general assembly in the Lord’s House at Race Track, Montana, on April 25, George Thompson of London is elected president of the church; William James, who had claimed the presidency for life “by birthright,” resting on Williams’s envelope “I leave you in charge,” stays away. Eleven members who decline to sustain Thompson are given thirty days to do so. At a court meeting on May 10 James is suspended “for the crimes of usurpation of authority, and heresy,” and John R. Eardley is put over the Warm Spring Creek stake (Church History Library, MS 32226; see Repositories); Daniel James is suspended too, as a teacher and member, “for rebellion against the teachings, laws and authority of the church.” A separate tally of the April vote lists seventeen votes for Thompson, women and girls among them (Church History Library, MS 31876, Minutes, 1886–1887). Those who held out were led by the officers of Stake No. 1, William M. James as stake president, Peter Steffanson as bishop and Daniel James as teacher; six of them met and sustained William James as their president, and rumors went round that “John the Revelator” was coming (Eardley to Hendrickson, May 2, 1886, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to Andrew Hendrickson, 1880–1889). That December Thompson was living in England, writing to the Montana brethren from Thornwood Common, Epping, Essex (MS 31876). Thompson is probably the last general president. Andrew Hendrikson, a Danish immigrant who was never a Latter-day Saint, presides over the Montana Morrisites from 1886 until his death on July 3, 1921.
  41. 1886The Spirit Prevails, a collection of Morris's revelations, articles, and letters, is published by George S. Dove & Company.
  42. 1887At the “Annual President Election” in the Lord’s House on April 25, Hendrikson reminds the church that “some time ago we thought that the Church would never be left without a Prophet,” and that Williams had told them to “allow the Majority to rule.” George Thompson is sustained as president of the church, John R. Eardley as president of Stake No. 1, Hendrikson as president of Stake No. 2, and George Johnson as bishop for the church; three trustees are appointed for the Lord’s House, which “under the new law” was otherwise “liable for Taxation” (Church History Library, MS 31876, Minutes, 1886–1887).
  43. 1887In March John R. Eardley reports that “we are to have the Doves here again on the 17th,” to the unease of some members, two years after the church had disowned them. He is also in touch with the followers of David Whitmer: Whitmer has sent a postcard promising pamphlets, the Expositor has printed an article on the “Church of Christ” signed “P.J.” and a dedicatory prayer by John Parson, and Brother Emmons, on a visit to Missouri, means to call on Whitmer at Richmond and go on to Independence and Lamoni. Joseph Smith III has written to Eardley of a proposed new place of worship opposite the Temple Lot at Independence. Later that month Eardley sails for England, where his mother is ill, planning to see Thompson and, on his way, the Kirtland Temple, Lamoni, Independence and “Father Whitmer”; Brother Strom is to conduct the meetings while he is gone (Eardley to Hendrickson, March 11 and 18, 1887, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to Andrew Hendrickson, 1880–1889).
  44. 1888–89George Thompson, still president of the church, writes from Thornwood Gate, Epping, that he is “here alone on an island,” uncertain how the parable of the ten virgins will be fulfilled, and wary of spiritualism: “I shall stick to the chosen few.” In January 1889 Eardley subscribes for himself and Hendrikson to The Return, a Whitmerite paper he calls “a thorn in the side of the Reorganized Church.” That winter he is chief clerk of the Council of the 16th Legislative Assembly at Helena (Thompson to Eardley, October 7, 1888, and February 17, 1889; Eardley to Hendrickson, January 7 and March 8, 1889, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to Andrew Hendrickson, 1880–1889).
  45. 1891The year that William James had named in 1880 for the coming of Christ passes. On November 10 Hendrikson tells the court that they had long expected “the ‘winding up scene’” this year, and Eardley proposes moving the Lord’s House nearer the members, since “the apostasy of so many of the Saints in Race Track” had left it out of the way (Church History Library, MS 31876, Minutes, 1891; see Teachings). Writing to the court beforehand, Eardley called 1891 “the year in which we have been led to expect so much.” That summer John Parson, now leading a mission of his own, wrote weekly letters to the Sunday Salt Lake Tribune announcing that the work under his charge would be “established this year,” and by October he had visited the Saints in the Deer Lodge Valley. In December Eardley wrote that a high priest whose ordination had been only temporary was “in open rebellion” against Hendrickson and the court, preaching at Warm Springs and meeting with others there; he feared that only a handful might be left as witnesses (Eardley to Hendrickson, June–December 1891, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to Andrew Hendrickson, 1891).
  46. 1892In San Francisco, James Dove publishes A Few Items in the History of the Morrisites for the California congregation, and A Treatise on Re-Incarnation or Re-Embodiment, which Kirk Watson calls the earliest book on the subject in the wider Mormon tradition (Watson, pp. 199, 208–64). A printed copy of A Few Items survives among Hendrickson’s letters at the Church History Library. On June 13, the thirtieth anniversary of the posse’s arrival at Weber, the former apostle John Parson dies of pneumonia at the Larsens’ ranch near Deer Lodge; Eardley calls the death “a great shock” that “undermines many of my calculations, and some of the predictions of Weber!” (Eardley to James Dove, June 18, and to Hendrickson, July 3, 1892, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to Andrew Hendrickson, 1892).
  47. 1893As the Salt Lake Temple nears its dedication on April 6, Eardley writes that “Joseph Morris prophesied it never would be completed,” and that he has been watching to see whether Morris spoke “by the spirit” or “by his own presumption”; no such prophecy has been found in The Spirit Prevails, whose revelations place the temple in Jackson County. On August 9, the church’s feast day, Peter Helstrom’s wife and some children are to be baptized at Race Track (Eardley to Hendrickson, February 28 and July 27, 1893, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to Andrew Hendrickson, 1893–1901).
  48. 1894After the division of 1891–92, Eardley hears that Staffanson and Mrs. Daniel James have agreed to let his group use “the meeting house” part of the time; which building is meant is not clear (Eardley to Hendrickson, March 28, 1894, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to Andrew Hendrickson, 1893–1901).
  49. 1901Eardley calls the members together with Hendrickson and George Johnson on April 25 “to prevent if possible the threatened collapse of God’s little church,” reminding Hendrickson that it “is not my Church, nor your Church” and that they are only its stewards (Eardley to Hendrickson, April 22, 1901, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to Andrew Hendrickson, 1893–1901). It is the latest dated crisis in the church’s papers at the Church History Library.
  50. 1921–1954George Johnson, Hendrikson’s associate of more than fifty years and likewise never a Latter-day Saint, presides at the Lord’s House until shortly before his death on April 29, 1954, at ninety-eight. Anderson calls him the last official Morrisite leader: by the 1940s fewer than a dozen members were active, and with Johnson’s death the church came to an end and the Lord’s House was boarded up (Revisited, pp. 212, 214). His funeral was conducted by an Episcopal priest, and the local paper called the Morrisite church “almost extinct these days” (Silver State Post, May 7, 1954, p. 7; see Newspapers).
  51. 1969Surviving Morrisites, all of the second or third generation, gather at the Lord’s House on August 9, the anniversary of its dedication and one of the church’s two feast days; by then, Anderson writes, the gatherings were more social than religious (Revisited, p. 218). Utah State University’s finding aid for the Morrisite collection says the Church of Jesus Christ of Saints of the Most High officially disbanded in 1969, and the description of Anderson’s papers says meetings were held irregularly until then; Anderson himself dates the end to 1954. See How and when did the Montana church end? Its records, its descendants, and the Racetrack church remain.
  52. 1981–1988C. LeRoy Anderson publishes For Christ Will Come Tomorrow (1981) and Joseph Morris and the Saga of the Morrisites (1988), the first book-length histories.
  53. 1990A later claimant. Neal Chase, interpreter for Leland Jensen’s Missoula-based sect, the Bahá’ís Under the Provisions of the Covenant, publishes Ezekiel’s Temple in Montana. Drawing on Anderson’s book, he cites George Williams’s prophecy that Christ would come in the Deer Lodge Valley, identifies the Montana State Prison at Deer Lodge (where Jensen was held from 1969) as Ezekiel’s temple, and dates the fulfillment to August 9, 1969, Jensen’s first full day in prison. Jensen already taught that the prison was Ezekiel’s temple; his followers say they first learned of the Morrisites and Williams on a trip to Deer Lodge in 1990. Anderson finds that the book attributes far more prophetic ability to Williams than he had, and that there is no substantive proof the 1969 gathering anticipated the Second Advent (Revisited, pp. 216–18). The sect was not Morrisite in origin; it adopted Morrisite prophecies as confirmation of its leader’s mission.
  54. 1996–2010After Jensen’s death in 1996 the sect divides over leadership, with Chase leading one faction; lawsuits continue to about 2010.
  55. 2006A monument to the Kington Fort–Morrisite War site is dedicated at South Weber.
  56. 2010Anderson’s final edition, Joseph Morris and the Saga of the Morrisites (Revisited), is published.
  57. July 2012Descendants of Morrisite families hold a reunion in Deer Lodge; the property owners allow them to visit the Racetrack church.
  58. 2026Kirk Watson publishes The Morrisites: Writings and Revelations, collecting writings of Morris, Williams, Davies, and the Doves. The Racetrack church still stands, in declining condition (see the condition record under Images).
History

Places

Sites connected with the movement, and what can be seen at each today.

Kington Fort

South Weber, Utah

Built in 1853 and abandoned in 1858, then reoccupied by the Morrisites. Nothing of the fort survives; a monument to the Kington Fort–Morrisite War site, Sons of Utah Pioneers marker no. 128, was erected in August 2006 at 6600 South and 475 East (N 41.14677, W 111.96884), sponsored by the South Weber chapter of the Daughters of Utah Pioneers, the Ogden Pioneer chapter of the Sons of Utah Pioneers and All Build Construction and Landscaping, with the site designed by Douglas B. Stephens (Barlow, 2022). The fort was named for Bishop Thomas Kington, and “Kington” is the correct spelling. The monument itself notes that the earliest sources (the notebook kept by Kington’s wife, his gravestone, Wilford Woodruff’s journals, and early church records) all spell it that way; recent scholarship such as Holley (2008) follows them. Many nineteenth- and twentieth-century accounts, and some modern references, including the Utah History Encyclopedia and Wikipedia, write “Kingston Fort.” Insiders used both: Forscutt, who lived in the fort, wrote “Kingston’s Fort” and called it “the proper name” of the place (“Sketch of Joseph Morris,” CHL MS 702). Search under both spellings. Not all the people in the fort were from South Weber. Holley (2008) names eleven men from Slaterville and Marriott, about nine miles to the northwest, among the prisoners of June 1862, and two of the family groups on the roll, Cowan–Bull and Higham, were Slaterville families (see “Families on the roll” on the roll page). A log cabin nearby, which the local historian Ed Cooper, from oral accounts of older South Weber residents, identified as the building Morris used to hold the three departing members whose detention led to the siege, is shown in MO-IMG-046–049; it has been moved from its original site, and the identification rests on local tradition. About 200 yards south, on the west side of the road, in the South Weber Pioneer Cemetery, a marker reads “Joseph Werner, ‘Morrisite,’ about 1870” (MO-IMG-021). The name lingered locally. In the early 2000s, while the editor was going door to door in South Weber, a woman then in her sixties said that elementary-school classmates had called her “Morrisite” as a taunt because of where in South Weber she lived; she said her family had no Morrisite connection. (Editor’s memorandum of recollection, written November 1, 2026; the conversation was not recorded at the time.) A cannonball from the siege was kept by the family of Al Peek, together with a portrait of Mary Christoffersen Anderson (MO-IMG-051–053; the portrait is MO-IMG-062).

VisitThe monument is public. The Pioneer Cemetery appears to lie on private land but is reached by a public easement; stay on the access route. The cabin is on private property.

Morristown

Soda Springs, Idaho

The Morrisites were among the founders of Soda Springs, one of the oldest towns in Idaho. Connor reported bringing 160 settlers (53 families, seven single men, and four widows), who arrived May 20, 1863, and were given building lots on a town site surveyed on the north bank of the Bear River, beside the military reservation of Camp Connor (Connor’s report, June 2, 1863, OR). A later marker describes a 200-acre survey west of Soda Creek, lots of 25 by 130 feet, and one-room log homes with dirt floors. The Morrisite “Lower Town” stood below the military “Upper Town”; Soda Springs was the first seat of Oneida County, 1864–66. After the troops left in 1865, hard winters and failed crops thinned the settlement. Many Morrisites moved on, some north to the Blackfoot area, and others sold to miners and to Latter-day Saint settlers, whose new town site dates from Brigham Young’s visit of 1870–71. Most of the old town now lies under Alexander Reservoir. Those who stayed, led by Niels Anderson, carried their quarrel with the Mormon hierarchy into territorial politics; the Niels Anderson Homestead marker credits his anti-polygamy campaign with helping deny Mormons the vote in Idaho. Accounts differ on some details: most give 160 settlers (one popular account says about 325), Camp Connor’s founding is dated May 20 or May 23, and the National Park Service marker wrongly calls Morris Welsh-born. Markers stand in town, including the Park Service’s Morrisite settlement marker in the parking lot of a Latter-day Saint meetinghouse (HMDB), the state’s Camp Connor marker (HMDB), and “Law & Order on the Frontier” on Camp Connor’s probable site near the hospital (HMDB). Descendants held a reunion in Soda Springs on August 3, 2013.

Niels and Mary Christoffersen Anderson, married at Morristown on July 20, 1863 (the date on their gravestone; some accounts give July 30), are buried in Fairview Cemetery at Soda Springs. Their gravestone carries a long bronze inscription giving a Morrisite account of the 1862 siege, in which Mary, then fourteen, lost her jaw to the first cannon shot fired into the fort. Forscutt, who was present, described her lower jaw “hanging only by a narrow strip of skin,” with two women dead beside her (“Sketch of Joseph Morris,” CHL MS 702; MO-IMG-061, with transcription; MO-IMG-062; HMDB; see also the Niels Anderson Homestead marker).

VisitThe markers and the Anderson gravestone in Fairview Cemetery are publicly accessible. The townsite is largely under Alexander Reservoir.

Carson City & California

Nevada · California

The western congregations, where George Dove published The Spirit Prevails and led the California church after 1882. In May 1863 Connor gave 150 Morrisites passage to Carson City in an empty quartermaster’s train bound there for stores (Connor’s report, June 2, 1863, OR).

VisitNo surviving sites identified for this guide. Information welcome.

Omaha & Council Bluffs

Nebraska · Iowa

Danish-speaking congregations whose 1869 membership and account books survive in translation. Their own minutebook, written up at Omaha in May 1871, tells how they began. Jens Jacob Poulsen, “a High Priest of the Weber House,” was sent ahead by George Williams to gather the scattered Morrisites, and spent about a year there. Williams himself arrived in Omaha on January 18, 1869, “from his home in South Cottonwood in Utah,” on his way to England, held meetings in Omaha and Council Bluffs, and left on February 5 with Poulsen and a Brother Mortensen, who went on to Denmark. On February 7, 1869, at Council Bluffs, S. P. Guhl, newly come from Montana, organized the church on Williams’s authority: Council Bluffs City Congregation No. 1, under Carlton Fellows, with Hans Nielsen over the Scandinavians at Crescent City, since the Danes “were not satisfied” to have an American president; and Omaha City Congregation No. 2, for all the Saints west of the Missouri, under Jens Jensen, with a district at Florence under Niels Jensen. Rasmus Rasmussen became the first bishop. A conference at Omaha on April 6, 1869, sustained “George Williams, called of the Lord Cainan,” as “our Prophet, Seer and Revelator,” and on May 30, as Guhl left on a mission to Denmark, Andrew Hendrikson was appointed to preside, with Jens Jensen and Hans Nielsen as counselors (minutebook, 1869–1871, Church of the Firstborn (Morrisites) records, MS 31876, Church History Library; translation from the Danish generated by Claude). In March 1871 the church gave up its yearly conferences, which Hendrikson said Guhl had brought in “in likeness with the Mormons’ custom” and Williams had never commanded, and met instead in councils in members’ houses and in “social gatherings.” That May one of its leaders, Sister Marie Mortensen, joined the Reorganized Church, and Hendrikson had the early records copied into a book, “for many of us must be called hence and go to other places.” The last entry is dated June 4, 1871, at Jens Jensen’s house in Omaha. Most of the congregation moved to the Deer Lodge Valley in 1871–72.

VisitNo sites identified; the record survives in documents only.

Deer Lodge Valley

Powell and Deer Lodge counties, Montana

The longest-lived center of the movement. The wood-frame Morrisite church, built in 1878–79, still stands on private land at Racetrack, about 8 miles south of Deer Lodge: on the north side of Dempsey Road, just west of the railroad and the Interstate 90 frontage road (Exit 195; about 46.29° N, 112.75° W; NE¼ sec. 8, T6N R9W, Powell County; until 1901 this was Deer Lodge County, the county named in older records and in this guide’s photograph captions). The county lists it as a frame building of 18 by 36 feet (Montana Cadastral, Montana State Library, consulted November 6, 2026). In the 1870s the council also met in members’ houses on Dempsey Creek, near the site: in December 1876 it set its next meeting for Peter Jansen’s place on Dempsey Creek (council minutes, December 3, 1876, Church History Library, MS 31876, Minutes, 1876; translation from the Danish generated by Claude). It was last photographed for this guide in August 2020. Called the Lord’s House, as the panel over its doors reads, it was the church’s meeting place: George Thompson was elected president of the church there in 1886, and Andrew Hendrikson (1886–1921) and George Johnson (1921–1954) presided over the Montana church from it. It is the only Morrisite church building known to survive, and its condition has worsened markedly since 2009. See the condition record under Images.

The Deer Lodge paper, the New North-West, spoke well of its Morrisite neighbors. In June 1878 it reported that the post office at Gwendale, Deer Lodge County, had been renamed Morristown, and that “as many of the people are Morrisites” they would likely take to the name. In 1883 it called the settlements on Warm Springs Creek and the upper Deer Lodge River “largely composed” of Morrisites and praised them as industrious, hospitable and law-abiding; in 1885 it noted “a large number of Morrisites in this valley” (New North-West, June 7, 1878, p. 3; April 13, 1883, p. 2; October 30, 1885, p. 3; see Newspapers). The paper’s notices place Gwendale in the upper Deer Lodge valley, near Warm Springs and Lost Creek. It was school district No. 6; in April 1875 “the citizens of Upper Deer Lodge Valley” met at the Gwendale school house on the railroad question, with Morgan Evans in the chair and a W. Watkin Thomas on the resolutions committee; Gwendale primaries were held at Wier’s store; and a smelter there began work in August 1877 (New North-West, April 16, 1875, p. 2; June 18, 1875, p. 3; August 31, 1877, p. 3). Morgan Evans, whose land later became Anaconda, gave Gwendale as his address. By 1883 the same school district, No. 6, was called Willow Glen, with Peter Jessen, a Gwendale man since the 1870s, as a trustee and J. I. Eardley as clerk (New North-West, May 4, 1883, p. 3). In March 1880 the land office listed Warm Springs settlers whose homestead claims were held up while it decided whether the ground was mineral land; among them were Daniel James and William P. Thomas, on Warm Springs Creek east of where Anaconda was built (secs. 33 and 34, T5N R10W), and Morgan Evans and Peter Jessen, in sec. 9, T4N R10W, near today’s Opportunity (New North-West, April 30, 1880, p. 4); all four claims were patented as homesteads in 1880–81. In January 1882 the paper reported that John R. Eardley, formerly of San Francisco, had built a store at “Daniel James’ corner, Warm Springs settlement,” and that the neighborhood had petitioned for a post office there “to be called Willow Glen”; in March it reported his appointment as postmaster “at Willow Glen on Warm Spring creek, where the Gwendale postoffice formerly was” (New North-West, January 20, 1882, p. 3; March 10, 1882, p. 3). Gwendale, renamed Morristown in 1878, and Willow Glen were thus successive post offices for the same neighborhood, between Anaconda, Opportunity and Warm Springs. The exact site of the Gwendale school house is still not known.

View onlyThe church is privately owned, unsafe and not open to the public. View it from Dempsey Road or the frontage road; do not enter the property.

Kingdom of Heaven

Walla Walla, Washington

William W. Davies's commune near Walla Walla, 1867–1881. Its site lay on the west side of Mormon Grade Road, a few hundred yards south of Scenic Loop Road, near Russell Creek (Dan Clark, Walla Walla 2020, correspondence with the editor, November 2026). An interpretive sign that Walla Walla 2020 put up at the corner of Scenic Loop and Mormon Grade roads was shot up, replaced, shot up again and finally stolen. The road name and Davies’s grave in Lyons Cemetery remain.

VisitLyons Cemetery is public. The colony site is not marked.
