Data

The guide’s data

The lists behind the roll, the person pages and the Where they went map, as files you can download and analyze yourself, and a table of the roll you can sort and filter here. For charts the guide has already drawn, see Who they were and Where they went.

Before you count. The roll is not a full list of members: its compiler wrote that many baptized were never recorded. Sex and name origin are judged from names alone. Family Tree matches are confident unless marked probable, and the facts drawn from Family Tree come from a shared, editable tree. A blank means “not yet found,” not “none.” Small numbers can mislead; say how a figure was reached when you use one.

Citing and reuse. The files are free to reuse under CC BY 4.0, with credit. Cite the release you used: Rogers, Eric Paul, ed. Morrisite.org Data. Release 1.2, November 12, 2026. https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24154693. Corrections are welcome at contact@morrisite.org.

Where they were, decade by decade

The number of different people from the roll whom a record places in each state or region in each decade, from census, testimony, land, death and other records (the same data as the Where they went map). A person found in two places in one decade counts in both. The counts follow the surviving records: the 1870 and 1880 censuses supply most of the 1870s and 1880s, and from 1900 on almost every entry is a death record, so the later columns show where people died rather than where they lived. An empty cell means no one has been found there yet.

Changes between releases
  • 1.0 (November 10, 2026): First release: the roll (430 names), whereabouts after 1862, family ties on the roll, and others named as Morrisites.
  • 1.1 (November 11, 2026): Correction: John Peter Lorensen’s death year (1870) and place (Nevada) removed from the roll and whereabouts files; Family Tree gives only “before 1870, probably Nevada,” and the 1870 census finds his family in Nevada without him. Others named: George Johnson’s birth (Denmark, 1856) added from a 1902 biography.
  • 1.2 (November 12, 2026): Others named: four people added from the Council Bluffs and Omaha registers of 1869–72 (Church History Library, MS 32226): Anders and Karen Søndermark and Hans Knudsen, baptized at South Weber in 1861, and Carleton Fellows; entries for Joseph Morris, Andrew Hendrikson, and Isabella and James Bowman expanded from the registers and the Hendrickson letters. Correction: George Williams’s entry no longer says he prophesied in 1879 that Deer Lodge County would be the site of the Second Coming. Roll: Josiah Price’s later affiliation (Reorganized Church) added.

The roll of membership, 1861–62

430 rows · 35 KB
What it is
One row for each of the 430 names on the roll printed by George S. Dove & Co. in 1886, with what the guide has added.
Download
morrisite-roll-1861-62.csv
Columns
roll_name
The name as printed on the roll. A repeated name takes its order on the roll in brackets, e.g. Hanson, Rasmus [1].
sex_from_name
F or M, judged from the given name alone; blank if unclear.
name_origin
Scandinavian, English-language, German or Swiss, or Unclear, judged from the name alone. Not evidence of birthplace.
familysearch_id
The FamilySearch Family Tree person matched to this name, where a match has been made. Blank does not mean no match exists.
familytree_name
The name and dates in Family Tree.
born, birthplace, died, death_place
From Family Tree, as pulled on November 3, 2026. Family Tree is a shared, editable tree; check the sources attached there.
company, company_year
The pioneer company the person crossed the plains with, from the records attached in Family Tree.
later_affiliation
Where the person is known to have gone in religion after 1862. Blank means not yet known.
person_page
The guide’s page for the person, if there is one.

Explore the roll

Sort by any column, or narrow the list with the filters. Each name links to its place on the roll, or to the person’s own page where there is one. The rows you see can be downloaded as a CSV.

Make a table

Count the people on the roll by any two of these, for example name origin against later affiliation. Unknowns are counted in a column and row of their own, so you can see how much of each count rests on missing information. The table uses the same filters as the list above.

Small counts mislead easily. A difference of a few people between groups is well within what the gaps in the record could produce.
