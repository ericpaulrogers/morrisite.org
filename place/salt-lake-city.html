Salt Lake City

Utah. The territorial capital: the prisoners of 1862, the trials of 1863, the graves of Morris and Banks, and Camp Douglas.

The territorial capital, where the Morrisites met the courts and the church they had left, and where several of the movement’s leaders lived before and after Kington Fort. Joseph Morris spent the winter of 1853–54 in the city with his brother George, and on July 5, 1854, a Joseph Morris was ordained a high priest there and enrolled in the city’s high priests quorum (George Morris reminiscences, MS 1614, p. 80; High Priests Quorum record of members, 1845–1871, CR 1000 2, p. 21, Church History Library). George Williams lived in the city with his family in 1860, and at the start of 1869 left “his home in South Cottonwood” for England; his family stayed in the 8th Ward (1860 U.S. census, ark:/61903/1:1:MH2W-GZF; Omaha minutebook, 1869–1871, MS 31876).

After the siege the prisoners were marched to the city. Burton’s posse reached the Court House on June 17, 1862, and the men were put in the county jail to await examination before Judge John F. Kinney, who bound ninety-five of them over for the March term (Journal History, June 17, 1862, CR 100 137; Deseret News, June 25, 1862). Morris and Banks were buried in the city cemetery’s strangers’ ground, in graves that are not marked (Salt Lake City Cemetery records, nos. 1469–1470; see Where is Joseph Morris buried?). The trials of March 1863 were held in the city, and John Parson carried Governor Harding’s pardon of March 31 to the penitentiary (Eardley, Gems of Inspiration, p. 31). Some Morrisites had spent the winter of 1862–63 at Camp Douglas, cutting wood for the troops, and in May 1863 many left the territory under Patrick Edward Connor’s protection (“The Morrisite Movement,” letter 7 of “The Elder,” Salt Lake Tribune, July 12, 1891, p. 3).

Mark H. Forscutt joined the Reorganized Church in the city in 1865 and left Utah after threats in 1866 (Saints’ Herald 10, 1866, pp. 142–43). Robert T. Burton was tried there in 1879 for the death of Isabella Bowman and acquitted (see The Morrisite War).

VisitThe Salt Lake City Cemetery is public; the graves of Morris and Banks are not marked.

  1. 1842–1853Joseph Morris’s early life, as his elder brother George tells it. Joseph joined the church in England, “I think in 1842,” received the gift of tongues and used it unwisely, and was badly burned by fire damp, the explosive methane gas of the coal mines. He married Mary Thorp and emigrated to St. Louis, where he worked on a river steamboat; after a serious illness he became, George writes, “very flighty and Visionary,” and in Pittsburgh he made himself president of a small branch. He reached Salt Lake City in the fall of 1853 with his wife and one child and lived for a time with George, who rejected his claims (George Morris reminiscences, Church History Library, MS 1614, pp. 115–20, 179; see Documents).The Morrisites’ own history, written from the other side, agrees on nearly all of this and adds detail: he worked as a hired lad among the farmers, became a collier at eighteen, and was “badly burned in a mine explosion which disabled him for one year”; he married Mary Thorpe in England; he was a fireman on a steamboat at St. Louis for about two years; at Pittsburgh the branch accepted his doctrine and made him president, but “owing to some dissatisfaction he resigned”; and in Salt Lake City he spent about six months with George digging wells, making adobes and fencing (The Spirit Prevails (1886), “History of Events”, p. 2). The two differ on his baptism: George thought it was in 1842, while the Morrisite history says he was baptized at Dukinfield “at the age of twenty-three,” about 1848. Which is right is not settled. The Church History Biographical Database dates the marriage to Mary Thorpe February 15, 1847, and names their child, Anna Eliza Morris.It lists Mary and the child in the Moses Clawson company of 1853, notes that Joseph is not on that company’s roster, and quotes George that “he drove a team for some one across the plains” to pay his way (Church History Biographical Database, “Joseph Morris (1822–1862),” accessed November 21, 2026; no sources shown on the page). Contemporary records bear out the outline. In the 1841 census at Dukinfield, Cheshire, a household headed by Joseph “Morreys,” 40, shoemaker, holds George, 25, shoemaker; William, 20, coal miner; Joseph, 15, coal miner; Mathew, 12; and Jane, four months: George’s brothers by name, the year before George emigrated (1841 census, Dukinfield, Cheshire, HO 107/109/12, p. 17, The National Archives). On February 15, 1847, in the parish church at Mottram in Longdendale, “Joseph Moris,” 23, bachelor, collier, of Stayley, married “Mary Thorp,” 20, spinster, weaver, of Stayley; both fathers were shoemakers, Joseph Moris and Thomas Thorp, and bride and groom both signed with a mark (Mottram in Longdendale, St. Michael, marriage register, 1844–1848, p. 205, no. 410 (Cheshire Archives and Local Studies)).In August 1850 Joseph Morris, 27, and Mary Morris, 23, were living in St. Louis with Ann Thorpe, 50, and William Thorpe, 27, all born in England (1850 U.S. census, St. Louis, Missouri, Ward 5, p. 214, enumerated August 5, 1850). Three children born to them died in infancy: Ann, born at Newton Moor, Hyde, on December 28, 1847; Sarah Jane, born June 26, 1849; and Joseph, born December 16, 1851, the last two at St. Louis (Merrill D. Beal, Henry Allen Beal and George Washington Bean: Pioneers on the Utah Frontier (Garland, Utah: V. W. Johns, 1971), p. 35); only Joseph is in the 1850 household. Their daughter, Ann Eliza, was born on the way west on September 2, 1853, at “Greenwood, Wyo,” according to her death certificate, which names her parents as Joseph Morris and Mary Thorpe; she married Alfred Pehrson and died at Ephraim, Utah, on June 3, 1939 (Utah certificate of death, Sanpete County, 1939, State Board of Health file no. 57625).   2. Mar–Apr 1854A petition for divorce in Sanpete County. On April 6, 1854, the probate court of Sanpete County, Judge George Peacock presiding, “took up the following case, Joseph Morris vs Mary Morris, Petition for Divorce”; the case was “investigated,” and the papers were filed in the clerk’s office and marked E (Sanpete County Probate Court, Minutes, vol. A, 1852–1866, p. 6; FamilySearch DGS 007638304, image 15). The minute does not record the outcome; in 1853 and 1854 the clerk often entered only that a case had been investigated and its papers filed, and the papers themselves have not been found. The petitioner is the later prophet. George Morris writes that his brother came to Utah in the fall of 1853 with his wife, Mary Thorp, and that she left him there (George Morris reminiscences, Church History Library, MS 1614, pp. 115–20).In the running narrative of his own life George is more exact: Joseph “lived with mee thro the winter,” and “in the Spring he went down South to his wifes Brother where she left him and he returned alone in the summer of 54” (MS 1614, p. 80). That agrees with the court record and with Mary’s staying in Sanpete, rather than with the Morrisite account below that she went back to Salt Lake City. John Banks, drawing on George S. Dove’s manuscript history of Morris, adds that after about six months with George he went south to San Pete in the spring of 1854, that his teaching got him into trouble there, and that the bishop caused his wife to leave him and take their property back to Salt Lake City (Banks, “A Document History of the Morrisites in Utah,” University of Utah thesis, 1909, p. 5; the same account is printed in The Spirit Prevails (1886), “History of Events”, p. 2).In March 1854 the Deseret News printed two seventies quorum reports that list a Joseph Morris of Great Salt Lake City, the 12th Quorum’s (March 2, 1854, p. 2) and that of the 37th Quorum, organized January 12, 1854 (March 30, 1854, p. 3); there were other men of the name in Utah, and neither entry has been confirmed as his. Kirk Watson found the divorce entry and the two lists (correspondence with the editor, November 2026). Mary stayed in Sanpete. On July 4, 1854, three months after the petition, she married Henry Beal at Ephraim (W. H. Lever, History of Sanpete and Emery Counties, Utah, Ogden, 1898, p. 302; Ephraim Enterprise, February 23, 1911, p. 4). Beal was later mayor of Ephraim and president of the Snow Academy; they had seven children, and Morris’s daughter Ann Eliza was raised in their household (Merrill D. Beal, Henry Allen Beal and George Washington Bean: Pioneers on the Utah Frontier (Garland, Utah: V. W. Johns, 1971), p. 35).Their daughter Mary Jemima was the mother of Henry Thorpe Beal Isaacson, a counselor in the First Presidency to David O. McKay from 1965 to 1970: the first wife of the Morrisite prophet was the grandmother of a Latter-day Saint general authority, who was not himself a descendant of Morris. Mary Thorpe Beal died at Ephraim on September 12, 1905, aged 78, having lived there, her death certificate says, for 51 years (Utah certificate of death, Sanpete County, 1905, registered no. 18). New
  3. 5 Jul 1854Ordained a high priest at Great Salt Lake City. The membership record of the High Priests Quorum enters a Joseph Morris as ordained at G.S.L. City on July 5, 1854, and enrolled in the quorum the same day, by J. L. Haywood [probably Heywood] and A. Hoagland; the entry gives no birthplace or parents (High Priests Quorum record of members, 1845–1871, Church History Library, CR 1000 2, p. 21). Morris himself later wrote that he had received an ordination on earth “to the office of a high priest” “in the High Priests’ Quorum, Great Salt Lake City,” which he said only acknowledged an ordination first received in heaven (Eardley, Gems of Inspiration, p. 53). His own statement names the same quorum, and the date falls after his spring in San Pete (see Mar–Apr 1854). Kirk Watson found the quorum entry and the passage (correspondence with the editor, November 2026).
  4. Nov 1859George S. Dove moves Morris from American Fork to Salt Lake City, where, by the Morrisites’ own history, he stays “in a vacant house and lot in the First Ward” and receives “the revelation called the Keys of the Priesthood” (The Spirit Prevails, “History of Events,” p. 3). The Doves’ earlier printing of 1879 dates the move to November 1859, says that George’s father, James Dove, had refused several times to allow it, and places the revelation “in an old wagon box covered with canvass, in the same lot with the house he [George] occupied, in the first Ward”; it adds that of the church’s leaders only Heber C. Kimball urged Brigham Young to hear him (George S. and James Dove, A Voice from the West, San Francisco, 1879, p. 6). Both accounts then send him to Slaterville, in the spring of 1860 (The Spirit Prevails) or the fall of 1859 (1879). The date of “Keys” is uncertain: Emanuel Strom’s Swedish copy dates it at South Weber, December 12, 1860 (see the Teachings).
  5. May–Jun 1861An informer’s report sent to Brigham Young describes Morris’s Sunday meetings. On May 12 six people are confirmed by three men laying on hands together, Morris, Richard Cook and John Parson, and Cook gives “an explanation of the diagram.” Morris says: “I say now I profess to be the Seventh Angel. The seventh thousand years is now ushered in. Joseph held the 6th seal of the 6th dispensation.” Parson reads a revelation of May 11 commanding “those who believed in him (Morris) to gather forthwith to South Weber.” On May 26 Morris says that “one month would not elapse before the judgments of God would be poured out” (“Doctrines of the Morrisites, May 12, 1861,” informer’s report, “Morrisite papers, 1861–1862,” Brigham Young office files, CR 1234 1, Church History Library).At Manti, in Sanpete County, the ward’s Sunday meetings took notice. On May 26 David Candland, newly come from Great Salt Lake City, warned against “apostacy even in our midst,” referred to “Little Joseph” (Joseph Smith III, who had taken the lead of the Reorganized church in 1860) and a “man named Morris,” and “reasoned against the folly of their pretenses”; on July 7 Bishop Moffat said that he “did not know what the New Prophet Morris and followers were at,” and called their pretensions “Tomfoolery”; on July 14 Father Snow spoke on “the False Prophet, Morris, now in this Territory,” saying that greater than he would yet come “to try us and shake those that would be shaken”; and on August 25 John Eagar, back from the city, spoke of having seen “a proselite of Morris the New Prophet,” who, he said, “was at his Zenith and would now begin to go down” (Manti Ward general minutes, Historical record, 1854–1862, Church History Library, LR 5253 11, images 185, 195–96, 204).
  6. 14 Jun 1862Rain falls all day; for years after, it was called “the Morrisite rain” (Julie A. Wright, “Mormon Movement to Montana,” 2004, p. 32, drawing on the papers of Ivy Hooper Blood Hill, whose father, William Blood, was in the posse). That evening the posse fires the Ogden cannon and “the ‘Old Sow,’” a large gun sent up from Salt Lake City (Chambers). The same day the acting governor, Frank Fuller, writes to Burton that “the shedding of blood in resistance to civil authority renders execution of the law imperative”: “Let your acts still be tempered with mercy, but see that the Laws are vindicated” (copy, Brigham Young office files, CR 1234 1, Church History Library).
  7. 16 Jun 1862Of 149 men taken, counting Morris and Banks, 49 are discharged at the fort: 41 mostly older men, among them Nathan Brooks, Erick Eliason and Ole Olsen, and eight who had “surrendered during the engagement on the 13, 14 & 15 June,” among them Mark H. Forscutt, John R. Eardley, John E. Jones, William James, Griffith Thomas, William Lloyd and Joseph Moss Jr. (“Morrisite prisoners, 1862 June,” Historian’s Office collected historical documents, CR 100 397, Church History Library; second copy in Brigham Young office files, CR 1234 1). That day Fuller ordered that only “all able bodied men among your prisoners, capable of bearing arms, and who have been found in resistance to civil authority” be held for the court, and congratulated Burton on “the small number of casualties to your force” (copy, Brigham Young office files, CR 1234 1, Church History Library).That day Briant Stringham brought Morris’s scepter, two others and his crown to the Historian’s Office in Salt Lake City, where they were shown to hundreds; Wilford Woodruff copied the inscriptions and described Morris’s scepter as five feet long, eight-sided and tipped with iron, and the crown as “tin painted yellow, with red cloth stufed inside.” The bodies of Morris and Banks were washed and laid out at the City Hall, where nearly 2,000 people crowded to see them before the doors were closed; Woodruff examined them and found Morris shot once through the head and twice in the breast and shoulders, Banks once through the neck (Wilford Woodruff journal, January 1, 1860 – October 22, 1865, MS 1352, Church History Library, transcribed at the Wilford Woodruff Papers, June 16, 1862).
  8. 17 Jun 1862The other 98 prisoners are brought to Salt Lake City (prisoner list, “Morrisite prisoners, 1862 June,” Historian’s Office collected historical documents, CR 100 397, Church History Library; Chambers dates the march to the day after the surrender). The Journal History entry for the day, compiled later in the Historian’s Office, has Burton and the posse reaching the Court House at 4 p.m. with “about 85 prisoners,” who were put in the county jail to await examination before Judge Kinney (Journal History, June 17, 1862, CR 100 137, Church History Library).
  9. 24–25 Jun 1862In Salt Lake City, by an entry the Historian’s Office copied from its “Documentary History of the Church,” “Parsons,” very probably John Parson, read to the Morrisites “(so it is reported)” an epistle in which Brigham Young told the bishops to employ and feed any of them who were willing to work; Parson told the others that they had been “grandly humbugged,” and that he meant to go to work and advised them all to do the same. The next day Young sent Dr. Jeter Clinton to the jail to dress the wounds of the Morrisite prisoners, and sent flour to feed them (Journal History, June 24 and 25, 1862, CR 100 137, Church History Library). Both entries are the church’s own record, written from the other side.
  10. Mar 1863In Salt Lake City, seven Morrisites are convicted of second-degree murder: Peter Klemgard is sentenced to fifteen years, Christian Neilsen to twelve, and Abraham Taylor, Kadrup Neilsen, Jens Christiansen, Andrew Lee and Andrew M. Mason to ten; John Parson and John E. Jones are acquitted. The other prisoners are fined $100 each for resisting arrest (Deseret News, April 1, 1863). Ten had been indicted for murder on March 19, Richard Cook, John Parson and John E. Jones among them; twenty of those bound over in June did not appear and forfeited their bail; and Lot Smith testified for the prosecution and Mark Forscutt for the defence in the trial for resisting process (Deseret News, March 18, p. 8, and March 25, p. 5, 1863). The Deseret News called the pardon a “flagrant outrage” (April 1, 1863, p. 5). The new territorial governor, Stephen S. Harding, pardons them all a few days later.Harding later recalled that no Mormon signed the petitions for pardon but Bill Hickman, who rode fourteen miles at night to sign, and that Bishop Woolley warned him he “could not answer for my safety” if he pardoned the men (Bill Hickman, Brigham’s Destroying Angel (New York, 1872), p. 216; see The Morrisite War). During the trials, on March 8 and 24, believers in Great Salt Lake City received a prophecy in tongues, “given by L. B.” and interpreted by “Br. B.”: they would be “gathered together again,” a “reorganisation of the faithful” would soon take place, those in prison would be “delivered as Daniel,” and “the Records hidden in the earth” would soon be in their hands (Community of Christ Library Archives, P11-1 f5). It is the earliest dated Morrisite text yet found from after the siege.
  11. May 1863Many Morrisites leave Utah under the protection of Patrick Edward Connor, commanding the District of Utah. On May 5 a party of 160 (53 families, seven single men, and four widows) sets out with a company of California Volunteers; it reaches Soda Springs on May 20, where Connor has a town site surveyed on the north bank of the Bear River beside a new military post, Camp Connor (established by order of May 23). The Morrisites call their town Morristown. Connor also gives 150 Morrisites passage to Carson City, Nevada, in an empty supply train. Others settle in California. (Connor’s report, June 2, 1863, Official Records, ser. 1, vol. 50, pt. 1, 226–29.) A writer who camped beside that train at Deep Creek, the Overland Mail station kept by Howard Egan, remembered six-mule army wagons carrying two Morrisite families each, bound for Fort Churchill under a lieutenant, with Richard Cook among them; Egan, who had been with the posse in 1862, gave them a supper and a dance (“The Morrisite Movement,” letter 7 of “The Elder,” Salt Lake Tribune, July 12, 1891, p. 3). Some Morrisites had spent the winter at Camp Douglas, cutting wood for the troops.John L. Bear, driving a Swiss brother’s ox team, reached Soda Springs “on the 3rd of June, 1863,” to snow that night; the settlers lived in dugouts, Captain Black’s men brought logs, flour and bacon, and “all felt happy to be out of Brigham’s dominion.” James Bowman, who had lost his wife at Weber, helped Bear start a Sabbath school, and later kept a store on the lot Alexander Dow left when he moved to St. Louis (Johann L. Baer, autobiography, MS 10240, Church History Library, pp. 31–36).
  12. 1860s–Some Morrisites return to the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, in Utah and elsewhere. The 2006 monument at South Weber notes that a few were rebaptized and remained there. Others find a home in the Reorganized Church (RLDS): the former Morrisite apostle Mark Hill Forscutt joins it in Salt Lake City in 1865, and John L. Bear (Johann Lebrecht Bär, 1838–1919), a Swiss convert from Affoltern am Albis who emigrated from Zürich in 1860 and lost his wife and child at Kington Fort, later serves as an RLDS missionary to Switzerland and Germany, dies in Independence, Missouri, and publishes his autobiography in the RLDS Journal of History. In 1865 RLDS missionaries at Malad, Idaho, baptize several people who appear on the roll.Others join only after years in the Morrisite groups: Erick and Caroline Eliason are baptized together at Soda Springs on June 9, 1870; Margaretta Cook leaves the Morrisites in Nevada in 1870 and is baptized RLDS in 1873; and Lena Hanson, at Soda Springs and then Deer Lodge, in 1895; Bear himself was baptized into the Reorganized Church at Malad on November 29, 1869, organized a branch at Soda Springs, and wrote a pamphlet, one copy for each Morrisite family still there, on “the fallacy and non fullfillment of many of Joseph Morris revelations”; some burned it unread, but by his account most of the Soda Springs Morrisites joined the Reorganization in time (autobiography, MS 10240, pp. 41–42; no copy of the pamphlet is known); in 1890 John R. Eardley reported that Brother Emmons of the Montana church had been baptized and that Mrs. Emmons meant to follow (see the roll).
  13. Nov 1865Writing from Salt Lake City to William James and John R. Eardley, Williams breaks into verse on the siege, his own poem in his own hand. It begins, on the pattern of Byron’s “The Destruction of Sennacherib” (“The Assyrian came down like the wolf on the fold”): “The Mormons came down like a wolf on the fold / On a morning in June that was glittering like gold.” Its lines tell of the cannon, of “the shrieks of the children and cries of the maid / Whose chin was torn off by the fierce cannonade” (Mary Christoffersen, later Mary Anderson, who testified in 1879 that a cannon ball took off her chin), and of a babe “torn from the breast by a bombshell’s embrace”; it ends with a prayer that “Seth,” Morris, “may be loosed.” Williams left it unfinished: “Here I end not that the Muse tires but that you may share the honor of placing in verse the terrible scene of immortal Weber; finish the tragedy yourselves.” It is a partisan poem written three years after the events, the earliest Morrisite telling of the siege so far found in verse (Williams to James and Eardley, G.S.L., November 1865, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to William James and John R. Eardley, 1864–1867; readings from the manuscript, some words uncertain).
  14. 1868Early in the year Williams, in Salt Lake City, thanks the Montana Saints for money sent “by Bro P. Thomas.” S. P. Guhl’s promise to send for him has come to nothing (“he tells me his horses were stolen”), his “family are very sick,” and someone is taking up a land claim for him near the Saints; George Thompson is already in England. Williams explains that by ministers he had sent “to Montana & Idaho” who “had cursed the saints instead of blessing them” he meant “the rebels Dow, Davies, Gudmanson and not … Bro Ghul,” that is, probably Alexander Dow, William W. Davies and Gudmund Gudmundson, the apostle of 1861: George Thompson wrote in 1869 of “Poor Brother Dow,” “Poor Livingstone,” “Poor Goodman Goodmunsen, poor W. W. Davis and others of the Weber House, good men,” who had been “deceived by supernatural manifestations” (George Thompson to William P. Thomas, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from George Thompson to William P. Thomas, 1869–1874). He signs “Cainan for the Lord’s sake, George Williams.” On December 14 he writes to “Bro.James & Saints of the Most High in Montana”: William James’s group is already in Montana and uses the name later borne by the Montana church. Williams wishes to visit “my beloved brethren & sisters in Montana” but has been “kept from coming,” for “the rebel who have entered my flock in Montana has laid a snare for Cainan’s life,” one he calls “the seventh born son,” who has “seduced others” but whom he does not name; “Joseph the first & Joseph the Second fell a sacrifice to plots.” Instead he is “called to travel to the States & Europe” (Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to William James and Montana followers, 1868).
  15. 1869Leaving for England. George Williams leaves Utah for England, passing through Omaha from January 18 to February 5, where the Council Bluffs and Omaha congregations are organized on his authority (see Omaha & Council Bluffs under Places); a year later he writes from Loughborough that it was “now one year since I left the continent of America”; his wife and children stay in Salt Lake City, and he asks the Montana members to send help for “my little ones” (Williams to the Saints of the Most High in Montana, January 10 [?], 1870, Church History Library, MS 31876). By June he is at Ashwicken, near King’s Lynn, Norfolk, writing to the Saints at Omaha and Council Bluffs, where Rasmus Rasmussen is “the presiding Bishop.” George Thompson is with him at Ashwicken that summer, and William P. Thomas and a Brother James come to England too: Thompson writes on July 29, 1869, to “Beloved Brethren W. P. and D. J.,” one at Llanybyther in Wales and the other at Derby.Summer and autumn in England. That summer Thompson, then a shoemaker at Ashwicken, lost touch with Williams from his letter of July 30 until September 10, when Williams wrote that he had “worked & preached and lectured in the field, harvest field, at night, on Sundays … with thieves for my companions”; S. P. Guhl had left Lynn for Hull and Denmark in July, meaning to publish a paper there (George Thompson to William P. Thomas, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from George Thompson to William P. Thomas, 1869–1874, July 29 and September 13, 1869). In September and October Williams writes from near Betchworth Castle, Surrey, “often in different parts of the Country”; on October 13 he writes that Thompson, “like a rock unto me,” will leave for Wales on October 20 to help Thomas, “then I am alone in England as far as the old Weber House is concerned,” and asks that letters be sent to him in care of W. P. Thomas at Llanybyther, Carmarthenshire.Wales, and the return of Thomas and James. His letter of December 24 is headed, apparently, from Llanybyther; “in England the Priesthood are united in love and in effort,” though the seed “certainly is falling on stony ground” (Williams to the Omaha church, September 16, October 13 and December 24, 1869, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Andrew Henrikson, 1869). The Omaha minutebook names him: on June 2, 1869, Guhl left for Denmark with “2 Welsh brethren, William P. Thomas, a High Priest, and an Aaronic Priest Daniel James,” called “to go to Wales on mission.” In March 1870 Williams writes that “Bro Thomas & Bro James,” having “attended the heavenly call,” are “now permitted to return to their families in Montana” by way of Omaha. An RLDS member at Silver Bow City, Montana, W. W. Thomas, had written on February 24 that “two members of the Canaanite persuasion have gone to Wales as Missionaries” and were expected back in the spring, adding that “they have very few followers in this country” (Saints’ Herald 17, 1870, p. 375): the earliest use of “Canaanite” for the Montana Morrisites found so far.1870–71. In May he reports the English followers “preaching on the building of the temple in this nation in our meetings on Sundays,” with the sacrament “not refused to any we know that are our real friends”; in December he writes that “the Lord is raising up a set of men to pull down Brigham’s cruel rule; I wish them success but beware of their infidel reasoning,” apparently of the Godbeites (Williams to Hendrikson and Rasmussen, May 1870, and to Hendrikson, December 28, 1870, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Andrew Henrikson, 1870). In April 1871 he thanked Hendrikson for sending the Salt Lake Tribune, the Godbeites’ new paper: it “contains some sound principles, but you will know how to sort the errors; it is also a powerful engine against the Tyrant of Salt Lake” (Williams to Hendrikson, April 1871, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Andrew Henrikson, 1871).Almerin Grow. In September 1870 he gives his own account of Almerin Grow, who had walked from California to Salt Lake on hearing of him, was told of “his mission to the Sultan & Priesthood of Mahommet,” and was later “sent to Constantinople & Jerusalem,” returning “without my consent”; Grow himself wrote from San Francisco in August 1870 that he had come overland, met “the assembly at Omaha,” and “did more in Turkey than any other living man could have done,” having “made up my mind to die in the streets of Constantinople rather than fail in my mission,” and that James Dove “has been with Davis but has left him” (Almerin Grow to Williams, August 29, 1870, enclosed with Williams to Hendrikson, September 21, 1870, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Andrew Henrikson, 1870); by 1878, John R. Eardley reported from California, Grow was preaching against Williams and “up George Dove” at San Juan (John R. Eardley to Williams, Hollister, California, [May] 1878, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to John R. Eardley, 1871–1880) (Williams to the Omaha church and to Andrew Hendrikson, 1869–70; George Thompson to William P. Thomas, 1869; and the Omaha minutebook, 1869–1871, MS 31876).Omaha and Council Bluffs. Danish-language congregations keep membership and account books in Omaha, Nebraska, and Council Bluffs, Iowa. Their registers show Andrew Hendrikson, born at Ondrup, Jutland, on July 29, 1843, baptized “in the Missouri River” at Omaha on January 22, 1869, by Rasmus Rasmussen and Hans Knudsen (“Record of members, circa 1869–1871,” Council Bluffs, Church of the Firstborn (Morrisites) records, 1869–1890, MS 32226, Church History Library; translation from the Danish generated by Claude).
  16. 20 Apr 1875Barnet Moses Giles, who that year published at Salt Lake City The Pure Testimony, claiming the presidency of the Latter-day Saint church by descent from Israel, writes to Nels Damander “and all the faithful Brethren and Sisters” in the Omaha region. He is glad they have received “the Books which i have Sent to you,” teaches that “Father adam” is “the only god whom we aught to worship,” and counsels them to make their own sacrament wine, to wash one another’s feet on the first Sunday of the month, and to be ready “to go to build up and to redeem Zion,” adding that nothing of consequence will happen before “(Saul) that is Brigham” has finished his work (copy “Writen By Josephine Jensen,” Church History Library, MS 31876, Niels Jensen record, 1870–1879; for the pamphlet, Singerman, Judaica Americana, entry 2504).
  17. 1885George S. and James Dove of the California congregation preach in Salt Lake City and the Cottonwoods, baptizing ten on September 14, then go on to Montana in October. At Willow Glen and Deer Lodge they hold meetings, baptize W. P. Thomas and L. C. Larson on October 26, and speak in the Deer Lodge courthouse; John R. Eardley orders James Dove out of his store, and at Race Track the members refuse them the Lord’s House and agree not to hear them. By the Doves’ account, “a number of brethren” in Montana pressed George S. Dove to publish Morris’s revelations “just as they were written”; it took him about seven months, “as the revelations were in a very disordered condition,” and The Spirit Prevails appeared in 1886 (James Dove, A Few Items in the History of the Morrisites, [1892], pp. 6–13, in Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to Andrew Hendrickson, 1892). In November the Montana council disowned the Doves as “pretenders” in the New North-West.

In the bibliography (6)

  • Acting Governor Frank Fuller as a Latter-day Saint. Fuller, who called out the posse, was born in Boston, trained as a physician and dentist, and worked as a newspaperman in New Hampshire before President Lincoln appointed him secretary of Utah Territory in 1861; no record found makes him a member of the Church (Mark Twain Project, biography of Frank Fuller; University of Utah, Marriott Library, Frank Fuller papers, finding aid). In March 1863 a report from Camp Douglas named him with ex-Governor Alfred Cumming and Chief Justice John F. Kinney, both non-Mormons, as officials who had followed Brigham Young’s lead (The War of the Rebellion: Official Records, ser. 1, vol. 50, pt. 2, p. 370). See Morrisite War.
