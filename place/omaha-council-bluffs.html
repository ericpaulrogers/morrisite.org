Omaha & Council Bluffs

Nebraska · Iowa. Danish-speaking congregations whose 1869 membership and account books survive.

Danish-speaking congregations whose 1869 membership and account books survive in translation. Their own minutebook, written up at Omaha in May 1871, tells how they began. Jens Jacob Poulsen, “a High Priest of the Weber House,” was sent ahead by George Williams to gather the scattered Morrisites, and spent about a year there. Williams himself arrived in Omaha on January 18, 1869, “from his home in South Cottonwood in Utah,” on his way to England, held meetings in Omaha and Council Bluffs, and left on February 5 with Poulsen and a Brother Mortensen, who went on to Denmark. On February 7, 1869, at Council Bluffs, S. P. Guhl, newly come from Montana, organized the church on Williams’s authority: Council Bluffs City Congregation No. 1, under Carlton Fellows, with Hans Nielsen over the Scandinavians at Crescent City, since the Danes “were not satisfied” to have an American president; and Omaha City Congregation No. 2, for all the Saints west of the Missouri, under Jens Jensen, with a district at Florence under Niels Jensen. Rasmus Rasmussen became the first bishop. A conference at Omaha on April 6, 1869, sustained “George Williams, called of the Lord Cainan,” as “our Prophet, Seer and Revelator,” and on May 30, as Guhl left on a mission to Denmark, Andrew Hendrikson was appointed to preside, with Jens Jensen and Hans Nielsen as counselors (minutebook, 1869–1871, Church of the Firstborn (Morrisites) records, MS 31876, Church History Library; translation from the Danish generated by Claude). In March 1871 the church gave up its yearly conferences, which Hendrikson said Guhl had brought in “in likeness with the Mormons’ custom” and Williams had never commanded, and met instead in councils in members’ houses and in “social gatherings.” That May one of its leaders, Sister Marie Mortensen, whom Williams had called the congregation’s “High Priestess” in 1870 (Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Andrew Henrikson, 1870), joined the Reorganized Church, and Hendrikson had the early records copied into a book, “for many of us must be called hence and go to other places.” The last entry is dated June 4, 1871, at Jens Jensen’s house in Omaha. Most of the congregation moved to the Deer Lodge Valley in 1871–72.

VisitNo sites identified; the record survives in documents only.

Places on the ground

The two congregations organized at Council Bluffs on February 7, 1869, and their districts, as the Omaha minutebook names them. All the markers are approximate: they show the towns, not buildings. No meeting place has yet been located. If you know where one of these places stood, please write.

Towns are placed at their old centres. The river is drawn roughly along its present course; it has moved since the 1870s (the Carter Lake bend was cut off by the flood of 1877), so its exact line then is not shown. The minutebook made the river the boundary: Omaha City Congregation No. 2 took in “all the Saints west of the Missouri.” The inset at upper left shows where Fremont and Lincoln lie, beyond this map; its rivers are drawn only roughly.

  • Council Bluffs: Congregation No. 1 Approximate 1869–1872 map
    Council Bluffs City Congregation No. 1, organized on February 7, 1869, at a conference held in Council Bluffs, with the High Priest Carlton Fellows as president. The Danish members “were not satisfied” with an American president, so Fellows was kept as president “for the English” and the Scandinavians were given their own district (see Crescent City). Councils met in Brother Damander’s house, and in 1871 Williams wrote to Andrew Hendrikson in care of Damander at P.O. Box 610, Council Bluffs. The house has not been located.
    Sources: Minutebook, 1869–1871, Church of the Firstborn (Morrisites) records, MS 31876, Church History Library; Williams’s envelopes, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Andrew Henrikson, 1870 and 1871.

    A district of Congregation No. 1 for the Scandinavian Saints at Crescent City and round about, under Hans Nielsen from February 1869 and, after he stopped holding meetings, under Peder Staffanson from March 1870.
    Sources: Minutebook, 1869–1871, Church of the Firstborn (Morrisites) records, MS 31876, Church History Library.
  • Omaha: Congregation No. 2 Approximate 1869–1872 map
    Omaha City Congregation No. 2, for “all the Saints west of the Missouri,” organized on February 7, 1869, under the High Priest Jens Jensen. Its conferences were held in Omaha, among them the one of April 6, 1869, that sustained Williams as prophet. Rasmus Rasmussen, the first bishop, had his post office box here (P.O. Box 40). The minutebook was written up at Omaha in May 1871.
    Sources: Minutebook, 1869–1871, Church of the Firstborn (Morrisites) records, MS 31876, Church History Library; Williams’s envelope to Rasmussen, June 1869, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Andrew Henrikson, 1869.
  • Florence: a district of No. 2 Approximate 1869–1870 map
    A district of Congregation No. 2 for the Saints at Florence and round about, under Niels Jensen “from Florence.” Florence, now part of north Omaha, was the site of the Latter-day Saints’ Winter Quarters of 1846–48 and a later outfitting point for emigrants to Utah.
    Sources: Minutebook, 1869–1871, Church of the Firstborn (Morrisites) records, MS 31876, Church History Library.
  • Fremont, Nebraska: a branch Approximate 1870–1873 map
    Niels Jensen “from Fremont” testified at the Omaha conference of October 16, 1870. By November 30, 1871, Fremont had a branch of its own: a council met that evening in Andrew Peterson’s house, with Andrew Hendrikson, Niels Jensen, Peter A. Just, J. Helstrøm and S. Jensen. In March 1873 Peterson wrote to Hendrikson in Montana that the Fremont members hoped to follow that summer and were trying to sell their place. About 30 miles northwest of Omaha, beyond this map.
    Sources: Minutebook, 1869–1871, Church of the Firstborn (Morrisites) records, MS 31876, Church History Library; council minutes, Fremont, November 30, 1871, and Andrew Peterson to Hendrikson, March 1873, MS 31876, Minutes, 1871, and Minutes, 1872–1873.

In the chronology

  1. 1869George Williams leaves Utah for England, passing through Omaha from January 18 to February 5, where the Council Bluffs and Omaha congregations are organized on his authority (see Omaha & Council Bluffs under Places); a year later he writes from Loughborough that it was “now one year since I left the continent of America”; his wife and children stay in Salt Lake City, and he asks the Montana members to send help for “my little ones” (Williams to the Saints of the Most High in Montana, January 10 [?], 1870, Church History Library, MS 31876). By June he is at Ashwicken, near King’s Lynn, Norfolk, writing to the Saints at Omaha and Council Bluffs, where Rasmus Rasmussen is “the presiding Bishop.” George Thompson is with him at Ashwicken that summer, and William P. Thomas and a Brother James come to England too: Thompson writes on July 29, 1869, to “Beloved Brethren W. P. and D. J.,” one at Llanybyther in Wales and the other at Derby. That summer Thompson, then a shoemaker at Ashwicken, lost touch with Williams from his letter of July 30 until September 10, when Williams wrote that he had “worked & preached and lectured in the field, harvest field, at night, on Sundays … with thieves for my companions”; S. P. Guhl had left Lynn for Hull and Denmark in July, meaning to publish a paper there (George Thompson to William P. Thomas, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from George Thompson to William P. Thomas, 1869–1874, July 29 and September 13, 1869). In September and October Williams writes from near Betchworth Castle, Surrey, “often in different parts of the Country”; on October 13 he writes that Thompson, “like a rock unto me,” will leave for Wales on October 20 to help Thomas, “then I am alone in England as far as the old Weber House is concerned,” and asks that letters be sent to him in care of W. P. Thomas at Llanybyther, Carmarthenshire. His letter of December 24 is headed, apparently, from Llanybyther; “in England the Priesthood are united in love and in effort,” though the seed “certainly is falling on stony ground” (Williams to the Omaha church, September 16, October 13 and December 24, 1869, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Andrew Henrikson, 1869). The Omaha minutebook names him: on June 2, 1869, Guhl left for Denmark with “2 Welsh brethren, William P. Thomas, a High Priest, and an Aaronic Priest Daniel James,” called “to go to Wales on mission.” In March 1870 Williams writes that “Bro Thomas & Bro James,” having “attended the heavenly call,” are “now permitted to return to their families in Montana” by way of Omaha. In May he reports the English followers “preaching on the building of the temple in this nation in our meetings on Sundays,” with the sacrament “not refused to any we know that are our real friends”; in December he writes that “the Lord is raising up a set of men to pull down Brigham’s cruel rule; I wish them success but beware of their infidel reasoning,” apparently of the Godbeites (Williams to Hendrikson and Rasmussen, May 1870, and to Hendrikson, December 28, 1870, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Andrew Henrikson, 1870). In April 1871 he thanked Hendrikson for sending the Salt Lake Tribune, the Godbeites’ new paper: it “contains some sound principles, but you will know how to sort the errors; it is also a powerful engine against the Tyrant of Salt Lake” (Williams to Hendrikson, April 1871, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Andrew Henrikson, 1871). In September 1870 he gives his own account of Almerin Grow, who had walked from California to Salt Lake on hearing of him, was told of “his mission to the Sultan & Priesthood of Mahommet,” and was later “sent to Constantinople & Jerusalem,” returning “without my consent”; Grow himself wrote from San Francisco in August 1870 that he had come overland, met “the assembly at Omaha,” and “did more in Turkey than any other living man could have done,” having “made up my mind to die in the streets of Constantinople rather than fail in my mission,” and that James Dove “has been with Davis but has left him” (Almerin Grow to Williams, August 29, 1870, enclosed with Williams to Hendrikson, September 21, 1870, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Andrew Henrikson, 1870); by 1878, John R. Eardley reported from California, Grow was preaching against Williams and “up George Dove” at San Juan (John R. Eardley to Williams, Hollister, California, [May] 1878, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to John R. Eardley, 1871–1880) (Williams to the Omaha church and to Andrew Hendrikson, 1869–70; George Thompson to William P. Thomas, 1869; and the Omaha minutebook, 1869–1871, MS 31876). Danish-language congregations keep membership and account books in Omaha, Nebraska, and Council Bluffs, Iowa. Their registers show Andrew Hendrikson, born at Ondrup, Jutland, on July 29, 1843, baptized “in the Missouri River” at Omaha on January 22, 1869, by Rasmus Rasmussen and Hans Knudsen (“Record of members, circa 1869–1871,” Council Bluffs, Church of the Firstborn (Morrisites) records, 1869–1890, MS 32226, Church History Library; translation from the Danish generated by Claude).
  2. 1870–71Williams’s followers in Denmark. S. P. Guhl, who had sailed for Denmark in June 1869, and J. P. Lorentzen found a congregation of “the Most High Saints” in Copenhagen, preaching publicly there from January 1, 1871, in a hall in Adelgade, and supporting themselves by their own work; Lorentzen, Guhl and C. B. Berg form a committee to publish tracts (letter from Copenhagen, February 10, 1871). By November 1871 Guhl has broken with Williams. Writing from Denmark on November 5 to Mikkel Christiansen and “Seth’s disciples,” he says they had organized the church in Scandinavia on Williams’s orders but have now “rejected him from our hearts and our assemblies,” objecting to Williams’s oaths (“God’s law says thou shalt not swear at all”), to his appointing one leader after another “to further his speculations to get money,” and to his claim to the Urim and Thummim, which Guhl says he “never had, or else lost” (Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to Andrew Hendrickson, 1869–1871; translation from the Danish generated by Claude). Guhl was back in Omaha, again under Williams, by 1874 (see the roll note).
  3. 1870sMany members regather in the Deer Lodge Valley, Montana, under George Williams, the “Prophet Cainan,” who claims to be Morris's successor. In July 1874, following a revelation in Williams’s epistle from Ashby of April 6, that “my Servant W. James take charge of those that are convenient unto him, and my Servant A. Henrickson take charge of those convenient unto him, and there shall be two Stakes under one Head, even my own chosen Servant Cainan,” the Montana church is divided into two stakes (Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1874–1875; William P. Thomas brought the epistle from the post office and read it to the Saints one Sunday): Stake No. 1 on Warm Spring Creek, which met at a meeting house at Willow Glen under William James, and Stake No. 2 at Race Track under Andrew Hendrikson (Church History Library, MS 32226; see Repositories). Stake No. 2’s own minutes record its founding general meeting on July 12, 1874. Hendrikson spoke to “the brothers and sisters who had chosen to be under his presidency”: they lived in two places, Warm Spring and Deer Lodge, about equally many in each, and Jens Jensen was appointed to preside over the meetings at Deer Lodge. From then on only members of “our organization” were to sit in its councils, not those of “the other organization” (general meeting, July 12, 1874, MS 31876; translation from the Danish generated by Claude). Hendrikson was living at Race Track by February 1878. A local history of Anaconda dates the first Morrisite settlers in the valley to 1863, among them William Price Thomas on Warm Springs Creek, east of the later townsite; has a group under William James arriving in 1867 as others left; and describes a caravan of thirteen covered wagons that came in June 1871, part of a larger migration from Iowa, Nebraska, Utah and Idaho: Scandinavian families, mostly Danes, with English, Scottish and Welsh believers. It places the two congregations in the upper valley near Lost Creek and near Race Track Creek, with about eighty members in all (Patrick F. Morris, Anaconda, Montana, 1997, pp. 15–17). Williams’s letters from England direct the move from Omaha: in September 1871 “Bro Merrick” sets out for Montana “with his cattle,” in the spring of 1872 Williams sends “instructions for gathering up to Montana,” and by December 1872 Andrew Hendrikson’s mail goes to the Warm Spring Creek post office (Williams to Hendrikson and the Omaha church, 1871–72, MS 31876). Not everyone left Nebraska at once. A branch at Fremont met in Andrew Peterson’s house in November 1871, and in March 1873 Peterson wrote to Hendrikson that the Fremont members hoped to come to Montana that summer and were trying to sell their place (MS 31876, Minutes, 1871, and Minutes, 1872–1873). Williams himself was in England, living by 1876 in George Thompson’s house at Walthamstow, Essex: “I am now writing in Bro Thompson’s house where I have been tarrying some weeks” (to William James, July 13, 1876, Church History Library, MS 31876).
  4. 2 Aug 1871Williams sends instructions for the gathering to Montana. Andrew Hendrikson had asked “the object of gathering together in Montana”: “it is our Lords Command that his Chosen People may come together to receive blessings Spiritual & temporal and the order of both Priesthoods,” with a temple “prepared to receive the ministering angels of our God.” The Saints need not all live in one place, but should be “in organized companies, that is, families near together, acknowledging one head set to preside”; William James is to preside, “taking the High & holy Priesthood, their first Quorum, himself & Counsellors, then as they are found to stand in twelve.” If John Merrick takes his cattle up to Montana, Hendrikson may go with him (Williams to Hendrikson, Loughborough, August 2, 1871: the English original, pages 5–10 only, is filed in Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Andrew Henrikson, 1872–1873; Hendrikson’s complete Danish copy, made at Omaha on August 28, 1871, is in the folder for 1871).
  5. 22 Sep 1872At the house of William P. Thomas on Warm Spring Creek, the Montana Saints meet “for the purpose of Organizing the Church of Jesus Christ of the Saints of the Most high,” following a general epistle Williams sent from England in August. William James is appointed president, with W. P. Thomas and Andrew Hendrikson as counselors; Rasmus Rasmussen is ordained bishop, with Daniel James and Nels Damander as his counselors; Peter Staffenson is to preside over the second quorum of Seventies; and Margaret Thomas is reordained “as the Lady elect to preside over the Female Order.” A revelation through Williams of August 22 had called James “on a Mission to Nevada,” leaving his counselors in charge, and he left in haste “with a Teamster down to Corrine.” A copy of the revelation, dated August 20, sent him to Austin, Nevada, to help “my son J. D. Bowen,” whose affairs were about to fall into trouble, so that his property might be “saved from those who are looking for speculation profit,” and to bring him back to Montana. Bowen wrote to William P. Thomas from Austin in October 1874 that he had since lost everything, but that Cainan had “sent his servant” two years before to try to save his property (Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to William P. and Margaret Thomas, 1871–1874; translation from the Welsh generated by Claude). A second meeting on November 10 “complete[s] the organization” by ordaining six men to the Seventies. The minutebook’s title page dates the emigration of “the Saints of the Most high from the States of Iowa and Nebraska” from the summer of 1871 (minutebook, September 1872–January 1874, Church of the Firstborn (Morrisites) records, 1869–1890, MS 32226, Church History Library).
  6. 1873The Montana council minutes, kept in English this year, show the church at Warm Springs while its leaders are away. William James is on “his Mission in Nevada.” Williams calls William P. Thomas “on a Mission to Salt Lake,” and he appoints Andrew Hendrikson by letter “to take the Presidency, assisted by the Bishop and his Counselors,” while both are absent (May 5, 1873). In March a committee of four tries a quarrel between Thomas and Daniel James over the line between their lands and Daniel James’s cattle, set on by dogs as they passed Thomas’s place. It tells the two to forgive each other, but Thomas rejects the ruling as “Develish talke.” In July, Damander, who had hosted the Council Bluffs councils, turns against Williams, saying “Cainan was a Man like Brigham Young to lead the People astray.” James takes back the presidency on July 20. In September the council meets privately at Rasmus Rasmussen’s house to keep Thomas out and agrees to report him to Williams; Thomas, Williams’s appointed first counselor and “our second man in authority,” is to be left alone until Williams answers. In December the sisters in the town of Deer Lodge complain that Margaret Thomas did not invite them to the sisters’ feast at Warm Springs (council minutes, March–December 1873 and January 12, 1874, Church History Library, MS 31876). In California the same year, George Dove, who with his father James had followed Williams and then Davies, begins receiving communications and organizes a Church of the Firstborn of about thirty people (Watson, p. 198). James Dove’s own account places the Davies years in San Francisco: back there in January 1868, he “found a few who had accepted W. W. Davies,” joined them and preached among them; it does not say whether he accepted Davies’s claims himself, nothing so far places the Doves at Walla Walla, and by 1892 he wrote of Davies with open hostility (James Dove, A Few Items in the History of the Morrisites, [1892], pp. 3, 6, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to Andrew Hendrickson, 1892). James Dove’s own account puts the organizing a little later: George’s communications began the year before John Parson set up his “kingdom” in San Francisco on April 7, 1874, and only after that did “the word of the Lord” come “that we were to organize into a church called the Church of the Firstborn,” with five baptized first and about twenty-nine members (James Dove, A Few Items in the History of the Morrisites, [1892], pp. 4–5, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to Andrew Hendrickson, 1892).
  7. 20 Apr 1875Barnet Moses Giles, who that year published at Salt Lake City The Pure Testimony, claiming the presidency of the Latter-day Saint church by descent from Israel, writes to Nels Damander “and all the faithful Brethren and Sisters” in the Omaha region. He is glad they have received “the Books which i have Sent to you,” teaches that “Father adam” is “the only god whom we aught to worship,” and counsels them to make their own sacrament wine, to wash one another’s feet on the first Sunday of the month, and to be ready “to go to build up and to redeem Zion,” adding that nothing of consequence will happen before “(Saul) that is Brigham” has finished his work (copy “Writen By Josephine Jensen,” Church History Library, MS 31876, Niels Jensen record, 1870–1879; for the pamphlet, Singerman, Judaica Americana, entry 2504).
  8. 1881–90The Reorganized Church gathers members in the Deer Lodge Valley. Its Deer Lodge Branch register opens with baptisms by W. W. Blair in the valley on August 21, 1881. Joseph C. Clapp, who in 1886 offers the Morrisites the use of the new RLDS meeting house in Deer Lodge City, baptizes at Deer Lodge and Willow Glen from 1886 to 1890, among others three men who had been Morrisites at Omaha: Peter Nielsen (1887), Niels Jensen (1890) and Niels Thorkelsen (1890) (Record of the Deer Lodge Branch, Montana District, #228, Community of Christ Library Archives; Record of members, 1869–1872, MS 32226, Church History Library).
