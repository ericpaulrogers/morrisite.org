England, Wales & Denmark

Overseas. George Williams and George Thompson in England, 1869–1886, the Welsh mission of 1869–70, and the congregation in Copenhagen.

George Williams, the “Prophet Cainan,” led his followers from England by letter for the last thirteen years of his life. He left Utah about January 1869, passing through Omaha, and by June was at Ashwicken, near King’s Lynn, Norfolk, where George Thompson, then a shoemaker, was with him; that autumn he wrote from near Betchworth Castle, Surrey, “often in different parts of the Country” (Williams to the Omaha church, 1869; George Thompson to William P. Thomas, July 29 and September 13, 1869, MS 31876). He wrote from Loughborough in 1870 and 1871, and from Ashby by Brigg, Lincolnshire, in 1874, where his epistle of April 6 divided the Montana church into two stakes. About January 24, 1875, he moved into Thompson’s house at 1 Aglan Place, Shernhall Street, Walthamstow, Essex, and he died there on April 25, 1882. Thompson, elected president of the church in Montana in 1886, was writing to the members that December from Thornwood Common, Epping, Essex (MS 31876).

In June 1869 two Welsh brethren from Montana, William P. Thomas, a high priest, and Daniel James, an Aaronic priest, left Omaha, called “to go to Wales on mission”; that summer Thomas was at Llanybyther, Carmarthenshire, and James at Derby, and in March 1870 Williams wrote that they could return to their families in Montana (Omaha minutebook, June 2, 1869; Williams to the Omaha church, 1869–70, MS 31876).

In Denmark, the Latter-day Saint mission president at Copenhagen reported in November 1868 that “the Morrisites have some half crazy representatives here” (Jesse N. Smith to George A. Smith, November 3, 1868, MS 1322). S. P. Guhl sailed for Denmark in June 1869, and with J. P. Lorentzen he gathered a congregation of “the Most High Saints” in Copenhagen, which was preaching publicly in a hall in Adelgade from January 1, 1871. By November 1871 Guhl had broken with Williams; whether the Copenhagen members followed him is not known, and Guhl was back in Omaha, again under Williams, by 1874 (MS 31876; see the chronology).

VisitNo Morrisite site is known to survive in England, Wales or Denmark.

In the chronology (12)

  1. 1869Leaving for England. George Williams leaves Utah for England, passing through Omaha from January 18 to February 5, where the Council Bluffs and Omaha congregations are organized on his authority (see Omaha & Council Bluffs under Places); a year later he writes from Loughborough that it was “now one year since I left the continent of America”; his wife and children stay in Salt Lake City, and he asks the Montana members to send help for “my little ones” (Williams to the Saints of the Most High in Montana, January 10 [?], 1870, Church History Library, MS 31876). By June he is at Ashwicken, near King’s Lynn, Norfolk, writing to the Saints at Omaha and Council Bluffs, where Rasmus Rasmussen is “the presiding Bishop.” George Thompson is with him at Ashwicken that summer, and William P. Thomas and a Brother James come to England too: Thompson writes on July 29, 1869, to “Beloved Brethren W. P. and D. J.,” one at Llanybyther in Wales and the other at Derby.Summer and autumn in England. That summer Thompson, then a shoemaker at Ashwicken, lost touch with Williams from his letter of July 30 until September 10, when Williams wrote that he had “worked & preached and lectured in the field, harvest field, at night, on Sundays … with thieves for my companions”; S. P. Guhl had left Lynn for Hull and Denmark in July, meaning to publish a paper there (George Thompson to William P. Thomas, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from George Thompson to William P. Thomas, 1869–1874, July 29 and September 13, 1869). In September and October Williams writes from near Betchworth Castle, Surrey, “often in different parts of the Country”; on October 13 he writes that Thompson, “like a rock unto me,” will leave for Wales on October 20 to help Thomas, “then I am alone in England as far as the old Weber House is concerned,” and asks that letters be sent to him in care of W. P. Thomas at Llanybyther, Carmarthenshire.Wales, and the return of Thomas and James. His letter of December 24 is headed, apparently, from Llanybyther; “in England the Priesthood are united in love and in effort,” though the seed “certainly is falling on stony ground” (Williams to the Omaha church, September 16, October 13 and December 24, 1869, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Andrew Henrikson, 1869). The Omaha minutebook names him: on June 2, 1869, Guhl left for Denmark with “2 Welsh brethren, William P. Thomas, a High Priest, and an Aaronic Priest Daniel James,” called “to go to Wales on mission.” In March 1870 Williams writes that “Bro Thomas & Bro James,” having “attended the heavenly call,” are “now permitted to return to their families in Montana” by way of Omaha. An RLDS member at Silver Bow City, Montana, W. W. Thomas, had written on February 24 that “two members of the Canaanite persuasion have gone to Wales as Missionaries” and were expected back in the spring, adding that “they have very few followers in this country” (Saints’ Herald 17, 1870, p. 375): the earliest use of “Canaanite” for the Montana Morrisites found so far.1870–71. In May he reports the English followers “preaching on the building of the temple in this nation in our meetings on Sundays,” with the sacrament “not refused to any we know that are our real friends”; in December he writes that “the Lord is raising up a set of men to pull down Brigham’s cruel rule; I wish them success but beware of their infidel reasoning,” apparently of the Godbeites (Williams to Hendrikson and Rasmussen, May 1870, and to Hendrikson, December 28, 1870, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Andrew Henrikson, 1870). In April 1871 he thanked Hendrikson for sending the Salt Lake Tribune, the Godbeites’ new paper: it “contains some sound principles, but you will know how to sort the errors; it is also a powerful engine against the Tyrant of Salt Lake” (Williams to Hendrikson, April 1871, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Andrew Henrikson, 1871).Almerin Grow. In September 1870 he gives his own account of Almerin Grow, who had walked from California to Salt Lake on hearing of him, was told of “his mission to the Sultan & Priesthood of Mahommet,” and was later “sent to Constantinople & Jerusalem,” returning “without my consent”; Grow himself wrote from San Francisco in August 1870 that he had come overland, met “the assembly at Omaha,” and “did more in Turkey than any other living man could have done,” having “made up my mind to die in the streets of Constantinople rather than fail in my mission,” and that James Dove “has been with Davis but has left him” (Almerin Grow to Williams, August 29, 1870, enclosed with Williams to Hendrikson, September 21, 1870, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Andrew Henrikson, 1870); by 1878, John R. Eardley reported from California, Grow was preaching against Williams and “up George Dove” at San Juan (John R. Eardley to Williams, Hollister, California, [May] 1878, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to John R. Eardley, 1871–1880) (Williams to the Omaha church and to Andrew Hendrikson, 1869–70; George Thompson to William P. Thomas, 1869; and the Omaha minutebook, 1869–1871, MS 31876).Omaha and Council Bluffs. Danish-language congregations keep membership and account books in Omaha, Nebraska, and Council Bluffs, Iowa. Their registers show Andrew Hendrikson, born at Ondrup, Jutland, on July 29, 1843, baptized “in the Missouri River” at Omaha on January 22, 1869, by Rasmus Rasmussen and Hans Knudsen (“Record of members, circa 1869–1871,” Council Bluffs, Church of the Firstborn (Morrisites) records, 1869–1890, MS 32226, Church History Library; translation from the Danish generated by Claude).
  2. 1870–71Williams’s followers in Denmark. As early as November 1868 the Latter-day Saint mission president in Copenhagen, Jesse N. Smith, wrote that “the Morrisites have some half crazy representatives here, who occasionally pick up some poor worn out Mormon of ours,” recalling Wilford Woodruff’s saying that “the Morris sieve was the coarsest” of all the sieves used to sift the Saints (Jesse N. Smith to George A. Smith, November 3, 1868, George A. Smith papers, MS 1322, Church History Library, images 40–41). S. P. Guhl, who had sailed for Denmark in June 1869, and J. P. Lorentzen found a congregation of “the Most High Saints” in Copenhagen, preaching publicly there from January 1, 1871, in a hall in Adelgade, and supporting themselves by their own work; Lorentzen, Guhl and C. B. Berg form a committee to publish tracts (letter from Copenhagen, February 10, 1871).By November 1871 Guhl has broken with Williams. Writing from Denmark on November 5 to Mikkel Christiansen and “Seth’s disciples,” he says they had organized the church in Scandinavia on Williams’s orders but have now “rejected him from our hearts and our assemblies,” objecting to Williams’s oaths (“God’s law says thou shalt not swear at all”), to his appointing one leader after another “to further his speculations to get money,” and to his claim to the Urim and Thummim, which Guhl says he “never had, or else lost” (known from Andrew Hendrikson’s copy, made at Fremont in March 1872: Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to Andrew Hendrickson, 1869–1871; translation from the Danish generated by Claude). Guhl was back in Omaha, again under Williams, by 1874 (see the roll note).
  3. 1870sMany members regather in the Deer Lodge Valley, Montana, under George Williams, the “Prophet Cainan,” who claims to be Morris's successor. In July 1874, following a revelation in Williams’s epistle from Ashby of April 6, that “my Servant W. James take charge of those that are convenient unto him, and my Servant A. Henrickson take charge of those convenient unto him, and there shall be two Stakes under one Head, even my own chosen Servant Cainan,” the Montana church is divided into two stakes (Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1874–1875; William P. Thomas brought the epistle from the post office and read it to the Saints one Sunday): Stake No. 1 on Warm Spring Creek, which met at a meeting house at Willow Glen under William James, and Stake No. 2 at Race Track under Andrew Hendrikson (Church History Library, MS 32226; see Repositories). Stake No. 2’s own minutes record its founding general meeting on July 12, 1874.Hendrikson spoke to “the brothers and sisters who had chosen to be under his presidency”: they lived in two places, Warm Spring and Deer Lodge, about equally many in each, and Jens Jensen was appointed to preside over the meetings at Deer Lodge. From then on only members of “our organization” were to sit in its councils, not those of “the other organization” (general meeting, July 12, 1874, MS 31876; translation from the Danish generated by Claude). Hendrikson was living at Race Track by February 1878. A local history of Anaconda dates the first Morrisite settlers in the valley to 1863, among them William Price Thomas on Warm Springs Creek, east of the later townsite; has a group under William James arriving in 1867 as others left; and describes a caravan of thirteen covered wagons that came in June 1871, part of a larger migration from Iowa, Nebraska, Utah and Idaho: Scandinavian families, mostly Danes, with English, Scottish and Welsh believers. It places the two congregations in the upper valley near Lost Creek and near Race Track Creek, with about eighty members in all (Patrick F. Morris, Anaconda, Montana, 1997, pp. 15–17).Williams’s letters from England direct the move from Omaha: in September 1871 “Bro Merrick” sets out for Montana “with his cattle,” in the spring of 1872 Williams sends “instructions for gathering up to Montana,” and by December 1872 Andrew Hendrikson’s mail goes to the Warm Spring Creek post office (Williams to Hendrikson and the Omaha church, 1871–72, MS 31876). Not everyone left Nebraska at once. A branch at Fremont met in Andrew Peterson’s house in November 1871, and in March 1873 Peterson wrote to Hendrikson that the Fremont members hoped to come to Montana that summer and were trying to sell their place (MS 31876, Minutes, 1871, and Minutes, 1872–1873). Williams himself was in England, living by 1876 in George Thompson’s house at Walthamstow, Essex: “I am now writing in Bro Thompson’s house where I have been tarrying some weeks” (to William James, July 13, 1876, Church History Library, MS 31876).
  4. 2 Aug 1871Williams sends instructions for the gathering to Montana. Andrew Hendrikson had asked “the object of gathering together in Montana”: “it is our Lords Command that his Chosen People may come together to receive blessings Spiritual & temporal and the order of both Priesthoods,” with a temple “prepared to receive the ministering angels of our God.” The Saints need not all live in one place, but should be “in organized companies, that is, families near together, acknowledging one head set to preside”; William James is to preside, “taking the High & holy Priesthood, their first Quorum, himself & Counsellors, then as they are found to stand in twelve.” If John Merrick takes his cattle up to Montana, Hendrikson may go with him (Williams to Hendrikson, Loughborough, August 2, 1871: the English original, pages 5–10 only, is filed in Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Andrew Henrikson, 1872–1873; Hendrikson’s complete Danish copy, made at Omaha on August 28, 1871, is in the folder for 1871).
  5. 22 Sep 1872At the house of William P. Thomas on Warm Spring Creek, the Montana Saints meet “for the purpose of Organizing the Church of Jesus Christ of the Saints of the Most high,” following a general epistle Williams sent from England in August. William James is appointed president, with W. P. Thomas and Andrew Hendrikson as counselors; Rasmus Rasmussen is ordained bishop, with Daniel James and Nels Damander as his counselors; Peter Staffenson is to preside over the second quorum of Seventies; and Margaret Thomas is reordained “as the Lady elect to preside over the Female Order.” A revelation through Williams of August 22 had called James “on a Mission to Nevada,” leaving his counselors in charge, and he left in haste “with a Teamster down to Corrine.” A copy of the revelation, dated August 20, sent him to Austin, Nevada, to help “my son J. D. Bowen,” whose affairs were about to fall into trouble, so that his property might be “saved from those who are looking for speculation profit,” and to bring him back to Montana.Bowen wrote to William P. Thomas from Austin in October 1874 that he had since lost everything, but that Cainan had “sent his servant” two years before to try to save his property (Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to William P. and Margaret Thomas, 1871–1874; translation from the Welsh generated by Claude). A second meeting on November 10 “complete[s] the organization” by ordaining six men to the Seventies. The minutebook’s title page dates the emigration of “the Saints of the Most high from the States of Iowa and Nebraska” from the summer of 1871 (minutebook, September 1872–January 1874, Church of the Firstborn (Morrisites) records, 1869–1890, MS 32226, Church History Library). Two dolls that belonged to Peter Staffanson’s granddaughters Myrtle and Evelyn Staffanson are at the Copper Village Museum and Art Center in Anaconda (see Images, MO-IMG-105 and 106).
  6. 1874–75In England, John R. Eardley has come to Williams “with his beloved wife & family and quickly engaged in business” (Williams to Margaret Thomas, July 27, 1874). Williams still means to come to Montana: in April 1874 he asks about crops, prices, the chance of “a Railway coming thro either from the States or from Ogden,” and “when I come where do you intend to put me for a while” (epistle of April 6, 1874, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1874–1875). About January 24, 1875, Williams leaves Ashby for George Thompson’s house at 1 Aglan Place, Shernhall Street, Walthamstow. Thompson, writing to William P. Thomas on January 31, asks: “Have you heard of Brother Warner? He claim[s] to have seen Bro Joseph & to be sent to warn the brethren to gather to Weber, that the Shepherds of Utah will be cut off in 1875 & the Saints be gathered to Weber in 1876.I fear this is too sweet to be true”; and, “I am looking for him & at Weber.” On May 10, 1875, Williams answers such prophecy: “if one cometh saying to Seth cometh to Weber in such a time or times or half a time, the chart & compass of a righteous judgment is gone.” In the same letter he warns against a man who “can keep putting off the coming of Jesus until years has passed away,” and writes that “John Parsons, Livingstone, Davy, Dove [or Dow] and others, after admitting the legal Priesthood of Cainan, fell and attempted a business of their own; these failed and did not do very much injury” (“Davy” is probably Davies, whom Williams elsewhere calls “Doctor Davy”) (Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Margaret and William P. Thomas, 1866–1882).In November 1875 John R. Eardley reported from Hollister, California, that “Bro. Warner—the German brother” had been through that country warning the Weber Saints “to prepare for the coming of Bro. Joseph next June” (John R. Eardley to William James, Hollister, California, November 9, 1875, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to William James, 1874–1875). Brother Warner was almost certainly Joseph Warner of South Weber, a German-born Morrisite who testified in 1879 that a vision of Joseph Smith had got him cut off from the Latter-day Saint church (see Who was “Brother Warner”?).
  7. 1878–79Montana Morrisites build a wood-frame church, the Lord’s House, a mile or so below Racetrack, south of Deer Lodge. About 1877 Williams wrote from England that he was glad the church “is to have a House and the workmen all in the covenant” (MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to William James and Montana followers, 1876–1878), but the site was settled only on February 24, 1878. In September 1878 Williams sent the words of dedication and told James to walk round the building, opening and closing “standing on the South East Corner, from whence cometh the greatest light” (Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to William James and Montana followers, 1876–1878). That day a joint council of both stakes met in Andrew Hendrikson’s house at Race Track, with William James presiding. Some wanted the house on Warm Spring Creek or on Peder Jansen’s land, and Rasmus Rasmussen cited “Cainan’s letter that it was to be build on Land of some faithful Brother.” By ten votes to five the council chose “H.Land,” Hendrikson’s land (council minutes, February 24, 1878). Peder Jansen superintended the building (“havde forestaaet Bygningen”). In July 1879 the Race Track council moved its public Sunday meetings into the Lord’s House at 10:30 a.m., with the brethren to sit on the right and the sisters on the left (council minutes, July 24, 1879). The House was dedicated on August 9, 1879: in July 1882 the council planned to celebrate August 9, “som var 3de Aarsdagen siden at Herrens Huus blev indviet” (“which was the third anniversary of the dedication of the Lord’s House”; council minutes, July 29, 1882). The first council minuted “i Herrens Huus” met on September 27, 1879. A special council for both stakes on October 25, 1879, found that the brothers and sisters had given $634.70 in cash, with $76.73 still owing, to be paid by November 10 (council minutes, 1878–82, Church History Library, MS 31876; translation from the Danish generated by Claude).
  8. 6 Jan 1879Writing from Walthamstow to William James and the “Saints of the Most High in Stake No. 1 on the Sides of the North in Montana,” George Williams says that the region where they live is “the Sides of the North” (Psalm 48:2), where “the City of the great King” will come down before the Lord’s coming: “you are the pioneers.” He sets no date. The same letter calls the Lord’s House, then building, “my first House of Prayer on the Sides of the North” (Andrew Hendrickson’s copy of 1883, letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1878–1881, MS 31876, Church History Library). It is “not the only House of Prayer for Montana; finish it and it will be revealed to you how to raise another more easily at the proper time.” Williams still hoped to come: “if our Merciful God will release me I shall be there to help. How long, dear & Merciful Father, am I required to be a Hunter & a witness for you?”; Peter Staffanson, he had heard, “has a waggon & wishes to ride me round” (same letter, original, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1878–1881). See Teachings.
  9. 1882–83Williams’s death. Williams dies in England on April 25, 1882, “being in his 68 year,” after about two years of great pain from “an enlargement of the bone” in his shoulder joint; George Thompson, who had done his writing for him, still knew of no one “appointed to fill his place” in August 1883 (Thompson to William P. Thomas, August 9, 1883, MS 31876). The movement splits among rival successors. In Montana, where the church is known as the Church of Jesus Christ of Saints of the Most High, Williams had directed that William James, its local president, be put before the church a year after his death: “Let the organisation remain as it is, for twelve months after my decease, then let brother James be placed before the whole Church when assembled at the years end, and if sustained by uplifted hand, there let him remain, and so on, from year to year” (Williams to the Montana brethren, January 29, 1882, Church History Library, MS 31876).“I leave you in charge.” An envelope he addressed to James, postmarked at New York on March 26, bears the words “I leave you in charge after my decease.” In his last weeks George Thompson, who lived near him in Walthamstow, answered the Montana church’s questions for him: on March 23, 1882, he wrote that “Bro Cainan is no better but gradually getting weaker & weaker,” and that if Williams named no one to replace a Sister Rasmussen in her office, the church should choose someone “and we will confirm your choice” (MS 31876). A week earlier, on March 16, Sister Theodora had written for him to Margaret Thomas of “my martyrdom out of sight of my family,” asking her to befriend his daughter Adelaide, then in Montana, “being absent from her Mother and Sisters” (Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Margaret and William P. Thomas, 1866–1882).His last letters. The same day he wrote to William James that Adelaide, living with the Daniel James family, “is now a member with you,” thanked Sister Daniel James, “the Sister who offered me a home if I could come to Montana,” for five dollars sent “in the time of my affliction,” and welcomed James’s hint of a match between Adelaide and his son: “our generations might meet and go down through time together; tell me his age, is he steady and likely to make a good husband” (Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to William James and Montana followers, 1879–1882). The match did not take place: Adelaide never married, and William R. James married Elizabeth Frances Emmons at Deer Lodge in 1886 (FamilySearch Family Tree, KWJ8-P3L; L6V6-ZNQ; California Death Index, ark:/61903/1:1:VPQR-KCV).James sustained, 1883. On April 25, 1883, a general assembly in the Lord’s House sustains James by 19 votes to 10, and he chooses Andrew Hendrikson and John R. Eardley as counselors. Several of the minority, among them Erick Eliason and Lars Beck, refuse to accept the vote, and the Race Track bishop, Rasmus Rasmussen, who will not sustain James, keeps the church’s records and is suspended (Church History Library, MS 32226; see Repositories).The minority removed. On May 26 the Race Track council removes those who would not accept the vote: Hendrikson said that the teachers “had left us of their own will, because they were not satisfied with the president election and the organization of this church.” Peder Jansen and Lars Beck are released as teachers, Erik Eliason as deacon and keeper of the Lord’s House, and Jens Jensen as Hendrikson’s counselor and scribe; the Jensens, the Becks, the Jansens, Hans Golde, Eliason and Peder Just are released “from membership and the church’s obligations,” since they “had separated themselves from us.” George Johnson is made bishop of the stake, and a Sunday school and a fortnightly prayer meeting are begun (minutes, May 26, 1883, Church History Library, MS 31876, Minutes, 1883–1884; translation from the Danish generated by Claude).Order restored, 1884. By February 1884 Johnson reports “no disorder among the Saints such as ther were before the separation” (minutes, February 24, 1884, Church History Library, MS 31876, Minutes, 1883–1884). (Eardley, later a stake president, leaves Montana about 1900.) In the Danish-speaking stake at Race Track, the council minutes record the other side of the vote: in December 1882 Andrew Hendrikson read Williams’s last instructions that, if no new servant of the Lord came before the anniversary, the church might “selv … vælge en Leder for Kirken” (“itself choose a leader for the church”); in February 1883 he said that since “Cainan havde recommenderet Wm. James” he would uphold him, while Lars Beck said he could not see that choosing a president would do them any good (Race Track council minutes, December 30, 1882, and February 24, 1883, MS 31876; translation from the Danish generated by Claude). George Dove leads the California congregation.
  10. 1886At the yearly general assembly in the Lord’s House at Race Track, Montana, on April 25, George Thompson of London is elected president of the church; William James, who had claimed the presidency for life “by birthright,” resting on Williams’s envelope “I leave you in charge,” stays away. Eleven members who decline to sustain Thompson are given thirty days to do so. At a court meeting on May 10 James is suspended “for the crimes of usurpation of authority, and heresy,” and John R. Eardley is put over the Warm Spring Creek stake (Church History Library, MS 32226; see Repositories); Daniel James is suspended too, as a teacher and member, “for rebellion against the teachings, laws and authority of the church.” A separate tally of the April vote lists seventeen votes for Thompson, women and girls among them (Church History Library, MS 31876, Minutes, 1886–1887).Those who held out were led by the officers of Stake No. 1, William M. James as stake president, Peter Steffanson as bishop and Daniel James as teacher; six of them met and sustained William James as their president, and rumors went round that “John the Revelator” was coming (Eardley to Hendrikson, May 2, 1886, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to Andrew Hendrickson, 1880–1889). That December Thompson was living in England, writing to the Montana brethren from Thornwood Common, Epping, Essex (MS 31876). Thompson is probably the last general president. Andrew Hendrikson, a Danish immigrant who was never a Latter-day Saint, presides over the Montana Morrisites from 1886 until his death on July 3, 1921.
  11. 1887In March John R. Eardley reports that “we are to have the Doves here again on the 17th,” to the unease of some members, two years after the church had disowned them. He is also in touch with the followers of David Whitmer: Whitmer has sent a postcard promising pamphlets, the Expositor has printed an article on the “Church of Christ” signed “P.J.” and a dedicatory prayer by John Parson, and Brother Emmons, on a visit to Missouri, means to call on Whitmer at Richmond and go on to Independence and Lamoni. Joseph Smith III has written to Eardley of a proposed new place of worship opposite the Temple Lot at Independence. Later that month Eardley sails for England, where his mother is ill, planning to see Thompson and, on his way, the Kirtland Temple, Lamoni, Independence and “Father Whitmer”; Brother Strom is to conduct the meetings while he is gone (Eardley to Hendrikson, March 11 and 18, 1887, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to Andrew Hendrickson, 1880–1889).
  12. 1888–89George Thompson, still president of the church, writes from Thornwood Gate, Epping, that he is “here alone on an island,” uncertain how the parable of the ten virgins will be fulfilled, and wary of spiritualism: “I shall stick to the chosen few.” In January 1889 Eardley subscribes for himself and Hendrikson to The Return, a Whitmerite paper he calls “a thorn in the side of the Reorganized Church.” That winter he is chief clerk of the Council of the 16th Legislative Assembly at Helena (Thompson to Eardley, October 7, 1888, and February 17, 1889; Eardley to Hendrikson, January 7 and March 8, 1889, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to Andrew Hendrickson, 1880–1889).
