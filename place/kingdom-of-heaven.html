Kingdom of Heaven

Walla Walla, Washington. William W. Davies’s community near Walla Walla, 1867–1881.

William W. Davies's commune near Walla Walla, 1867–1881. Its site lay on the west side of Mormon Grade Road, a few hundred yards south of Scenic Loop Road, near Russell Creek (Dan Clark, Walla Walla 2020, correspondence with the editor, November 2026). An interpretive sign that Walla Walla 2020 put up at the corner of Scenic Loop and Mormon Grade roads was shot up, replaced, shot up again and finally stolen. The road name and Davies’s grave in Lyons Cemetery remain.

VisitLyons Cemetery is public. The colony site is not marked.

Places on the ground

Sites of Davies’s community, 1867–1881, and of what remains of it. Solid markers and tracts are located; outlined markers are approximate; sites not yet located are listed without a marker. The colony land is private today. If you know where one of these places stood, please write.

Tracts are drawn from their legal descriptions on the BLM section grid. The corner of Scenic Loop and Mormon Grade roads is from OpenStreetMap.

  • The colony’s eighty acres Located 1867–1881 map
    After the 1881 judgment against Davies, the sheriff levied on his land, “the W½ NW¼ sec. 34, T7N R37E,” less one acre, and sold it at the courthouse door on July 16, 1881, for $1,975. A witness in the case spoke of the eighty acres “where he resides,” so this was almost certainly the camp. The tract lies on the west side of Mormon Grade Road just south of its corner with Scenic Loop Road, which matches Dan Clark’s placing of the site near Russell Creek. Private land; the site is not marked.
    Sources: Washington State Archives, Eastern Region Branch, Walla Walla Frontier Justice, case 2373 (WAL-3153); Dan Clark, Walla Walla 2020, correspondence with the editor, November 2026; section lines from the BLM National PLSS dataset.
  • Scenic Loop and Mormon Grade roads Located today map
    The road named for the colony. Walla Walla 2020 put an interpretive sign about Davies’s community at this corner; it was shot up, replaced, shot up again and finally stolen.
    Sources: Walla Walla 2020; OpenStreetMap.
  • Swen O. Hagg’s homestead Located patented 1890 map
    Swen O. Hagg (the roll’s Svend Hegg), who came to Walla Walla with the first five families in 1867 and was living in Davies’s house in 1880, patented a 170-acre homestead about three miles east-southeast of the camp in 1890 (S½ SE¼ sec. 1, T6N R37E, and lots in sec. 6, T6N R38E). The square shows the part in section 1.
    Sources: Bureau of Land Management, General Land Office Records (glorecords.blm.gov); section lines from the BLM National PLSS dataset.
  • The Davies cabin, Fort Walla Walla Museum Located today map
    A cabin in which Davies and his family lived has been moved to the grounds of the Fort Walla Walla Museum. Public, during museum hours.
    Sources: Wikipedia, “William W. Davies”; OpenStreetMap.
  • Davies’s other land Not yet located 1870s
    A witness in 1881 listed 160 acres on Mill Creek, 160 acres “on the mountains” in Oregon or near the line, and 40 to 80 acres more. These tracts have not yet been found in the land records.
    Sources: Deposition of V. D. Lambert, case 2373.
  • Davies’s grave, Lyons Cemetery Not yet located 1906
    Davies died in 1906 and is buried in Lyons Cemetery, north of Mill Creek Road. Public. Its position is not yet on this map.
    Sources: Wikipedia, “William W. Davies.”

In the chronology

  1. 1867–1881William W. Davies, who in an 1867 pamphlet declared that a third dispensation had been opened through him on January 24, 1866, setting baptism aside for a covenant vow (Watson, p. 124), leads a Morrisite offshoot, the Kingdom of Heaven, near Walla Walla, Washington. He declares his infant son Arthur to be Christ returned, the “Walla Walla Jesus.” Lawsuits by former members end the community: in 1881 a court awards three of them $3,225, and the sheriff sells the community’s livestock and its eighty acres to satisfy the judgment.
  2. 1873The Montana council minutes, kept in English this year, show the church at Warm Springs while its leaders are away. William James is on “his Mission in Nevada.” Williams calls William P. Thomas “on a Mission to Salt Lake,” and he appoints Andrew Hendrikson by letter “to take the Presidency, assisted by the Bishop and his Counselors,” while both are absent (May 5, 1873). In March a committee of four tries a quarrel between Thomas and Daniel James over the line between their lands and Daniel James’s cattle, set on by dogs as they passed Thomas’s place. It tells the two to forgive each other, but Thomas rejects the ruling as “Develish talke.” In July, Damander, who had hosted the Council Bluffs councils, turns against Williams, saying “Cainan was a Man like Brigham Young to lead the People astray.” James takes back the presidency on July 20. In September the council meets privately at Rasmus Rasmussen’s house to keep Thomas out and agrees to report him to Williams; Thomas, Williams’s appointed first counselor and “our second man in authority,” is to be left alone until Williams answers. In December the sisters in the town of Deer Lodge complain that Margaret Thomas did not invite them to the sisters’ feast at Warm Springs (council minutes, March–December 1873 and January 12, 1874, Church History Library, MS 31876). In California the same year, George Dove, who with his father James had followed Williams and then Davies, begins receiving communications and organizes a Church of the Firstborn of about thirty people (Watson, p. 198). James Dove’s own account places the Davies years in San Francisco: back there in January 1868, he “found a few who had accepted W. W. Davies,” joined them and preached among them; it does not say whether he accepted Davies’s claims himself, nothing so far places the Doves at Walla Walla, and by 1892 he wrote of Davies with open hostility (James Dove, A Few Items in the History of the Morrisites, [1892], pp. 3, 6, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to Andrew Hendrickson, 1892). James Dove’s own account puts the organizing a little later: George’s communications began the year before John Parson set up his “kingdom” in San Francisco on April 7, 1874, and only after that did “the word of the Lord” come “that we were to organize into a church called the Church of the Firstborn,” with five baptized first and about twenty-nine members (James Dove, A Few Items in the History of the Morrisites, [1892], pp. 4–5, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to Andrew Hendrickson, 1892).
