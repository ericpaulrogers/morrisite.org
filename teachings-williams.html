George Williams and the Montana church (1863–1954)

George Williams, the Prophet Cainan (1863–1882)

What Williams kept and changed

Williams built on Morris’s teaching and also altered it. He kept the order of sevens and birthrights, the diagram of worlds, Cain’s line, the robe (which he said Morris had wanted every minister to wear) and the condemnation of Brigham Young. His line of servants, from Enos (Joseph Smith) to Seth (Morris) to Cainan, follows a sequence Morris had already given in 1860, telling Young that “Joseph Smith was my forerunner and I was the forerunner to Adam” (address to Brigham Young, September 22, 1860, Joseph Morris Papers, P11-1 f5, Community of Christ Library Archives). Other things changed. Morris had looked for a temple in “the land of promise” and given no endowments “in this place” (The Spirit Prevails, No. 30); Williams said Jackson County had “left the paths of virtue,” placed the gathering on “the Sides of the North” in Montana, and brought robes and “Temple ordinances” into the Lord’s House there.

Morris set days for Christ’s coming; Williams wrote that “it is not for us to set the times.” Morris foretold the destruction of the wicked; Williams taught that nearly all would be saved, by grades. Tongues, central at Kington Fort, were ruled out of public meetings in Montana in 1881; the “elect lady” of 1861 grew into the office of High Priestess; and Williams departed from Morris’s rule of one wife for eternity. In 1881 he also read Morris’s claim to be Moses as a manner of speaking: Morris “meant he represented Moses.” Each point is documented in the paragraphs that follow and under Joseph Morris above.

His calling

Williams, the Prophet Cainan, told of visions in 1862. On April 15, near Harker’s Canyon, at the foot of the mountains on the west side of Salt Lake Valley, he saw the armies of heaven and the Father and the Son; from August 25 to 27, on a mountain east of the valley, John the Revelator baptized him, and Seth and Enos, who had “sealed our testimony with our blood,” declared him “Cainan, then Melchisedec, now George Williams” and gave him the keys of the priesthood, among them the keys of birthright and of sealing (“Interviews with Celestial Beings,” Watson, pp. 81–90; Gems of Inspiration). A Danish copy of the account, in a record book begun in 1870 among copies of Williams’s letters of that year, shows it already circulating among the Danish-speaking Saints by about 1870. The copy names Harker’s Canyon, gives Williams’s birthplace as “England, Surrey district,” and says that John, who tarried with the prophet Bartholomew in a northern land “on the other side of the North Pole,” baptized him in a spring beside a brook (Church History Library, MS 31876, Niels Jensen record, 1870–1879; translation from the Danish generated by Claude).

Seth is Morris. Enos was long taken in this guide to be John Banks, killed with him, but Williams himself wrote in December 1881 that “Joseph Morris said Joseph Smith was Enos, but meant he represented Enos; he also said he was Moses, but meant to say he represented Moses,” and that he, Williams, had used the same “mode of expression” in calling himself Cainan; omitting the word “represent,” he wrote, “may have caused wrong impression” (Williams to Andrew Hendrikson, December 29, 1881, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1878–1881).

George Thompson had used the same reading in 1869: “The Ministers of Enos — or J. S. — brought something like the little Book. Seth — or J. Morris — brought something very much resembling the little Book” (George Thompson to William P. Thomas, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from George Thompson to William P. Thomas, 1869–1874, July 29, 1869). An earlier witness is the Danish record of a meeting at Omaha on January 26, 1869, with Williams present: Hans Knudsen testified to “the three prophets Enos, Seth and Cainan,” and Cainan said that “40 years back in time Enos came forth with a revelation and organized a church,” and that Joseph Smith had died twenty-five years before, leaving a church without prophet, seer and revelator (“Cainans Diverse Skrivelser,” January 26, 1869, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Andrew Henrikson, 1869; translation from the Danish generated by Claude).

Williams himself wrote that autumn of “our Lord and his apostles, Joseph the first, Joseph the second and the Lord’s Servant,” and that “the Josephs, Seth & Enos leaveth me not comfortless” (Williams to the Omaha church, September 16 and October 13, 1869, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Andrew Henrikson, 1869). In 1872 he listed those whose birthright entitled them “to be visited by these immortals”: “Adam with Jehovah, Moses with Jehovah, Abraham with Jehovah, Jesus with Moses & Elijah, Paul with Jesus, Joseph with Moroni, with Peter, James & John, Joseph the second with the Lord of Hosts, Cainan with John the Revelator, Seth & Enos” (Williams to the Saints in Montana, August 1872, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Andrew Henrikson, 1872–1873). The line of servants in his letters runs from Enos to Seth to Cainan, that is, from Joseph Smith to Morris to Williams; Smith and Morris had both “sealed” their testimony with their blood.

Williams also claimed the use of the Urim and Thummim and said he had met Muhammad, Joseph Smith and Morris (Watson, pp. 80, 92–96). From 1869 he led from England, by letter (see Chronology). In May 1875 he wrote that God would lay no other foundation in this generation than “Seth, Enos & Cainan the first Born Sons of the first Presidency in the last quorum of Heavens,” and that “I could not avoid being Cainan, and whosoever is offended with me is offended with the Eternal Father that have chosen me” (Williams to Margaret Thomas, May 10, 1875, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Margaret and William P. Thomas, 1866–1882).

Mahomet

In August 1877, with Russia and Turkey at war, Williams told the Montana Saints of an earlier “Interview with Mahomet.” His own copy of it, headed “Copied from my Journal in the year 1867,” places it in Utah: “While on a visit with a friend called the Stranger under the Mountains by the Mouth of Little Cottonwood Kanyon,” a voice told him to send the friend away, and in “a wood of cottonwood trees” a figure in green appeared and said, “I am Mahomet, and before I was Mahomet I was Ishmael the Son of Abraham and Hagar” (Church History Library, MS 31876, Writings of Cainan, circa 1867–1872). The 1877 retelling likewise has Mahomet appear to him “as he sat on the banks of the river Jord[an]” (Williams to Hendrikson, summer 1877, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1876–1877).

Mahomet, “who was Ishmael by Hagar Abraham’s second wife in holy records,” had appeared to him with a message for the Sultan and the Sheik-ul-Islam: the Muslims were to “be Brothers with the Christians” and learn their arts and sciences, or be “scourged by fire & sword,” for “Messiah of the Christians and Mahomet the Prophet of God to you is in harmony,” and so were Williams, “Enos & Seth.” Almerin Grow, Williams wrote, carried the revelation “without purse and scrip” through England, France and Gibraltar to Constantinople and presented it to the Sultan through the American minister, but the Sheik-ul-Islam, “fearing some foreign interference with his own office,” advised the Sultan “to take no action”; the war, in Williams’s view, was “the result of this … non-acceptance” (Williams to Hendrikson and Stake No. 2, August 9, 1877, Montana copy in the record of letters from Cainan, 1873–1879, Church History Library, MS 31876).

About 1871 he had written that “the mission to the House of Mahomet was undertaken by Grow. No other could accomplish this,” and that Grow “delivered, altho’ in weakness, the message of the Lord to that people,” but that the work was now “hidden from his vision, and he rages against the instrument employed” (Williams to Robert Ford, Loughborough, February 7, 1871, letter copy book, circa 1866–1877, Church of the Firstborn (Morrisites) records, 1869–1890, MS 32226, Church History Library). For Williams’s account of 1870 see the Chronology.

New records

Williams said he had translated ancient records from St. Ann’s Hill in Surrey; the chapters that survive describe the origin of the gods, their dwellings, their incarnations and reincarnations, and the making of worlds (Watson, pp. 96–110). In 1876 he told the Montana Saints that it was “not wisdom at this time” to give them the records “until there is union of feeling,” for “it is the highest sacrilege to debate or dispute on sacred things”: “the Book of Mormon nearly overthrew the work of the first Joseph” (Williams to the Saints in Montana, April 23, 1876, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1876–1877). He also explained Enoch’s Zion: at Enoch’s request it was “broken off from this Planet & set between this & Paradise,” where it still revolves, and it “will be reunited to this” (Williams to Andrew Hendrikson, 1877, same folder). A Montana copy of two chapters of these records, the first in Swedish, survives in the record of letters from Cainan, 1873–1879, Church History Library, MS 31876.

The second, “of all existence,” tells how the Almighty Parent called the elements into a first celestial planet, and how “the Mighty Goddess brought forth the firstborn, a son & daughter,” who “hold the first birthright,” so that “each one has a 1st born son & daughter of God to commence each creation, even as Adam & Eve.” It ends “Translated by Cainan, who desires this to be read with great care & earnest prayer.” About 1871 Williams told the Thomases he had sent Montana “two Chapters from the Records” and was forwarding things “connected with the records & the European temple.” In July 1871 he wrote to Hendrikson that the records were “taken from the Hill St Anns by the Lord’s Servant. The first Chapter is sent to Montana and will be continued as the work is translated,” and that they would not please “the curious & vain” or “those who are looking for wonders and miraculous powers” (Williams to Hendrikson, July 24, 1871, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Andrew Henrikson, 1871); this is the earliest date found for them.

The English original of the first chapter, in Williams’s hand, is headed “Translation from Records taken from the Hill St. Annes, County of Surrey, England,” and begins “In the first beginning — space, Element, intelligence, power and spirit”; a copy was being made in the Deer Lodge Valley by March 10, 1872, and Andrew Hendrikson translated it into Danish (Church History Library, MS 31876, Writings of Cainan, circa 1867–1872; translation from the Danish generated by Claude). In 1876 he held back further chapters “on account of the disunion,” then in July sent Margaret Thomas a chapter “on Chambers of the Dead, copied from the St Ann’s Hill records by Sister Theodora,” of the English sisterhood, who in 1874 had signed herself “Theodora, High Priestess in England” (Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Margaret and William P. Thomas, 1866–1882). Two copies of this third chapter survive: on a small planet set beside the earth, “called the Chambers of the Dead,” are kept “the Bodies of the just,” of Adam, Seth, Enos, Cainan and the patriarchs, of Elijah and Moses “and Jesus our dear Lord,” first bodies that are “animated by the voice and command of God to be used for his purposes and the Earth’s salvation” (Church History Library, MS 31876, Writings of Cainan, circa 1867–1872).

Earlier lives and birthrights

At Council Bluffs and Omaha in 1869 Williams told converts who they had been in earlier lives: Carlton Fellows had been Jonas, the father of Simon Peter, and Maren Fellows had been Abigail (record book, 1869, MS 31876). In England that summer he received William P. Thomas at Derby in what he called “a Holy place,” and George Thompson asked Thomas whether he had “past through some order of endowment,” and whether it was “anything like what you had in the Land of Salt. Was your Name the same” (George Thompson to William P. Thomas, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from George Thompson to William P. Thomas, 1869–1874, July 29, 1869). Thompson remembered too that “Joseph Morris taught at Weber that the Devil took this earth by Guile and he said by Guile we shall” take it back, and judged that Morris “did not believe the Mormons could kill him” (August 23, 1869).

In 1881, writing for Williams, Thompson gave another saying: “Brother Joseph often said ‘Stick no stake but what you can easily pull up again,’” and used it to argue that all revelation so far was “Proxy or foreshadowing, nothing really substantial or unalterable,” and that “as ages roll on, Revelation after Revelation will bear remodelling” (George Thompson to Hendrikson, August 1881, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1878–1881). Williams taught that almost all would be saved: those who could not abide the fulness would “fall to their own level and be saved with Catholics or Methodists or Mormons or Pagans or Mahomet or any other profession in the many mansions set apart in our Father’s House, for behold all will be saved except the fallen spirits or Sons of Perdition” (epistle of April 6, 1874).

Spirits that had been “careless, not valiant in good works” in the first estate were “chastised with punishment in mortality,” led by angels to “the body in the womb prepared for them” and to “what they merited of labour, of pain, of degradation,” so that the “unsound material” they had absorbed might be purified; in mortality “another race shall commence,” and “the last may be first and the first last” (Williams to Eardley and Moss, July 16, 1874; both Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1874–1875). A single typescript fragment says that the Prophet read men’s priesthood by feeling their heads, and that no descendant of Cain would be let into the church; it is the only source for this (see Did the Prophet Cainan read birthrights by phrenology?). In May 1874 Williams wrote that Theodora had “passed thro her order of Birthright,” while Margaret Thomas in Montana had not yet “passed the order and had her own Celestial name given by the Servant of the Lord”: celestial names were given in an order of birthright, to women as well as men.

In April 1866 he told Eardley that an account written for the sisters the previous October, on “their position in the Priesthood — their throne of authority — their equal privileges — their voice in their own assemblies,” was still held back, for “a positive word cometh, ‘not yet’” (Williams to Eardley, April 16, 1866, copied in the letter copy book, circa 1866–1877, MS 32226, Church History Library). In August 1866 he wrote to Eardley that “the female Priesthood likewise is in advance of this present time” (Williams to Eardley, August 4, 1866, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to William James and John R. Eardley, 1864–1867).

In 1870 he called Sister Mortensen, who led the sisters’ meetings at Omaha, “High Priestess,” and told her, if she could not attend, to “call upon another good Sister and lay her hands upon her and appoint her by voice in prayer to officiate as High Priestess in her absence,” and to “stand forth and deliver to the Sisterhood such things as the holy Spirit shall present unto her” in the sisters’ meeting (Williams to the Omaha church, March and May 1870, and to Hendrikson, July 10, 1870, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Andrew Henrikson, 1870). In February 1875 he asked the Race Track stake whether it had “appointed a Sister to preside over your charge,” and if not to send him “the Sisters’ names whether single or not & I will appoint” (Williams to Hendrikson, February 5, 1875, Church History Library, MS 31876; for Theodora and Margaret Thomas, Williams to Margaret Thomas, May 6, 1874, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Margaret and William P. Thomas, 1866–1882).

The Sides of the North

Williams was calling Montana “the Sides of the North” by April 1874, when a revelation in his epistle of that month divided “my people on the Sides of the North, even in Montana” into two stakes (Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1874–1875). In January 1877 he wrote to William James of his position as “a high Priest and authorized Minister of our merciful God on the Sides of the North” (Williams to James, January 22, 1877, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to William James and Montana followers, 1876–1878). In a letter of January 6, 1879, Williams told the Montana Saints that the region where they lived was “the Sides of the North” (Psalm 48:2), where “the City of the great King” would come down, after wise men had first come to make ready for the Lord’s coming: “you are the pioneers.” He described a land made new, with a milder climate, no beasts of prey and a pure language, but he set no date (Andrew Hendrickson’s 1883 copy, letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1878–1881, MS 31876).

The same letter calls the Lord’s House, then being built, “my first House of Prayer on the Sides of the North,” and tells the Saints to keep his letters, which would one day be gathered and bound “as our old Bible was gathered up.” An undated letter of about 1878 sets the Sides of the North in place of Jackson County, which “once was fair & young & beautiful, wedded to our dear Lord,” but “left the paths of virtue”: “Old Israel want it at Jerusalem, the Latter day want it in Jackson County, the Mahomet want it at Mecca … All these and some others will be stakes under the Government located on the Sides of the North” (Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to William James and Montana followers, 1876–1878).

Seth’s Diagram

A record book of Williams’s letters kept by the Scandinavian members opens with a drawing in red and blue ink labeled “Seth’s Diagram.” A sun with a star beside it is ringed by circles of dots; below, stations are labeled, in Swedish, Adam’s world, Enoch’s Zion, the spirit world, paradise, hell, outer darkness, the nine and a half lost tribes of Israel and “144,000 celestial worlds”; and a line descends from Seth through Enos, Cainan, Mahalaleel, Jared and Enoch to Nachor, the order of birthright in which Morris was Seth and Williams Cainan (record of letters from Cainan, 1873–1879, Church History Library, MS 31876; labels translated from the Swedish by Claude). In August 1870 Williams wrote that Morris “gave the Diagram & revealed the two Priesthoods, entered the treasure house of the Lord and brought forth the keys of the fulness,” and that the powers of darkness, “having nearly overcome the Weber House,” caused him to be slain because “he could not be overcome in any other way”; he “laid down his life more quiet than a Lamb” (Williams to the Omaha church, August 1870, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Andrew Henrikson, 1870).

In 1869 the Omaha council heard S. P. Guhl on the “principles of the Diagram”; in 1891 Eardley was still looking for “the next servant according to birthright, even Mahalaleel” (see the Montana church, below). Williams himself had looked for a successor in 1872: “The longing for my mantle is amusing … I don’t know who may be the Lord’s choice, yet I suspect it may be seen a youth, as I am to feel thro the house for him,” and he asked Hendrikson and Eardley to describe “the form & the features and general turn of mind & action, intelligence and marks of courage of all you know, young or old, that I may pick him out” (Williams to Hendrikson, private, about August 1872, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Andrew Henrikson, 1872–1873). New members were baptized “to the lesser priesthood,” and their genealogy sent on to him: “If you baptize any, let it be to the lesser priesthood, & send on their genealogy & keep your records correct & reliable” (Williams to James, October 18, 1876, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1876–1877).

The same letter traces the opposition to the early leaders who had not been given “chief authority”: it “began early with such men as Taylor, Dow, Parson[s], mighty men indeed, but overlooked,” who had resented it “after seeing & hearing Seth and being almost Apostles.” When Brigham Young died, Williams wrote that the Latter-day Saint leaders would try “separately to clutch the mantle of authority, which is not for either of them, they failing in Birthright,” and would quarrel, “some for John, some for Orson, some for Wilford, some for Snow, some for young Joseph,” until “one be sent unto them with Keys of power”; Young, he wrote, had refused to receive “the Lord’s Servant, who was commanded to visit him” (Williams to James, Hendrikson and the Montana Saints, October 12, 1877, same folder).

Names, steps and Adam

In August 1881 George Thompson, writing for the dying Williams, explained that names held by birthright were “only temporary”: “the 144,000 High priests stand on a ladder with 144,000 rounds or steps,” the name going “with the step & not with the person.” When one moves up, all move up: “thus Seth stands on the step at the top & Jesus on the one below. When Seth move up, he leaves the name Seth & take that of Adam, Enos takes the name Seth,” and so on down, until Jesus “leaves his step & name for Abel.” Williams had written in 1878 that when Christ has lifted the earth “into celestial glory … not till then will he hand up the Kingdom of the Planet to the Father of the family, even Adam” (Thompson to the Montana brethren, August 21, 1881, and Williams to Hendrikson, August 28, 1878, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1878–1881).

Answering Brother Steffanson, who had found an “inconsistency” in his teaching on the Fall, Williams wrote in September 1880 that “eight estates are opened up”; those who pass through them become “equal in holiness, knowledge & felicity to God their eternal father, but not equal in power unless a firstborn son or daughter.” Adam and Eve had passed through eight estates, and “in one life Adam was a Saviour and saved a planet under him.” Adam’s body “never saw corruption but is laid by in chambers of the departed,” his spirit “waiting his next eternal life” (Williams to Hendrikson and the Saints in Deer Lodge, September 19, 1880, Montana copy in the record of letters from Cainan, 1873–1879, Church History Library, MS 31876). Already in 1871 he had described a government “constructed with Adam at its head, to govern this planet when arriving in the Celestial Quorum or 4th Estate. Then Adam fills the position now occupied by Jehovah, and Abel will be the next Jesus Christ” (Williams to Robert Ford, Loughborough, February 7, 1871, letter copy book, circa 1866–1877, Church of the Firstborn (Morrisites) records, 1869–1890, MS 32226, Church History Library).

Context: the Adam–God teaching

These passages place Adam above Christ as “the Father of the family,” to whom the kingdom is returned. They resemble the teaching Brigham Young first preached in Salt Lake City in April 1852, that Adam “is our Father and our God, and the only God with whom we have to do” (Journal of Discourses 1:50), which was taught in Utah, though never uniformly accepted, while Morris, Williams and most of their followers were Latter-day Saints in the 1850s; Williams lived in Salt Lake Valley until 1869. The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints later rejected the doctrine, most explicitly under President Spencer W. Kimball in 1976. The Morrisite version differs from Young’s. In Thompson’s account “Adam” is a rank on a ladder of names, taken in turn by Seth, Enos and those below them, not the single person Young identified as God the Father; and Williams’s Christ hands the kingdom up to Adam, as Joseph Smith had taught that Adam, the Ancient of Days, would preside over his posterity (Daniel 7; Doctrine and Covenants 107:53–56).

Whether the Morrisite teaching came from Young’s, from Smith’s, or from Morris himself is not known; Part of it goes back to Morris: his catechism on the first chapter of Genesis teaches that the earth was organized by “Adam’s own Father” for Adam his firstborn, and that Abel was Adam’s firstborn son and Cain the second-born, “an angel of death” who claimed the right to lead in the second estate (The Spirit Prevails, “Concerning the Creation,” p. 660; manuscript copies in the Joseph Morris Papers, P11-1 f3, Community of Christ Library Archives, and the journal of Josiah D. Price, P26-22 f1). Williams’s “Abel will be the next Jesus Christ” builds on that. Whether Morris placed Adam above Christ has not been found in what has been read for this guide.

Judgment, prophecy and the Masons

Williams kept Morris’s prophecies in mind. In the winter of 1871, reading in the English papers of Brigham Young’s indictment, he reminded the Thomases: “Seth told you at Weber he would be broken up and take to flight but the Law would find him.” In November 1871 he wrote to Hendrikson that he had “generally abstained from mentioning Brighams atrocities knowing well he had a fearful reckoning to answer for causing to be slain the Almighties Choice Servant Seth,” who had told them that Young’s reign “should end disastrously,” after which the high priesthood with “Abels Priesthood” would “pass thro that Dominion declaring the fulness of the Gospel” (Williams to Andrew Hendrikson and the Saints in Council Bluffs, Omaha and region, Loughborough, November 1871, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Andrew Henrikson, 1871). In 1873 he wrote that if the Latter-day Saints would now receive “the message of our merciful God sent unto them by the seventh Angel whom they wickedly slew,” they might find mercy, and if not, “confusion & war among themselves will open a gate to the Gentiles.” He would not have dates set for the gathering (see 1874–75 in the chronology).

In 1876 he wrote that “the Masons will injure no one and their knowledge is good & their brotherly conduct is highly esteemed,” but that they were “bound to secrecy & oaths.” In 1880 he foresaw his writings gathered after his death: “there will be hunters come in days when I am gone home to my Father to gather up in every place the written word, as was done before; the result is our Bible” (Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Margaret and William P. Thomas, 1866–1882).

Polygamy, the household and practice

Asked by Robert Ford about polygamy, a question he called “personal,” Williams wrote on February 7, 1871: “I never taught the doctrine or counselled the practice.” In the same answer he described Susanna, “a priceless companion to me,” “a loving, discreet counsellor to Louisa,” his wife, and “a tender mother to my little ones,” who had no children of her own; the letter does not say more of her place in the household (see What did Morris teach about marriage?). Of the eternities he wrote otherwise. Three weeks later he taught that the faithful high priest is rewarded with “a Daughter of Adam to be given after her second birth thro celestial Parents,” and that “before an high priest becomes a God like Jehovah he must perform 144 000 missions on Mortal Earths, taking one Daughter of each God to make him equal to every God and in relationship with them all, and eligible to commence a new Creation” (Williams to the Saints of the Most High at Council Bluffs and Omaha, February 28, 1871, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Andrew Henrikson, 1871).

Williams married Susannah Woolfitt at Rotherham Minster on September 8, 1846 (Rotherham parish register, Sheffield Archives PR-87-3-19, p. 67, indexed at FamilySearch, ark:/61903/1:1:66G5-MC3V). Louisa King, whom FamilySearch Family Tree records as marrying him at Salt Lake City on March 8, 1857 (KWJ8-P3L; no record of that marriage has been found), lived in his household with Susannah: the 1860 census lists George, Susannah and Louisa Williams together in Salt Lake with Louisa’s first children (1860 U.S. census, ark:/61903/1:1:MH2W-GZF), and the 1880 census found Susannah, 65, and Louisa, 51, in one household in the 8th Ward with five of Louisa’s daughters (1880 U.S. census, Salt Lake City, 8th Ward, ark:/61903/1:1:MNSV-RYF). In July 1874 Williams wrote that Louisa, who no longer wrote to him, had told Margaret Thomas: “your Husband had another wife & two children, he is a false Prophet, he has large sums of money in the bank & gives you none.” He answered that these were “not her own thoughts but the suggestions & inventions of him she has been listening to,” “exactly the story of a seducer of another man’s wife” (Williams to Margaret Thomas, July 27, 1874, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Margaret and William P. Thomas, 1866–1882).

To William James in September 1874 Williams called the “tales” that came “torn from my own family” “false & forged by Mormons at Salt Lake … I will not repeat them here, they are too contemptible” (Williams to William James, September 4, 1874, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1874–1875). No other source yet found bears on the charge itself. The same letter says that a temple and gathering “in Idaho” had been permitted, “but the wicked combined with evil Powers & suffered it not, as yet”; looks forward to “another ordinance,” “even the washing of feet”; and gives counsel on eating and drinking: “Take no distilled spirit while in health. Wine, if it is pure, in limited moderation. Sparingly of fat meat … Good cold water is better than coffee or tea.” Seth, Enos and Cainan, he wrote, had together on “a Holy Mountain” considered “a whole planet” and its plan, “beginning with the Weber House,” his name for Morris’s church (Williams to Robert Ford, Loughborough, February 7, 1871, letter copy book, circa 1866–1877, Church of the Firstborn (Morrisites) records, 1869–1890, MS 32226, Church History Library).

The great and the common salvation

Williams distinguished the “common salvation,” by which people of faith, hope and works fill the mansions of the Father’s house, from “the great Salvation,” which needs knowledge: “knowledge is the passport to Eternal honors,” and the faithful “are the aristocracy thereof; Mormons, Catholic, Protestant, Jews & all professing worshipers come in behind” (Williams to James, August 16, 1877, and to Hendrikson, January 11, 1877, Church History Library, MS 31876). The distinction goes back at least to June 1869, when he wrote from Ashwicken that “every heir of Salvation will be saved in one of the many mansions,” but “the great salvation … is reserved for the Saints of the Most High”; that October he wrote that he had shown the Saints in England “the eternal worlds as a map” and “the Mansions of Paradise, showing them plain the locations there in which they are entitled” (Williams to the Omaha church, June 1 and October 13, 1869, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Andrew Henrikson, 1869).

The living could raise or lower their dead: by faithfulness the Saints would become “rulers, Priests and Kings to God” and “in this position could help their Dead to shine as stars in the resurrection,” but by unfaithfulness “them that do such folly and their Dead also loose all honors and must be ruled over by others thro all Eternity” (Williams to Hendrikson, July 10, 1870, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Andrew Henrikson, 1870).

In February 1871 he wrote that the inhabitants of the mansions of Paradise could receive the gospel only in part, “some from the Pope, some from Luther, some from Swedenborg, some from Wesley, some from Mahomet, some from Brigham, from Zoroaster, from Confucius, from Budd[h]a and from Calvin & young Joseph,” because “the material of their Spirit is inferior from its beginning” and “must be thrashed in the third Estate before entering on the Resurrection or fourth Estate.” In the same letter he described “other high Priests who come from the Heavens above our Eternal Father who are not after the order of Seth nor Melchisedec”: they “bring the heavenly sciences,” architecture, the compass, astronomy, the “Electric wire to speak across continents,” steam and music, and lead “in the Government of the Nations, Washington, Lincoln, Newton and the like master spirits,” with “Females also of this order” who bring “virtuous holy lovely principles” (Williams to the Saints of the Most High at Council Bluffs and Omaha, February 28, 1871, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Andrew Henrikson, 1871).

He wrote in October 1876 that the common salvation “taken in negroes, Indians and every vile spirit that transgressed the righteous laws in their first estate. There they were filthy; no bleaching here will whiten them,” and that “our merciful God gave keys of detection, that if possible they may not press into our borders” (Williams to James, October 18, 1876, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1876–1877). See Did the Prophet Cainan read birthrights by phrenology? Of the times, he wrote: “It is not for us to set the times for the accomplishment of Jehovah’s purposes, but as faithful servants wait at his call” (Williams to James, April 2, 1877, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to William James and Montana followers, 1876–1878).

Hymns

About 1871 Williams asked that Margaret Thomas “attend her high & holy office with the Sisters” in Montana and “also compose songs of rejoicing to be sung in the assembly,” and that John Bowen, if he came, be invited “to compose some suitable Hymns” (Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to William James and Montana followers, 1870–1873).

Feast days

In the same letter of February 1874 Williams asked the Montana Saints to set apart “a day of rejoicing,” coming together “in best raiment with suitable provisions,” when “all be free to speak, to discuss (not in opposition), to sing, to dance”: “the first legal thanksgiving day in this last dispensation” (Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1874–1875). Later the church kept February 14, Williams’s birthday, which he asked for in 1877, and August 9, the anniversary of the Lord’s House (see the Glossary). The August feast was William James’s proposal: in September 1880 Williams wrote that “Brother James did well in proposing & carry out a feast, a day of rejoicing on the anniversary opening of our little Temple,” and that it “shall be a perpetual day, a holy day in our generation in all places where we inhabit, like the 14 day of Feb.” (Williams to Hendrikson and the Saints in Deer Lodge, September 19, 1880, Montana copy in the record of letters from Cainan, 1873–1879, Church History Library, MS 31876).

Dancing among strangers was another matter: in July 1881 Rasmus Rasmussen told the Race Track council that “Cainan had written that it was unsuitable for the Saints of the Most High to be at dance places” (minutes, July 31, 1881, Church History Library, MS 31876, Minutes, 1881; translation from the Danish generated by Claude).

The robe, the court and the Lord’s House

Williams first prescribed a robe for “President James” in February 1874: “a linen robe large and broad, reaching down to the feet, opening from top to bottom in front,” to be worn “until by continued faithfulness a crown is handed.” He wrote that Seth, that is, Morris, had wanted every minister so “clothed” (Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1874–1875; see Where did the rituals come from?). In May 1875 he regretted that “the instructions on the robe were so lightly esteemed”: many were privileged to wear “a certain robe in imitation of Celestial order,” and the doors to higher understanding were “guarded by guards that require a sign of worthiness” (Williams to Margaret Thomas, May 10, 1875, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Margaret and William P. Thomas, 1866–1882).

In April 1877 he sent James instructions for a fuller robe of white linen, “full & flowing,” reaching to the feet, with sleeves “in shape of what is commonly called Chinese sleeve but of course larger,” and a red silk band and white silk for “the figure of the Sun worked in needle work,” to be worn “in semblance to the Celestial attiring of the Celestial Priesthood, a link of that chain reaching up to the first God of all.” The same letter set up “the Court of the Lord’s House,” three officers who were to meet every May 10 (from August 1877 also every November 10) “that a portion of the weight of this infant Kingdom may not rest upon one,” and gave its opening prayer (Williams to James, April 2, 1877, and to Hendrikson, August 8, 1877, Church History Library, MS 31876). In October 1877 he asked for “a building” that, “when finished, will serve to carry out Temple ordinances,” and in September 1878 he sent the words of dedication, to be spoken by James walking round the building and standing on the southeast corner, “from whence cometh the greatest light” (Williams to James, Hendrikson and the Montana Saints, October 12, 1877, and to James, September 1878, Church History Library, MS 31876). James wore the robe to administer the sacrament in the 1880s (see the Montana church, below).

Williams’s later letters add other rites. A high priest was to take a newborn child in his arms and say, “by virtue of the high & holy Priesthood given to me I bless this child in the name of Jesus”; the sacrament, of bread and water or wine, was a “Spiritual Strength” given only when “administered legally by the High Priesthood,” and the priesthood was to call on Christ to make good his word “at the very time of changing whatsoever he called his Body & Blood into what we were about to partake of”; and, answering the council’s meeting of November 10, 1881, Williams ruled “that none partake of it outside the Church in fair standing,” admitting children from eight years old.

In the same answer he taught that the elements were Christ’s “real body and blood,” by “an eternal and heavenly principle beyond the conception of men in mortality, and the means by which a portion of eternal life is conveyed to us here”; how it was done was “no business of theirs or ours,” and it might be taken “even every week if it could be” (Williams to Hendrikson, October 4, 1879, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1878–1881; “The Lord’s Supper,” signed “Geo Williams & Cainan,” in the same folder, marked “Copied by J.R.E.,” and a copy in Writings of Cainan, circa 1867–1872, MS 31876). The Montana council later opened it to friends who were not members (see below).

Worship and order in Montana, 1880–81

Williams’s elders baptized new converts at Omaha, at Council Bluffs and Crescent City, Iowa, and at Fremont, Nebraska, from January 1869, and later at the Race Track in Montana, in 1880 and 1882 (“Record of members, 1869–1872” (Omaha), “Record of members, circa 1869–1871” (Council Bluffs), and the Montana record of members, 1874–1887, MS 32226, Church History Library). The Race Track council’s minutes show how Williams’s teaching was lived in the Lord’s House. At a joint meeting on May 10, 1880, “Cainan’s Epistle” was read and the sacrament “administered by Bro James who was clothed in his Robe.” James then gave a date: “Orson Pratt made it out that 61 years after the organisation of Church of J Smith will be the coming of Christ. it was set out in the rocks of the Pyramides of Egypt. if we have but 11 years more then it will be a short work to be done,” that is, in 1891 (minutes, May 10, 1880, Church History Library, MS 31876, Minutes, 1880). The gifts of the spirit continued, under order.

In March 1881 the council examined a member who said he received the influence of “spirits,” and it recalled Cainan’s answer that the Evil One could give an influence that seemed good but was false. President Andrew Hendrikson ruled that anyone who would speak in tongues in a public meeting was led by the wrong influence, since God’s spirit would never lead anyone to overstep the order of the church (minutes, March 26, 1881, MS 31876, Minutes, 1881; translation from the Danish generated by Claude). The Montana church (1882–1954)

Continuity with Weber

Writing for the dying Williams in March 1882, George Thompson told the Montana church that “those who have been baptized by Joseph or under his ministry at Weber are members of the fullness under Cainan without being rebaptized,” while others must be baptized “into the fullness” before they could become members, and that Cainan’s church was “a continuation of the Church formed by Joseph at Weber” (Thompson to the Montana church, March 23, 1882, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to William James and Montana followers, 1879–1882).

How an RLDS missionary judged them, 1885–86

In November 1885 the Montana leaders published a notice, reprinted in the Saints’ Herald, against the Doves, who claimed to speak for the Morrisites: the signers, printed as “William M. Jones” (very probably William M. James), Andrew Hendrickson and Jno. R. Eardley, spoke for the Morrisites “organized in this county and Territory for many years past under the name of the ‘Church of the Saints of the Most High’” and wrote, “We utterly repudiate their claims, teachings and practices” (Saints’ Herald 32, December 19, 1885, p. 821). J. C. Clapp, who had preached among them, wrote in January 1886 that they “believe a great many things which to me are strange” and, he thought, “erroneous,” but were “sound on most of the essential doctrines of the Latter Day Work”; “the great and most damaging difference is in respect to the right of presidency” (Clapp, Stuart, Montana, January 18, 1886, Saints’ Herald 33, January 30, 1886, p. 86).

From prophet to vote

Before he died in 1882 Williams wrote that the organization should stay as it was for twelve months and that William James should then be put before the whole church and, if sustained “by uplifted hand,” remain from year to year. After him the church chose its presidents at general assemblies (1883, 1886); Andrew Hendrikson presided from 1886 to 1921 and George Johnson until shortly before his death in 1954, with which, Anderson writes, the church came to an end; USU’s finding aid gives 1969 for its formal disbanding (see How did a prophet’s movement become a church run by vote?).

The Son and the Father

In October 1897 Andrew Hendrickson drafted an answer to a reader’s question in the Christian Herald of New York about “who our dear Lord are, and his true position.” From the Gospel of John and 1 Corinthians 15 he argued that the Son is subject to the Father, that “the greater always send the lesser,” and that there is “no room for equality” (Hendrikson to the Christian Herald, Race Track, October 20, 1897, draft, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to Andrew Hendrickson, 1893–1901).

Worship and neighbors

The Lord’s House near Racetrack, built in 1878–79 and dedicated on August 9, 1879, held Sunday meetings. The council kept the sacrament open to non-members who came as “friends for the gospel’s sake,” and in 1886 it accepted an invitation to use the Reorganized Church’s meeting house in Deer Lodge City (see How did Latter-day Saints, the Reorganized Church and the Morrisites live alongside one another?).

What the later church kept

Gems of Inspiration, compiled by John R. Eardley and printed by Joseph A. Dove in 1899, is the movement’s own selection a generation after Morris. About a third of it is a history of the Morrisites, written against the Mormon church; the rest is Morris’s articles, among them “Keys of the Priesthood” and “The Order of Sevens,” and Williams’s visions, letters and records, including two letters to the Saints in Montana. It keeps the order of gods, angels and birthrights and the line from Morris to Williams, condemns polygamy, and does not use the word reincarnation. What was taught in the church’s last decades is not yet known.

Doctrine and disappointment, 1887–91

The church’s court, which Hendrikson said existed “to argue principles, doctrines or anything else, and as Cainan is not with us there is no other place to appeal,” set out “the doctrines of Cainan” in May 1887: a high priest’s “first birthright in the spiritual state” placed him in his father’s priesthood, and his birthrights in mortality gave him experience of everything, “so that when he becomes a God he then can judge and rule in righteousness from his own experience,” while some of the heirs of salvation “merely pass thro a mortal womb so as to go across over to Paradise,” according to their behavior “in their spiritual estate” (High Court minutes, May 10, 1887, Church History Library, MS 31876, Minutes, 1886–1887). In November 1874 George Thompson had looked further off, to “about the year 1902,” “the day when Joseph will return & be introduced to us by his 1st & 2nd Councilors … in fulfillment of what was foreshadowed at Weber”; until then “our Weber family” were consigned to darkness “to atone for their misstep in giving up the Prophet to the Mormons” (George Thompson to William P. Thomas, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from George Thompson to William P. Thomas, 1869–1874, November 2, 1874).

In 1880 William James had looked for the coming of Christ in 1891. On November 10, 1891, Hendrikson told the court that they had been “led for many years past to expect that in the present year the ‘winding up scene’ would take place,” but that he still believed “he should live to behold the coming of the Son of Man, as he had been promised by the Lord’s servant Cainan.” Eardley looked instead to a coming “war of the nations,” which Williams had once foretold to him, and to “the coming forth of the next servant according to birthright, even Mahalaleel”; he likened the Saints to the wise and foolish virgins, “drowsy and sleepy,” though some “have retained their faith.” He also urged prayer standing with hands raised, as Williams prayed, “with body erect, and hands uplifted to heaven,” and called kneeling “the Sectarian method” (court minutes, November 10, 1891, Church History Library, MS 31876, Minutes, 1891). Eardley had already conceded a point a year earlier.

The church, he wrote in March 1890, was shrinking toward Daniel’s stone “cut out of the mountain without hands,” and “Bro Cainan represented himself as that stone, he was mistaken as all other prophets have been in some things” (Eardley to Hendrikson, March 20, 1890, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to Andrew Hendrickson, 1890). The hope outlived 1891. In June 1892 George Thompson asked how the churches would receive “our Joseph with his two Councelers — Enoc and Cainan —” if they appeared to gather “the scattered sheep of Weber,” purge the church in Utah and make headquarters in Jackson County (the spelling “Enoc” is Thompson’s). After Parson’s death that month Eardley concluded that “Joseph’s mission was to reveal the diagram, and Foreshadow the coming kingdom. All other things which transpired were of a secondary consideration” (Thompson to Eardley, June 26, 1892; Eardley to James Dove, June 18, 1892, copy; Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to Andrew Hendrickson, 1892).

The doctrine of birthrights reached print in 1891, in one of the Salt Lake Tribune letters that Eardley said were Parson’s: since the priesthood came by lineage from Adam through Seth, “no man in the church knows whether he is in the right place or not,” and those whose birthrights did not match their offices would be put in their right places “when a prophet comes having the Urim and Thummim by which their birthright can be shown” (“The Priesthood Mistake,” letter of “The Elder,” Salt Lake Tribune, June 21, 1891, p. 3).
