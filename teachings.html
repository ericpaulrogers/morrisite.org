Teachings

What the Morrisites taught

A summary of the movement’s teachings, period by period, with the sources for each. It describes what the Morrisites and their successors believed; it does not argue for or against it. Where scholars disagree, both readings are given.

How we know

The sources are uneven. Joseph Morris’s revelations were published only after his death, by a follower, as The Spirit Prevails (1886). His letters to Brigham Young survive in Young’s office files, and Kirk Watson’s The Morrisites: Writings and Revelations (2026) prints them with other manuscripts of Morris, George Williams, William W. Davies and the Doves, many for the first time. Gems of Inspiration (1899) shows what the later church chose to keep. Mark H. Forscutt, an insider who later left, wrote a sketch of Morris; the newspapers of 1862 were hostile; and C. LeRoy Anderson’s history is the only book on the movement. For the later church there are Williams’s letters and the Montana minutes at the Church History Library (MS 31876 and MS 32226), and for Davies’s community the sworn testimony of 1880–81 (see Repositories). As Watson notes, almost everything written on the Morrisites has been about the siege, and almost nothing about their beliefs (Watson, p. 6). Teachings also changed over a century, so this page takes them period by period. Translations from the Danish are generated by Claude.

Joseph Morris (1857–1862)

A prophet with a message for Brigham Young. Morris, an English convert, believed the Lord spoke through him, and he recorded more than 300 revelations. His letters to Young, from 1858 to 1860, show him first offering to work with Young and then, as no answer came, growing desperate; he believed he had been called to lead the church (Watson, pp. 5, 11–49). A revelation of January 1860 charges Young with turning the Lord’s servant away from his door; a clerk docketed it “Balderdash” (see Documents and Did Brigham Young ever answer Morris?). His followers later claimed that church leaders drew on the revelations he had sent and presented them as “new truths” (Watson, p. 11, citing Dove’s preface).

Moses, and Seth, the seventh angel. Morris claimed to be the reincarnation of Moses (Watson, p. 6). He also taught an order of seven angels who preside over the last dispensations of each earth: Seth, Enos, Cainan, Mahalaleel, Jared, Enoch and Methuselah, with Seth, the seventh, presiding (“The Order of Sevens,” in Gems of Inspiration). Morris called himself the Seventh Angel, and George Williams’s writings call the prophet of South Weber “Seth” (Watson, p. 90). Both identities appear in the sources.

Gods, angels and Holy Ghosts. In a revelation on the Holy Ghost, an early form of “Keys of the Priesthood” (The Spirit Prevails, No. 2), Morris taught that Holy Ghosts are men who once lived on a mortal earth, “millions” of them, made one as the Father and the Son are one, and that each god is subject to the one above him, up to “the first God of all” (Watson, pp. 50–53). Forscutt later remembered the theology as monotheistic at its root but wide enough to find a place for the faithful of every religion (Forscutt, Sketch, pp. 17–18).

Reincarnation. Morris taught that spirits return to the earth in new bodies; Joseph Dove dated the teaching to a revelation of 1859, and Forscutt called it Morris’s “strange theory of repeated embodiment.” Where it came from is disputed: see Where did the Morrisite teaching of reincarnation come from? and the Glossary.

The coming of Christ, and consecration. Morris taught that Christ would come soon to deliver his people, and he tied the coming to the consecration of their property. On December 28, 1861, a revelation promised that the Lord would not delay once the people were prepared; on December 30 another promised that he would come “to-morrow morning” if those present consecrated all their property in the way appointed (The Spirit Prevails, Nos. 186 and 188–189, pp. 336–39). When the morning passed, a revelation of January 6, 1862, blamed those who had consecrated unwillingly, and one of January 11 refused to set another time (Nos. 195 and 199, pp. 350, 359); in February deliverance was promised after the people had taken an oath (No. 224, p. 419). Revelations of January and April 1862 forbade using the property of “the rebellious” and forbade letting those who left take back what they had consecrated (Nos. 202 and 254, pp. 368, 490), the dispute that led to the writs and the siege. John Parson, speaking at South Weber in September 1861, set consecration against Brigham Young’s church, whose apostles, he said, had promised that land would not be monopolized and that all would have equal rights, and had not kept the promise (Watson, pp. 64–67). See How did believers survive failed prophecy?

Judgment. The revelations of 1861–62 also foretell judgment on the wicked. A revelation read at South Weber on September 29, 1861, says Christ will come “as a thief in the night” and as a refiner, and will destroy his enemies, among them the leaders of his former church (Watson, pp. 60–64; The Spirit Prevails, No. 104). In a tongue of June 8, 1862, the “3rd Captain of the Lord’s Host,” an angel from an earlier earth, told of a camp like Kington Fort whose faithful, at “the test,” killed the hypocrites among them, and whose prophet then enforced the law of God on pain of death (The Spirit Prevails, pp. 638–42; Watson, pp. 72–79). Watson reads such passages as showing that Morris expected human as well as divine agents of judgment, including against dissenters in his own community (Watson, p. 8); Anderson held that the judgment Morris foretold was God’s alone (Anderson, Revisited, pp. 108, 111).

Spiritual gifts. Speaking in tongues and their interpretation, and messages from angels, were part of worship at Kington Fort. An interpretation of June 7, 1862, taught that the gifts are given to mortals and cease only at the resurrection, when the faithful become angels who give them to others, and that they had left the older church because a council in Brigham Young’s house decided to put them down (Watson, pp. 70–72). See What became of the spiritual gifts?

Church order and ritual. Morris organized his church on April 6, 1861, at South Weber, with a First Council and apostles (Glossary). Scepters, a robe and crown, and a ceremony at Kington Fort in May 1862 belong to its ritual life; what Morris taught they meant is little documented (Anderson, Revisited, pp. 116–17; see Where did the rituals come from?).

Marriage. Later writers often say that Morris opposed plural marriage; Watson argues that he held it to be part of the heavenly order (Watson, pp. 7–8). Morris’s own article of June 4, 1861, explaining Joseph Smith’s revelation on celestial marriage, says something between the two: the biblical law of many wives and concubines was given for this life, “not being a celestial law,” and the kings and prophets who had many wives “can have but one sealed to them each for eternity” (The Spirit Prevails, pp. 651–52). The same article defines a concubine as a fallen spirit “of the seed of Cain.” In the Montana church in 1876 the term was used of members: the council heard that an excluded member had been called “a fallen spirit,” and a man who left the church that December complained that members called his wife one (council minutes, April 1 and December 3, 1876, Church History Library, MS 31876, Minutes, 1876; translation from the Danish generated by Claude). The 3rd Captain’s testimony calls polygamy true “in its proper time and place.” It was not practiced at Kington Fort (Watson, p. 8). By 1899 the movement’s own history condemned polygamy outright (Gems of Inspiration), which may be where the later tradition began. See What did Morris teach about marriage?

George Williams, the Prophet Cainan (1863–1882)

His calling. Williams, the Prophet Cainan, told of visions in 1862. On April 15, near a canyon in Salt Lake Valley, he saw the armies of heaven and the Father and the Son; from August 25 to 27, on a mountain east of the valley, John the Revelator baptized him, and Seth and Enos, who had “sealed our testimony with our blood,” declared him “Cainan, then Melchisedec, now George Williams” and gave him the keys of the priesthood, among them the keys of birthright and of sealing (“Interviews with Celestial Beings,” Watson, pp. 81–90; Gems of Inspiration). Seth is Morris. Enos was long taken in this guide to be John Banks, killed with him, but Williams himself wrote in December 1881 that “Joseph Morris said Joseph Smith was Enos, but meant he represented Enos; he also said he was Moses, but meant to say he represented Moses,” and that he, Williams, had used the same “mode of expression” in calling himself Cainan; omitting the word “represent,” he wrote, “may have caused wrong impression” (Williams to Andrew Hendrikson, December 29, 1881, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1878–1881). The line of servants in his letters runs from Enos to Seth to Cainan, that is, from Joseph Smith to Morris to Williams; Smith and Morris had both “sealed” their testimony with their blood. Williams also claimed the use of the Urim and Thummim and said he had met Muhammad, Joseph Smith and Morris (Watson, pp. 80, 92–96). From 1869 he led from England, by letter (see Chronology).

New records. Williams said he had translated ancient records from St. Ann’s Hill in Surrey; the chapters that survive describe the origin of the gods, their dwellings, their incarnations and reincarnations, and the making of worlds (Watson, pp. 96–110). In 1876 he told the Montana Saints that it was “not wisdom at this time” to give them the records “until there is union of feeling,” for “it is the highest sacrilege to debate or dispute on sacred things”: “the Book of Mormon nearly overthrew the work of the first Joseph” (Williams to the Saints in Montana, April 23, 1876, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1876–1877). He also explained Enoch’s Zion: at Enoch’s request it was “broken off from this Planet & set between this & Paradise,” where it still revolves, and it “will be reunited to this” (Williams to Andrew Hendrikson, 1877, same folder).

Earlier lives and birthrights. At Council Bluffs and Omaha in 1869 Williams told converts who they had been in earlier lives: Carlton Fellows had been Jonas, the father of Simon Peter, and Maren Fellows had been Abigail (record book, 1869, MS 31876). A single typescript fragment says that the Prophet read men’s priesthood by feeling their heads, and that no descendant of Cain would be let into the church; it is the only source for this (see Did the Prophet Cainan read birthrights by phrenology?).

The Sides of the North. Williams was calling Montana “the Sides of the North” by April 1874, when a revelation in his epistle of that month divided “my people on the Sides of the North, even in Montana” into two stakes (Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1874–1875). In January 1877 he wrote to William James of his position as “a high Priest and authorized Minister of our merciful God on the Sides of the North” (Williams to James, January 22, 1877, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to William James and Montana followers, 1876–1878). In a letter of January 6, 1879, Williams told the Montana Saints that the region where they lived was “the Sides of the North” (Psalm 48:2), where “the City of the great King” would come down, after wise men had first come to make ready for the Lord’s coming: “you are the pioneers.” He described a land made new, with a milder climate, no beasts of prey and a pure language, but he set no date (Andrew Hendrickson’s 1883 copy, letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1878–1881, MS 31876). The same letter calls the Lord’s House, then being built, “my first House of Prayer on the Sides of the North,” and tells the Saints to keep his letters, which would one day be gathered and bound “as our old Bible was gathered up.” An undated letter of about 1878 sets the Sides of the North in place of Jackson County, which “once was fair & young & beautiful, wedded to our dear Lord,” but “left the paths of virtue”: “Old Israel want it at Jerusalem, the Latter day want it in Jackson County, the Mahomet want it at Mecca … All these and some others will be stakes under the Government located on the Sides of the North” (Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to William James and Montana followers, 1876–1878).

Seth’s Diagram. A record book of Williams’s letters kept by the Scandinavian members opens with a drawing in red and blue ink labeled “Seth’s Diagram.” A sun with a star beside it is ringed by circles of dots; below, stations are labeled, in Swedish, Adam’s world, Enoch’s Zion, the spirit world, paradise, hell, outer darkness, the nine and a half lost tribes of Israel and “144,000 celestial worlds”; and a line descends from Seth through Enos, Cainan, Mahalaleel, Jared and Enoch to Nachor, the order of birthright in which Morris was Seth and Williams Cainan (record of letters from Cainan, 1873–1879, Church History Library, MS 31876; labels translated from the Swedish by Claude). In 1869 the Omaha council heard S. P. Guhl on the “principles of the Diagram”; in 1891 Eardley was still looking for “the next servant according to birthright, even Mahalaleel” (see the Montana church, below).

Names, steps and Adam. In August 1881 George Thompson, writing for the dying Williams, explained that names held by birthright were “only temporary”: “the 144,000 High priests stand on a ladder with 144,000 rounds or steps,” the name going “with the step & not with the person.” When one moves up, all move up: “thus Seth stands on the step at the top & Jesus on the one below. When Seth move up, he leaves the name Seth & take that of Adam, Enos takes the name Seth,” and so on down, until Jesus “leaves his step & name for Abel.” Williams had written in 1878 that when Christ has lifted the earth “into celestial glory … not till then will he hand up the Kingdom of the Planet to the Father of the family, even Adam” (Thompson to the Montana brethren, August 21, 1881, and Williams to Hendrikson, August 28, 1878, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1878–1881).

Context: the Adam–God teaching. These passages place Adam above Christ as “the Father of the family,” to whom the kingdom is returned. They resemble the teaching Brigham Young first preached in Salt Lake City in April 1852, that Adam “is our Father and our God, and the only God with whom we have to do” (Journal of Discourses 1:50), which was taught in Utah, though never uniformly accepted, while Morris, Williams and most of their followers were Latter-day Saints in the 1850s; Williams lived in Salt Lake Valley until 1869. The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints later rejected the doctrine, most explicitly under President Spencer W. Kimball in 1976. The Morrisite version differs from Young’s. In Thompson’s account “Adam” is a rank on a ladder of names, taken in turn by Seth, Enos and those below them, not the single person Young identified as God the Father; and Williams’s Christ hands the kingdom up to Adam, as Joseph Smith had taught that Adam, the Ancient of Days, would preside over his posterity (Daniel 7; Doctrine and Covenants 107:53–56). Whether the Morrisite teaching came from Young’s, from Smith’s, or from Morris himself is not known; Morris’s own revelations have not yet been searched for it for this guide.

The great and the common salvation. Williams distinguished the “common salvation,” by which people of faith, hope and works fill the mansions of the Father’s house, from “the great Salvation,” which needs knowledge: “knowledge is the passport to Eternal honors,” and the faithful “are the aristocracy thereof; Mormons, Catholic, Protestant, Jews & all professing worshipers come in behind” (Williams to James, August 16, 1877, and to Hendrikson, January 11, 1877, Church History Library, MS 31876). He wrote in October 1876 that the common salvation “taken in negroes, Indians and every vile spirit that transgressed the righteous laws in their first estate. There they were filthy; no bleaching here will whiten them,” and that “our merciful God gave keys of detection, that if possible they may not press into our borders” (Williams to James, October 18, 1876, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1876–1877). See Did the Prophet Cainan read birthrights by phrenology? Of the times, he wrote: “It is not for us to set the times for the accomplishment of Jehovah’s purposes, but as faithful servants wait at his call” (Williams to James, April 2, 1877, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to William James and Montana followers, 1876–1878).

Feast days. In the same letter of February 1874 Williams asked the Montana Saints to set apart “a day of rejoicing,” coming together “in best raiment with suitable provisions,” when “all be free to speak, to discuss (not in opposition), to sing, to dance”: “the first legal thanksgiving day in this last dispensation” (Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1874–1875). Later the church kept February 14, Williams’s birthday, which he asked for in 1877, and August 9, the anniversary of the Lord’s House (see the Glossary).

The robe, the court and the Lord’s House. Williams first prescribed a robe for “President James” in February 1874: “a linen robe large and broad, reaching down to the feet, opening from top to bottom in front,” to be worn “until by continued faithfulness a crown is handed.” He wrote that Seth, that is, Morris, had wanted every minister so “clothed” (Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1874–1875; see Where did the rituals come from?). In April 1877 he sent James instructions for a fuller robe of white linen, “full & flowing,” reaching to the feet, with sleeves “in shape of what is commonly called Chinese sleeve but of course larger,” and a red silk band and white silk for “the figure of the Sun worked in needle work,” to be worn “in semblance to the Celestial attiring of the Celestial Priesthood, a link of that chain reaching up to the first God of all.” The same letter set up “the Court of the Lord’s House,” three officers who were to meet every May 10 (from August 1877 also every November 10) “that a portion of the weight of this infant Kingdom may not rest upon one,” and gave its opening prayer (Williams to James, April 2, 1877, and to Hendrikson, August 8, 1877, Church History Library, MS 31876). In October 1877 he asked for “a building” that, “when finished, will serve to carry out Temple ordinances,” and in September 1878 he sent the words of dedication, to be spoken by James walking round the building and standing on the southeast corner, “from whence cometh the greatest light” (Williams to James, Hendrikson and the Montana Saints, October 12, 1877, and to James, September 1878, Church History Library, MS 31876). James wore the robe to administer the sacrament in the 1880s (see the Montana church, below). Williams’s later letters add other rites. A high priest was to take a newborn child in his arms and say, “by virtue of the high & holy Priesthood given to me I bless this child in the name of Jesus”; the sacrament, of bread and water or wine, was a “Spiritual Strength” given only when “administered legally by the High Priesthood,” and the priesthood was to call on Christ to make good his word “at the very time of changing whatsoever he called his Body & Blood into what we were about to partake of”; and in November 1881 Williams ruled that “none partake of it but the Church in fair standing,” admitting children from eight years old (Williams to Hendrikson, October 4, 1879; “The Lord’s Supper,” 1881; Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1878–1881). The Montana council later opened it to friends who were not members (see below).

Worship and order in Montana, 1880–81. The Race Track council’s minutes show how Williams’s teaching was lived in the Lord’s House. At a joint meeting on May 10, 1880, “Cainan’s Epistle” was read and the sacrament “administered by Bro James who was clothed in his Robe.” James then gave a date: “Orson Pratt made it out that 61 years after the organisation of Church of J Smith will be the coming of Christ. it was set out in the rocks of the Pyramides of Egypt. if we have but 11 years more then it will be a short work to be done,” that is, in 1891 (minutes, May 10, 1880, Church History Library, MS 31876, Minutes, 1880). The gifts of the spirit continued, under order. In March 1881 the council examined a member who said he received the influence of “spirits,” and it recalled Cainan’s answer that the Evil One could give an influence that seemed good but was false. President Andrew Hendrikson ruled that anyone who would speak in tongues in a public meeting was led by the wrong influence, since God’s spirit would never lead anyone to overstep the order of the church (minutes, March 26, 1881, MS 31876, Minutes, 1881; translation from the Danish generated by Claude). See What became of the spiritual gifts?

The Montana church (1882–1954)

From prophet to vote. Before he died in 1882 Williams wrote that the organization should stay as it was for twelve months and that William James should then be put before the whole church and, if sustained “by uplifted hand,” remain from year to year. After him the church chose its presidents at general assemblies (1883, 1886); Andrew Hendrikson presided from 1886 to 1921 and George Johnson until shortly before his death in 1954, with which, Anderson writes, the church came to an end; USU’s finding aid gives 1969 for its formal disbanding (see How did a prophet’s movement become a church run by vote?).

Worship and neighbors. The Lord’s House near Racetrack, built in 1878–79 and dedicated on August 9, 1879, held Sunday meetings. The council kept the sacrament open to non-members who came as “friends for the gospel’s sake,” and in 1886 it accepted an invitation to use the Reorganized Church’s meeting house in Deer Lodge City (see How did Latter-day Saints, the Reorganized Church and the Morrisites live alongside one another?).

What the later church kept. Gems of Inspiration, compiled by John R. Eardley and printed by Joseph A. Dove in 1899, is the movement’s own selection a generation after Morris. About a third of it is a history of the Morrisites, written against the Mormon church; the rest is Morris’s articles, among them “Keys of the Priesthood” and “The Order of Sevens,” and Williams’s visions, letters and records, including two letters to the Saints in Montana. It keeps the order of gods, angels and birthrights and the line from Morris to Williams, condemns polygamy, and does not use the word reincarnation. What was taught in the church’s last decades is not yet known.

Doctrine and disappointment, 1887–91. The church’s court, which Hendrikson said existed “to argue principles, doctrines or anything else, and as Cainan is not with us there is no other place to appeal,” set out “the doctrines of Cainan” in May 1887: a high priest’s “first birthright in the spiritual state” placed him in his father’s priesthood, and his birthrights in mortality gave him experience of everything, “so that when he becomes a God he then can judge and rule in righteousness from his own experience,” while some of the heirs of salvation “merely pass thro a mortal womb so as to go across over to Paradise,” according to their behavior “in their spiritual estate” (High Court minutes, May 10, 1887, Church History Library, MS 31876, Minutes, 1886–1887). In 1880 William James had looked for the coming of Christ in 1891. On November 10, 1891, Hendrikson told the court that they had been “led for many years past to expect that in the present year the ‘winding up scene’ would take place,” but that he still believed “he should live to behold the coming of the Son of Man, as he had been promised by the Lord’s servant Cainan.” Eardley looked instead to a coming “war of the nations,” which Williams had once foretold to him, and to “the coming forth of the next servant according to birthright, even Mahalaleel”; he likened the Saints to the wise and foolish virgins, “drowsy and sleepy,” though some “have retained their faith.” He also urged prayer standing with hands raised, as Williams prayed, “with body erect, and hands uplifted to heaven,” and called kneeling “the Sectarian method” (court minutes, November 10, 1891, Church History Library, MS 31876, Minutes, 1891). The hope outlived 1891. In June 1892 George Thompson asked how the churches would receive “our Joseph with his two Councelers — Enoc and Cainan —” if they appeared to gather “the scattered sheep of Weber,” purge the church in Utah and make headquarters in Jackson County (the spelling “Enoc” is Thompson’s). After Parson’s death that month Eardley concluded that “Joseph’s mission was to reveal the diagram, and Foreshadow the coming kingdom. All other things which transpired were of a secondary consideration” (Thompson to Eardley, June 26, 1892; Eardley to James Dove, June 18, 1892, copy; Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to Andrew Hendrickson, 1892).

William W. Davies and the Kingdom of Heaven (1866–1881)

Davies, who had been with Morris, began his own community in Montana in 1866 and led it near Walla Walla, Washington, from 1867; many of its members were converts from San Francisco and Portland who had never been Morrisites. Watson calls it “a novel and mystical Christian sect” with roots in Morris and Joseph Smith (Watson, p. 111). Davies declared in 1867 that a third dispensation had been opened through him on January 24, 1866, in which baptism was set aside for “the Holy Vow of THE COVENANT”; members gave their labor and property to a common fund, and he signed himself the Standard of Israel and dated his papers by the Millennial Era (Watson, pp. 124, 128, 184). He testified in 1881 that reincarnation was “taught & believed” (Watson, p. 186), and he taught that his sons Arthur, later called the “Walla Walla Jesus,” and David carried the spirits of the Son of God and of God the Father; both died of diphtheria in February 1880 (Watson, pp. 191–93). His own pamphlets, hymns and interviews, which give his side, are in Watson, pp. 111–97. See William W. Davies and the Kingdom of Heaven.

Other branches

The Doves. James Dove and his son George followed Morris, then Williams, then Davies; in 1873 George began receiving communications and organized a Church of the Firstborn in California of about thirty people, seeing himself as the publisher and organizer of Morris’s revelations rather than a prophet (Watson, pp. 198–99). The Doves published The Spirit Prevails and the roll of membership in 1886, and James Dove’s Treatise on Re-Incarnation (1892), which Watson calls the earliest book on the subject in the wider Mormon tradition (Watson, pp. 199, 208–64). The Montana council disowned them as “pretenders” in 1885.

James Brighouse. His Order of Enoch in Independence, Missouri (1884), taught celibacy; no Morrisite source for its ideas has been traced (see the lineage chart under History).

Later echoes

What follows shows how Morrisite teaching has been read and borrowed since, not that it was carried on. Leland Jensen’s Missoula sect already held that the Montana State Prison at Deer Lodge was Ezekiel’s temple; after its members came on the Morrisites in 1990 through Anderson’s history, they took Williams’s 1879 letter as confirmation, and Neal Chase set out the connection in Ezekiel’s Temple in Montana (1990). Anderson finds that the book credits Williams with far more prophetic ability than he had (Revisited, pp. 216–18). Chad Daybell’s assignment of past lives and talk of marriages across lives have invited comparison with Williams’s practice, but no line of descent has been shown, and both draw on older Latter-day Saint ideas; Israel A. J. Dennis’s Church of the First Born (1889) shows how readily the name and the doctrine could arise on their own. See Is there a line from Morrisite teaching to Chad Daybell’s?

Common misreadings

  • “Morris opposed polygamy.” His own article treats plural marriage as a law for this life, at God’s time, with one wife sealed for eternity; it was not practiced at the fort (see Marriage, above).
  • “Reincarnation came from Charles B. Thompson.” Anderson proposed it; Watson disputes it (see the question).
  • “Church of the Firstborn” as Morris’s own name for his church. It is later usage (Glossary).
  • “Williams prophesied that Deer Lodge County would be the site of the Second Coming.” His letter names the region where the Saints lived, “the Sides of the North,” and sets no date.
  • Davies’s community as a Morrisite congregation. It was a new movement led by a former Morrisite, made up largely of new converts.
