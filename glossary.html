Glossary

Names and terms

The names of the churches, places, offices and titles that readers meet across this guide, in brief. Each entry links to where the guide says more.

Church of the Firstborn
The name by which Joseph Morris’s church, organized on April 6, 1861, at South Weber, Utah, is now usually known; its members are usually called Morrisites. It was not the church’s first name. Morris’s revelations call it simply “my Church,” and the revelation on baptism of April 3, 1861, speaks of members of “the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter Day Saints, under the fullness of the gospel” (The Spirit Prevails, No. 55, p. 78). Anderson, following Richard W. Young (1890), gives the name at the organization as the Church of Jesus Christ of Saints of the Most High, and says it “later on” became known as the Church of the Firstborn. The Prophet Cainan declared that the church would “also be called The Church of the Firstborn,” and the Doves’ California congregation used the name from the 1870s (Anderson, Revisited, pp. 57, 66, 182). See more
Morrisites
Followers of Joseph Morris, and members of the churches that descended from his church. In South Weber the word lingered long enough to be used as a schoolyard taunt. During the siege Daniel H. Wells struck “Morrisite” and “Morrisitism” from Burton’s dispatches and told him to do the same, “as we do not wish to perpetuate them. When you speak, or write of the place, call it South Weber, or Kington’s Fort” (Wells to Burton, June 15, 1862, Daniel H. Wells, letterpress copy book, MS 1344, Church History Library, pp. 13–14). See more
Kington Fort (Kingston Fort)
The abandoned 1853 settlers’ fort at South Weber where Morris gathered his followers in 1861–62, and where the posse besieged them in June 1862. It was named for Bishop Thomas Kington; “Kington” is the correct spelling, but many accounts write “Kingston.” Search under both. See more
Morrisite War
The common name for the siege of Kington Fort, June 13–15, 1862, by a territorial posse led by the deputy marshal Robert T. Burton, which ended in the deaths of Joseph Morris and others and the arrest of the men in the fort. See more
Roll of membership
The list of 430 people baptized into Morris’s church in 1861–62, printed in San Francisco by George S. Dove & Co. in 1886. Its compiler wrote that many baptized were never recorded, and Josiah D. Price, baptized on December 3, 1861, counted himself the “431st person” in the church (Josiah D. Price journal, 1858–1873, photocopy, P26-22 f1, Community of Christ Library Archives), so the printed roll is not complete. Pencil notes in one copy mark officers; they are not part of the printed roll. See more
The Spirit Prevails
Morris’s revelations, articles and letters, published by George S. Dove & Co. in 1886. For his followers it was scripture. See more
First Council
A council of Morris’s church. Forscutt, who was its clerk, names among its members John Parson, John and Richard Cook, and himself (Forscutt, “Sketch of Joseph Morris,” MS p. 51). Pencil notes in one copy of the roll mark John Banks and Richard Cook as of the presidency (pencil annotations in the copy of the roll transcribed by Larson). See more
Apostle
An office in Morris’s church. The pencil notes in one copy of the roll mark fourteen men as apostles, among them James Dove, John R. Eardley, Mark H. Forscutt, John E. Jones and R. J. Livingston (pencil annotations in the copy of the roll transcribed by Larson). See more
Morristown
The Morrisite settlement at Soda Springs, Idaho, founded in May 1863 under the protection of Patrick Edward Connor (Shupe, Caribou County Chronology, p. 13); its “Lower Town” stood below the soldiers’ Camp Connor. Most of it now lies under Alexander Reservoir. See more
Prophet Cainan
The name taken by George Williams (1814–1882), an Englishman who claimed to be Morris’s successor and led the church that gathered in the Deer Lodge Valley, Montana, from the late 1860s. He left America for England about January 1869 and led the Montana Saints by letter for the rest of his life; in his last illness George Thompson wrote for him. He died in England on April 25, 1882, and the church kept his birthday, February 14, as one of its two feast days (Church History Library, MS 31876; Anderson, Revisited, p. 218). Church of Jesus Christ of Saints of the Most High
According to Anderson, following Richard W. Young (1890), the name Morris’s church took at its organization on April 6, 1861 (Revisited, p. 66; see Church of the Firstborn). It was the name of the Montana church, used by its council by 1873 and kept after Williams’s death on April 25, 1882, when it was led in turn by William James, George Thompson, Andrew Hendrikson and George Johnson. By Anderson’s account it ended with Johnson’s death in 1954 (Revisited, pp. 212, 214); USU’s finding aid gives 1969 for its formal disbanding. See more
Lord’s House

The Montana Morrisites’ wood-frame church near Racetrack, built in 1878–79 on Andrew Hendrikson’s land and named for the panel over its doors. Williams first asked for it in October 1877, as a building that “when finished, will serve to carry out Temple ordinances,” left the choice of site to “the majority” in January 1878, and in August 1878 called it “a Temple in miniature and place of worship for the faithful,” and sent the words for its dedication in September 1878 (Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1876–1877, and Letters from Cainan to William James and Montana followers, 1876–1878).

It was dedicated on August 9, 1879 (Race Track council minutes, July 29, 1882, Church History Library, MS 31876), and the church kept the day as its anniversary and one of its two feast days, on which surviving Morrisites still gathered there in 1969 (Anderson, Revisited, p. 218); in 1884 John R. Eardley paid to have it painted (Church History Library, MS 32226). The anniversary of its opening became a feast day at William James’s proposal, which Williams confirmed in September 1880 as “a perpetual day … like the 14 day of Feb.” (record of letters from Cainan, 1873–1879, Church History Library, MS 31876). Five months after the dedication the RLDS missionary E. C. Brand preached in it and called it “a neat, clean, well finished house,” “nicely hard finished, and painted, with a handsome pulpit, cloak room, etc.,” and the work of the members’ own hands (Butte City, January 27, 1880, Saints’ Herald 27, 1880, p. 77). In 1887 three trustees were appointed so that it could be exempted from taxation.

In November 1891 Eardley proposed moving it, because it stood out of the way across fields, tramps broke into it and sparks from passing locomotives had set the grass round it alight, to “the other end of Bro. Hendrickson’s field, adjoining the Public School House”; the matter was laid over (Church History Library, MS 31876, Minutes, 1886–1887, and Minutes, 1891). It was still the church’s meeting place at the end of 1892, when Eardley urged the Saints to see that “the Lord’s House is none other than the very gate of Heaven to them” (Eardley to Hendrikson, December 25, 1892, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to Andrew Hendrickson, 1892); whether it was moved later is not yet known. In April 1934 a neighbor wrote that its door had been “securely locked and the windows neatly boarded up” (Saints’ Herald 81, April 10, 1934, p. 460). It is the only Morrisite church building known to survive.

Kingdom of Heaven
The community William W. Davies led near Walla Walla, Washington, from 1867 to 1881 (Anderson, Revisited, pp. 157, 187–92), also called “the Kingdom of Heaven on Earth”; its members called themselves Christians of the Millennial Era. Many of them were San Francisco and Portland converts who had never been Morrisites. See more
Standard of Israel (S.I.)
Davies’s title for himself, which he signed after his name. He also signed letters “Father” (Watson, pp. 124, 128, 184). See more
Millennial Era (M.E.)
Davies’s reckoning of years from January 24, 1866, when, he taught, the presence of God descended upon him. His papers are dated both ways, as “1876 & 11” (Watson, pp. 124, 128, 184). See more
The vow
The covenant a member of Davies’s community made on joining, administered by Davies or his missionaries. Members gave their labor and property to the common fund (Watson, pp. 124, 128, 184). See more
Walla Walla Jesus

The popular name for Arthur Davies (1868–1880), son of William W. Davies. Davies said that on the eighth day after the boy’s birth he proclaimed the Messiah come again in him and named him “Messiah Son of David”; the boy later took the name Arthur (1882 interview, Watson, p. 192). His younger brother David was held to carry the spirit of God the Father. Both died of diphtheria in February 1880 (Watson, pp. 191–93).

The belief spread beyond Walla Walla: in 1877 the Carson Valley News reported that R. J. Livingston had sold his property at Genoa, Nevada, to live near a child born at Walla Walla who was to be the Savior (May 29, 1877, p. 3). George Williams, writing from England in April 1877, counted “another with his come to Walla Walla and see my little Jesus” among “miserable speculators on your credulity” (Williams to William James, April 2, 1877, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to William James and Montana followers, 1876–1878). The name was current in his lifetime: at his death in February 1880 the Walla Walla Watchman called him “the Walla Walla Jesus Christ, so called” (reprinted in the Seattle Daily Intelligencer, March 4, 1880, p. 3; see Questions). The name is also used in the Washington State Archives’ index to the 1880 lawsuit against Davies, in Tony Zbaraschuk’s 1989 paper and in the Fort Walla Walla Museum’s collection description.

Seventh Angel

A title Joseph Morris claimed, after the angel who sounds the last trumpet in Revelation. For Morris it marked a stage in the soul’s ascent through the angelic ranks. The phrase was already in Latter-day Saint use: Doctrine and Covenants 88:112 names “Michael, the seventh angel, even the archangel,” and in 1844 W. W. Phelps’s funeral sermon for Joseph Smith alluded to him as the seventh angel who pours out the last vial of wrath (Revelation 16).

He claimed it before the apostles John Taylor and Wilford Woodruff at South Weber on February 11, 1861, saying also that he held “six times more keys of the Priesthood” than Joseph Smith (Wilford Woodruff journal, February 11, 1861, MS 1352, Church History Library). An informer heard him claim it again on May 12, 1861: “I say now I profess to be the Seventh Angel. The seventh thousand years is now ushered in” (“Doctrines of the Morrisites, May 12, 1861,” informer’s report, “Morrisite papers, 1861–1862,” Brigham Young office files, CR 1234 1, Church History Library).

In Morris’s “Order of Sevens” the seven angels of the seven churches are Seth, Enos, Cainan, Mahalaleel, Jared, Enoch and Methuselah, and Seth, the seventh, presides (Gems of Inspiration, 1899); George Williams’s writings call Morris “Seth” (Watson, p. 90), and in 1873 Williams wrote of “the seventh Angel whom they wickedly slew” (Williams to Margaret Thomas, July 24, 1873, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Margaret and William P. Thomas, 1866–1882). James Brighouse later used it for the title of his tracts.

Sides of the North
George Williams’s name for the Montana Saints’ region, from Psalm 48:2 (“the city of the great King” on “the sides of the north”). He wrote in 1879 to the “Saints of the Most High in Stake No. 1 on the Sides of the North in Montana” that the City of the great King would come down there before the Lord’s coming, and called the Lord’s House “my first House of Prayer on the Sides of the North” (MS 31876, Church History Library). See more
Reincarnation
The teaching that spirits return to the earth in new bodies. Morris dated it to a revelation of 1859; Forscutt called it Morris’s “strange theory of repeated embodiment.” Davies’s community taught it too. George Williams told the men he anointed at Council Bluffs and Omaha in 1869 who they had been: Carlton Fellows “was the Father to Simon Peter; Jonas was your name,” Hans Nielsen had been Eliab, David’s brother, and Jens Jensen had been Titus (record book, 1869, Church of the Firstborn (Morrisites) records, MS 31876, Church History Library; translation from the Danish generated by Claude). Latter-day Saint writers called the belief by many names, among them re-embodiment, repeated embodiment, “baby resurrection,” plural or multiple mortal probations and “re-habiliment of spirits”; in this tradition, Kirk Watson notes, all of them meant a person reborn as a human of the same sex, never as an animal (Watson, Scriptural Mormonism, Ep. 113, November 14, 2026, at 6:16–7:04). See more
Consecration (law of consecration)
Giving all one’s property to the church. The phrase comes from Joseph Smith’s revelations (Doctrine and Covenants 42). Morris’s revelation of December 28, 1861, made laying all one possessed at the prophet’s feet the test of who could be delivered (The Spirit Prevails, No. 185), and the members consecrated their property on December 30, 1861 (Josiah D. Price journal, P26-22 f1, Community of Christ Library Archives). Revelations of 1862 forbade giving consecrated property back to those who left, the dispute that led to the writs and the siege (Nos. 202 and 254). See more
Oath (1862)
The oath every member of Morris’s church swore to the kingdom of God, to keep all the laws of God and the counsels of his prophet, given and to come. The revelation prescribing it is dated January 23, 1862 (The Spirit Prevails, No. 208); by Josiah D. Price’s account it was sworn on February 12, 1862, twelve people at a time, in the order of office set by No. 209. A separate soldier’s oath to defend the kingdom was sworn on May 26, 1862 (Josiah D. Price journal, P26-22 f1, Community of Christ Library Archives). See more
Foreshadowing Day
May 30, 1862, at Kington Fort, when Morris’s church acted out the coming kingdom in a procession round the fort. Morris rode first, as the representative of Jesus, followed by the twelve generals on horseback, the first four on white, red, black and pale horses, and then seven companies of armed men; the leaders wore crowns on their hats, seven on Morris’s and fewer down the order of rank, and in the afternoon the lesser priesthood marched (The Spirit Prevails, “History of Events,” pp. 6–7; Eli Smith to Christopher Layton, June 2, 1862, CR 1234 1, Church History Library; W. P. Thomas to his wife, May 30, 1862, MS 31876, folder 21, translation from the Welsh generated by Claude). It was the “foreshadow” of the coming kingdom that the revelations promised; two weeks later the posse arrived. See also Where did the rituals come from? See more
Seth
The name George Williams and his followers used for Joseph Morris. In Morris’s “Order of Sevens” (below), Seth is the seventh and presiding angel, and Williams’s line of servants ran from Enos (Joseph Smith) to Seth (Morris) to Cainan (himself) (Gems of Inspiration, 1899; Watson, p. 90). Hence “Seth’s Diagram,” and S. P. Guhl’s letter of 1871 to “Seth’s disciples.” See also Prophet Cainan and Seventh Angel. See more
Order of Sevens
Morris’s teaching of seven angels who preside over the last dispensations of each earth: Seth, Enos, Cainan, Mahalaleel, Jared, Enoch and Methuselah, with Seth, the seventh, presiding (“The Order of Sevens,” in Gems of Inspiration, 1899). Williams kept it. See also Reincarnation. See more
Seth’s Diagram (the diagram)
Morris’s chart of the worlds: a quorum of 144,000 celestial globes, each governed by a god, all under the Most High God, with Enoch’s Zion, Adam’s world, the spirit world, Paradise, hell and outer darkness. Morris explained it at Weber in 1861; Eardley printed it in 1899 as “The Rounds of Eternity: Joseph Morris’ First Diagram” (Gems of Inspiration); a hand-coloured version with Swedish labels heads Williams’s record of letters of the 1870s (Church History Library, MS 31876); and missionaries were shown “the Eternal Rounds” near Warm Springs in 1899 (John Welch, missionary journal, July 17–18, 1899, MS 29881, Church History Library). In 1892 Eardley wrote that “Joseph’s mission was to reveal the diagram, and Foreshadow the coming kingdom” (Eardley to James Dove, June 18, 1892, copy, Church History Library, MS 31876). See more
Birthright
The teaching that each man’s place in the priesthood was fixed by lineage from Adam through Seth and by his “first birthright in the spiritual state.” Williams kept it from Morris; the Montana court set it out as “the doctrines of Cainan” in May 1887 (High Court minutes, May 10, 1887, MS 31876, Church History Library); and an 1891 Salt Lake Tribune letter said men whose birthrights did not match their offices would be put in their right places “when a prophet comes having the Urim and Thummim.” William James claimed the Montana presidency for life “by birthright” in 1886. See more
Robe, crown and scepter
The regalia of Morris’s church. Morris had a robe and crown, and he and his officers carried scepters; on Foreshadowing Day he rode in his robe with the rod in his left hand. The crown, as Wilford Woodruff saw it on June 16, 1862, was of tin painted yellow and lined with red cloth. Three painted wooden scepters survive in the Church History Museum: Morris’s, declaring him sent “to RULE over all ADAM’S posterity,” and those of Hugh Park (“number five”) and Daniel Smith (“number seven”) (LDS 65-3058 to 3060; Anderson, Revisited, pp. 116–17). Williams prescribed a linen robe for Montana’s president in 1874 and a fuller robe “in semblance to the Celestial attiring” in 1877, saying Morris had wanted every minister so clothed. See more
Soldiers of Israel
The armed companies of Kington Fort. On May 16, 1862, the “First Division of the Soldiers of Israel” was formed, with Morris as Captain at Arms, John Fred. Klingbeck as Lieutenant at Arms and seven captains, each over twelve men, and a second division of three companies followed; officers and men swore to defend “the Law of God.” Richard W. Young printed the oath and rosters, 142 men, in 1890 (The Contributor 11, no. 8, p. 284). See more
Stake No. 1 and Stake No. 2
The two divisions of the Montana church from 1874: William James’s Stake No. 1, at Warm Springs, and Andrew Hendrikson’s Danish-speaking Stake No. 2. Williams ruled in 1877 that each member might choose “the Stake convenient,” under “two distinct Presidencies” (Williams, letter of January 11, 1877); that May the stakes agreed to keep out of each other’s ground (council minutes, 1877–78, Church History Library, MS 31876). In 1886 James and the officers of Stake No. 1 broke with the church over George Thompson’s election (Church History Library, MS 32226, folders 3 and 7). See more
Court of the Lord’s House
The Montana church’s court of three officers, created by Williams in April 1877 to meet every May 10, and from August 1877 also every November 10, “that a portion of the weight of this infant Kingdom may not rest upon one” (Williams to James, April 2, 1877, and to Hendrikson, August 8, 1877, MS 31876, Church History Library). Hendrikson said it existed “to argue principles, doctrines or anything else,” since “Cainan is not with us.” See more
Elect lady, Lady Elect and High Priestess
Women’s offices. A form “for ordaining an elect lady,” signed by Morris in 1861, anointed the woman “that thou mayest be an heiress of the Priesthood of Melchisedec” (CR 1234 1, Church History Library). In Montana in 1872 Margaret Thomas was reordained “as the Lady elect to preside over the Female Order” (MS 32226). Williams called Marie Mortensen the Omaha congregation’s “High Priestess” in 1870 and had Sister Rasmussen ordained “High Priestess” for Stake No. 2 in 1877 (MS 31876). Earlier still, at Council Bluffs in 1869, Petronella Borgström was “ordained a priestess after Abel’s order” after her baptism on May 28, and Sister Damander was ordained “to Abel’s order” with her husband in July; Mortensen’s written ordination sets her apart, with the birthright of Esther, “to preside over the Sisters in Nebraska.” In Montana in 1890 two girls, Anna Johnson and Mary Ann L. Beck, were ordained “to the lesser priesthood” (Church History Library, MS 31876, Member records, circa 1869–1890; translations from the Danish generated by Claude). See more
Josephites
The common nineteenth-century name for members of the Reorganized Church, after its leader Joseph Smith III. Danish “Josephites” were challenging S. P. Guhl to debate in Omaha by 1874. See Reorganized Church (RLDS). See more
Gems of Inspiration (1899)
A collection compiled by John R. Eardley, a former Morrisite, and printed in San Francisco by Joseph A. Dove: sayings and teachings of the movement, including “The Order of Sevens,” the “Rounds of Eternity” diagram and Eardley’s history of Kington Fort and the surrender. Public domain; at the Internet Archive. See more
Posse (posse comitatus)
The force that besieged Kington Fort in June 1862 was a posse comitatus, not an army: men of the territorial militia called out on Acting Governor Frank Fuller’s order of June 11, 1862, at the request of the territorial marshal, to help the deputy marshals Robert T. Burton and Theodore McKean serve the Third District Court’s writs for the arrest of Morris and others (Fuller’s order, printed in the Wells–Shaffer correspondence, Deseret Evening News, October 29, 1870, p. 2; see Documents). See Morrisite War. See more
Adam–God teaching
Brigham Young’s teaching, first preached in April 1852, that Adam is “our Father and our God” (Journal of Discourses 1:50). The Latter-day Saint church later rejected it (Spencer W. Kimball, general conference, October 1976). Morris taught that the earth was organized by “Adam’s own Father” for Adam his firstborn, and Williams that Adam would head the planet’s government; see the At a glance table. See more
Reorganized Church (RLDS)
The Reorganized Church of Jesus Christ of Latter Day Saints, now Community of Christ, which Forscutt and other former Morrisites joined in the 1860s and later. See more
“Probable”
On this site, a mark that an identification is likely but not proven: a FamilySearch match, or a person in the sources who may or may not be the one on the roll. See more
