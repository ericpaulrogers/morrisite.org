Open questions

The Evans and Thomas families of Gwendale

Two of the guide’s questions for research, on two Welsh families of the upper Deer Lodge Valley: the Thomases, who were Morrisites, and the Evanses, their neighbors and in-laws, after whom Gwendale may have been named. Pictures of the Evans house and family are in the Images gallery. Answers and corrections are welcome at contact@morrisite.org.

Were the Evans and Thomas families of Gwendale connected to the Morrisites?

Answered (in part)
In brief

For the Thomases, yes. William Price Thomas and his wife Margaret were Morrisites: he was baptized at South Weber and marched on Foreshadowing Day in 1862, George Williams wrote to them from 1866 to 1882, and the Montana church was organized in their house on Warm Spring Creek in 1872. W. W. Thomas was at Kington Fort too, though he joined the Reorganized Church in 1865 (see their notes on the roll). For the Evanses, no record yet shows that they belonged. No Evans is on the roll or in the church’s member lists, none appears in Williams’s letters to the Thomases, and neither family history calls them Morrisites. They were the Thomases’ neighbors and, through two marriages, their in-laws.

Both families were Latter-day Saints in Utah. Morgan and Ann Evans came over in 1856 with Welsh Latter-day Saint emigrants on the S. Curling (Saints by Sea, passenger 23128) and in 1860 were living at Brigham City, the settlement the Saints called Box Elder (1860 U.S. census, Brigham post office, p. 240; the page is headed Cache County). W. P. and Margaret Thomas had received Latter-day Saint patriarchal blessings in 1852 and 1855 (MS 31876), and W. P. and W. W. Thomas lived among the Welsh members at Box Elder until the winter of 1861–62, when they fled to Morris’s camp at Weber (Forscutt’s diary; see the note for Richards, William on the roll). The Thomases chose Morris and the Evanses did not, as far as the records show. In 1885 W. P. Thomas described his own leaving of Utah in 1863: “Renouncing the Mormon faith, he with a large number of others started for Montana,” escorted by General Connor as far as Snake River. Morgan Evans’s sketch in the same volume says only that he lived in Utah until 1864 and then freighted between Virginia City and Utah, and names no church (Leeson, History of Montana, 1885, pp. 1067–68, 1092). Whether the Evanses kept up their Latter-day Saint membership in Montana is not known: neither Morgan Evans’s obituary nor Joseph H. Thomas’s names a church or a minister (Butte Inter Mountain, October 2, 1906, p. 1; Anaconda Standard, January 4, 1894, p. 3).

The Reorganized Church’s Saints’ Herald names W. W. Thomas as a member from 1865 but none of the Evanses or the other Thomases; an Anna Evans near Anaconda, baptized into the Reorganization by W. W. Blair, was the wife of William J. Evans, not of Morgan’s family as far as is known (Saints’ Herald 53, 1906, p. 830). The Reorganized Church’s Deer Lodge branch register, 1880–1919, agrees: its Evanses are this Ann, baptized by Blair in 1881, and two younger Evans women baptized with her, probably her daughters; its Thomases are a Welsh family from Malad, Idaho, headed by Reese P. Thomas. No member of Morgan Evans’s family or of W. P. Thomas’s appears in it (Record of the Deer Lodge Branch, Montana District, no. 228, Community of Christ Library-Archives; Church History Library microfilm copy, MS 30683).

The only Morrisite record found so far that names Morgan Evans points the same way. At a council at Warm Spring on April 1, 1877, Andrew Hendrikson spoke on music and dancing, which he thought right within limits, and repeated what Morgan Evans had told him when Hendrikson’s people first came up to the valley: that there was a dance every week, and that the young women there went to dances at every chance. The minutes call the members “Brother”; Evans is not given the title, and he appears as a neighbour remarking on the valley’s ways, not as one of the congregation (Minutes, 1877, images 22–23, Church of the Firstborn (Morrisites) records, MS 31876, Church History Library; translation from the Danish generated by Claude).

Morgan Evans

Morgan Evans was one of the valley’s leading ranchers (his house at Gwendale is pictured at MO-IMG-116 and MO-IMG-117). By 1885 he held 800 acres in the upper valley and another thousand under fence on Race Track Creek, with a sawmill and the first grist mill in the county, built in 1872 (Leeson, History of Montana, 1885, pp. 1067–68).

In the Nez Perce War of 1877 he was captain of the Gwendale volunteers, among them John and Dan Thomas, very probably W. P. Thomas’s sons. When the call came in late July, “in ten minutes after the order came Capt. Morgan Evans left with scouts for the Big Hole,” going up the river to the summit, before Governor Potts recalled the volunteers (“The Call for Help,” New North-West, August 10, 1877, p. 2). Three letters to “Captain Morgan Evans” from James H. Mills of Deer Lodge, July 27–28, 1877, about the Nez Perce retreat, and a receipt for a breech-loading gun bought from Potts, survive as the Morgan Evans Papers (Mss 088, Archives and Special Collections, Mansfield Library, University of Montana). His obituary says that he was at the Big Hole under Gibbon (Butte Inter Mountain, October 2, 1906, p. 1), and Jim Furshong of the Copper Village Museum and Art Center understands that the company served as an ambulance team. The roster of W. A. Clark’s ambulance train and armed escort, which went from Butte to the battlefield after the fight of August 9–10, does not name him, though it says that the list is not complete (“To the Rescue,” from the Butte Miner, August 14, in the New North-West, August 17, 1877, p. 3). Whether he went to the Big Hole after the battle is not yet known.

Two families joined, 1876–1894

He and William P. Thomas were Gwendale election judges together in 1876, and in 1880 their homestead claims were among the four held up by the land office (see the roll note for Thomas, W. P., probably the Morrisite of that name). The Evanses were Welsh, like William James, the Morrisites’ local president. The family account has them in Utah for about eight years before they came to the valley in 1865 (In the Shadow of Mount Haggin, p. 134), but Women’s History Matters says Gwenllian Evans emigrated to the United States in 1868, and her obituary, a clipping shared by Jim Furshong whose newspaper and date are not yet recorded here, says she came to Montana in 1869. Neither family history calls the Evanses Morrisites. The same obituary says “the late Mrs. E. P. Thomas” was her daughter. A family chart shared by a reader names her as Gwenllian Evans (1847–1891), born at Penderyn, Breconshire, Morgan Evans’s sister, and her husband as Evan Price Thomas (1845–1891), born in Carmarthenshire, a rancher on Lost Creek; the two were killed by lightning in July 1891 (Find a Grave memorial 106925533).

Her mother’s obituary confirms that she was Morgan Evans’s sister: “The late Mrs. E. P. Thomas was her daughter” (Anaconda Standard, February 14, 1892, p. 3; see the Gwendale question). The chart and Find a Grave both make him the son of William Price Thomas, and a 1945 account of the valley’s first settlers says that William Price Thomas was already settled on Warm Springs Creek when Morgan Evans’s party came in 1865 (Montana Standard, September 23, 1945). This is very probably the Morrisite W. P. Thomas, who was also born in Carmarthenshire (patriarchal blessing, MS 31876, Church History Library; see his note on the roll).

The Anaconda Standard reported the deaths. Lightning struck their log house on Lost Creek at about half past midnight on July 17, 1891, and killed husband and wife in their bed; their son Morgan, six, asleep between them, escaped with a blistered tongue. The paper gave their ages as 46 and 44, said they had lived on the ranch since 1866, and named five children: Willie, 19, Maggie, 15, Gwynne, 13, Joe, about 8, and Morgan. Evan Thomas belonged to the Ancient Order of United Workmen, a fraternal lodge, and the two were buried together with lodge ceremonies (“In Each Other’s Arms,” Anaconda Standard, July 18, 1891, p. 3; a first report the day before had only the wife killed, July 17, 1891, p. 5).

The chart records a second marriage between the families: Morgan Evans’s daughter Rachel (1864–1951) married Joseph Henry Thomas (1863–1894), also a son of William Price Thomas, at Anaconda on September 18, 1890. Two of W. P. Thomas’s sons thus married into the Evans family. Joseph H. Thomas’s obituary confirms both links: he died at Willow Glen on January 3, 1894, “a son-in-law of Morgan Evans” and the brother of the Thomas “who was struck by lightning and instantly killed two years ago,” leaving a wife and two children and his father (Anaconda Standard, January 4, 1894, p. 3). The father’s own obituary, four months later, names among his sons “the late Joseph Thomas who died last January, and Eph Thomas, who was killed with his wife by lightning about three years ago”; “Eph” is very probably E. P. (“Death of W. P. Thomas,” Anaconda Standard, May 8, 1894, p. 3). That makes the newspapers, and not only the family chart, the evidence that Evan Price Thomas was W. P. Thomas’s son. That ties the families closely, but it does not show that the Evanses were Morrisites.

Where to start
The Evans and Thomas homestead case files (National Archives, Record Group 49); the 1870 and 1880 censuses of Deer Lodge County; the Montana church’s minutes and letters not yet read in full (Church History Library, MS 31876 and MS 32226), for the name Evans; Latter-day Saint records of the Evanses in Utah before 1865; family records, which descendants are invited to share; and the identity of a second Margaret Evans Thomas. Leeson’s History of Montana (1885, p. 1092) has a “Mrs. Margaret W. Thomas” of Stuart, born Margaret Evans, who married William Harris on July 4, 1843, and later a Mr. Thomas, who died on September 5, 1879; the Thomases were at Bannack and Virginia City in 1863, Brigham City in December 1863 and Malad City, as its first settlers, in 1864, and came to the Deer Lodge Valley on October 1, 1868. She was not W. P. Thomas’s wife, and her husband is not yet identified (compare Harris, William and Thomas, William W. on the roll).

Was Gwendale named for Gwenllian Evans?

Open
In brief

Probably, by the family’s own account: in 1982 her great-grandson said that Morgan Evans named his farm Gwendale Farms for her. But the family had three Gwenllian Evanses, the account is late and confuses two of them, and no record seen so far says that the post office took its name from the farm or that a Gwenllian Evans kept it. The land records show two Gwenllians filing claims on the same day in 1870; which was which is not yet known.

Why it matters

Gwendale was the post office of the upper Deer Lodge Valley, near Warm Springs and Lost Creek, where Morrisite settlers such as W. P. Thomas lived; in 1878 it was renamed Morristown because, the New North-West said, many of its people were Morrisites (see Deer Lodge Valley). The Evans family called its homestead Gwendale Farm (In the Shadow of Mount Haggin, p. 134). The Montana Historical Society’s Women’s History Matters calls Gwenllian Evans, Morgan Evans’s mother, Montana’s first woman homesteader and “one of the territory’s first post mistresses” (“A Farm of Her Own,” May 20, 2014).

The family account

A family account names the farm for her. In a 1982 interview, her great-grandson George P. Wellcome Jr., who drew on family notes kept by his mother, said that Morgan Evans built an estate near the family homestead in 1875 and named it Gwendale Farms “in honor of his wife’s mother.” The article makes Gwenllian the mother of Morgan’s wife, Ann, but she was Morgan’s own mother, so on this account the farm was named for her (Ed Kemmick, “George Wellcome Has ‘Copper Royalty’ in His Past,” Montana Standard, February 1, 1982, p. 5; clipping shared by Jim Furshong, who noticed the slip). Two older sources agree that Ann’s parents were Morgan and Sarah Ann Evans, not a Gwenllian: Michael A. Leeson’s History of Montana, 1739–1885 (1885), p. 1068, which says Morgan married Ann in 1854, and the Society of Montana Pioneers register of 1899, p. 77.

The first woman homesteader

The same article says that Granville Stuart’s Forty Years on the Frontier (1925) credits her as the first woman in the state to file a homestead claim. Stuart does give that distinction to the family, but to a daughter: “In May, 1869, Miss Guine Evans, daughter of Morgan Evans, filed on one hundred and sixty acres of land on Warm Spring creek. This was the first homestead entry made by a woman in Montana” (Granville Stuart, Forty Years on the Frontier, ed. Paul C. Phillips, 1925, vol. 2, pp. 34–35; his index spells her “Gwine”).

Morgan did have a daughter of that name, the eldest of his ten children: “Gwenllian, born August 21, 1855; now Mrs. David Evans, resides in Beaver Head Co.” (Leeson, History of Montana, p. 1068). She crossed the Atlantic with Morgan and Ann in 1856, aged one, on the S. Curling (Saints by Sea, passenger 23130), and appears as “Gwaan,” five, born in Wales, in their household in the 1860 census of Utah Territory (Brigham post office, p. 240, dwelling 1825); she died in 1918 (FamilySearch Family Tree LJL6-1CZ, a probable match; her brother John M. Evans’s obituary of 1914, a clipping shared by Jim Furshong, lists “Mrs. David Evans of Melrose” among his sisters). She married David Evans on December 15, 1877, at the McBurney House in Deer Lodge: “Mr. David Evans, of Glendale, and Miss Gwenne Evans, of Warm Springs” (New North-West, December 21, 1877, p. 3). They lived afterwards in Beaverhead County. But she was only thirteen in May 1869, too young to file on land, so either Stuart’s date or his “daughter” is wrong. An unmarried “Miss” Evans old enough to file in 1869 would fit Morgan’s sister Gwenllian (1847–1891), the elder Gwenllian’s daughter and later Mrs. E. P. Thomas (see the next question). There were thus three Gwenllian Evanses in the family: Morgan’s mother, his sister and his daughter.

The elder Gwenllian, 1802–1892

The elder Gwenllian was alive and in Montana in 1870. The census of the Cable City post office, which takes in the upper Warm Springs Creek, lists Gwenllian Evans, 64, born in Wales, with personal property worth $1,000 in her own name, next to Benjamin Evans, 22, a miner. With her are a girl of fifteen born in Wales, whose name is hard to read but may be Gwen, and two girls of twelve born in Utah, Sarah A. and one whose name is unclear (1870 U.S. census, Deer Lodge County, Montana Territory, Cable City, p. 58, dwelling 108; FamilySearch image group 004273884, image 105). The girl of fifteen would fit Morgan’s daughter Gwenllian, born in Wales in 1855, living with her grandmother. Morgan’s sister Gwenllian, then about twenty-three, is not in the household.

In June 1880 she was 78, widowed, and living with her two youngest grandsons, Morgan’s sons David, 5, and John, 4, as the “Mother” of the head of a household in the Upper Deer Lodge Valley; Morgan, Ann and their other children were counted in their own house the next day (1880 U.S. census, Upper Deer Lodge Valley, Deer Lodge County, Montana, enumeration district 12, dwellings 28 and 61; FamilySearch image group 005161273, images 358 and 361; Leeson, History of Montana, 1885, p. 1068, for the boys’ births in 1874 and 1875). The head of her household is on a page not yet found.

She was living with her daughter’s family in July 1891. The Anaconda Standard’s account of the lightning deaths of Evan P. Thomas and his wife says that in the other bed of the house was “Mrs. Thomas, aged 89 years, the mother of E. P. Thomas and of Morgan Evans of this city” (July 18, 1891, p. 3). Evan’s own mother, Margaret Thomas, was seventy-two and lived until 1904; the paper meant Mrs. E. P. Thomas’s mother, the elder Gwenllian, who was 89 that summer.

She died at Morgan’s house on February 13, 1892. Her obituary settles who she was: “Mrs. Gwenllian Evans died at the residence of her son, Morgan Evans, in the valley yesterday. She was born in Wales April 15, 1802, and was consequently nearly 90 years of age. She came to Montana in 1869. The late Mrs. E. P. Thomas was her daughter” (Anaconda Standard, February 14, 1892, p. 3). The funeral “was very largely attended” (New North-West, February 20, 1892, p. 3). Neither notice mentions the homestead, the post office or Gwendale. Family Tree records her as Gwenllian Rees, married to William Evans at Penderyn, Breconshire, in 1831 (KC4W-24F).

What the land records show

The federal land office issued only one Montana land title (a patent) to a Gwenllian Evans. Signed on October 30, 1877, it covered 160 acres near where Opportunity now stands (BLM General Land Office Records, accession AGS-0440-023). The land office’s ledger of claims, its tract book, shows how she got the land. It was not a homestead. She claimed it as a settler with the right to buy it first, a “pre-emption” under the law of 1841. She paid for it in July 1872, not in cash but with land scrip, a federal certificate that could be used instead of money. Her first filing gives two dates, apparently April 20, 1869, when she settled on the land, and May 12, 1870, when she filed her claim. That fits Stuart’s “May, 1869,” though the year is faint (Bureau of Land Management tract book, Montana, vol. 5, pp. 159–160, township 4 north, range 10 west, sections 4, 9 and 10; images on FamilySearch, “United States, Bureau of Land Management Tract Books”, sign-in required).

On the same day, a “Gwen Evans” filed a claim on the land next to it. Her claim lapsed and was given up at the end of 1871, and Henry Evans bought that land in 1872. Morgan Evans had settled nearby in July 1868.

The law allowed one such claim per person, so two different Evans women named Gwenllian filed that day. Only a head of family, a widow, or a single adult over twenty-one could make a claim. That rules out Morgan’s daughter, who was fourteen, but fits his mother and his sister. Stuart’s “Miss Evans” was most likely the sister, then twenty-two, whose claim became the 1877 title. The ledger does not say which woman was which; the full claim file at the National Archives should. No record seen so far says that the post office took its name from the farm, or that the elder Gwenllian kept the Gwendale office.

Where to start
The case file for Gwenllian Evans’s 1877 patent (accession AGS-0440-023; National Archives, Record Group 49), which should give her age and family standing, and the papers of Gwen Evans’s lapsed claim (declaratory statement No. 1000); the Post Office Department’s Record of Appointment of Postmasters, 1832–1971 (National Archives, Record Group 28; microfilm M841), for Deer Lodge County; the department’s site reports for Gwendale and Morristown; the New North-West; and the sources behind the Women’s History Matters essay.
