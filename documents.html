Documents to read online

Primary documents of the Morrisite movement that can be read online, with links to the institutions that hold them. Short government records are transcribed here; the transcriptions are the editor’s, and corrections are welcome. Page images belong to their repositories, and some need a free account.

Have a scan of a Morrisite pamphlet, letter or record that is not listed? Write to contact@morrisite.org. Forscutt’s “Sketch of Joseph Morris” is being edited separately and is not reproduced here.

Government records

Order calling out the posse, June 11, 1862

Utah State Archives, series 2210
What it is
Acting Governor Frank Fuller’s order to Gen. Daniel H. Wells to furnish the marshal a military force to arrest Joseph Morris. The original has not been found; this copy is in a letter from Nauvoo Legion headquarters of January 14, 1871 (document 1,215), which copies it among “communications … from predecessors of Governor Shaffer.”
Text

Executive Department
Great Salt Lake City June 11th 1862

To Genl D H Wells Commanding Militia of Utah Territory:

Sir— A requisition has been made upon me this day, by Henry W. Lawrence, Esqr.— Territorial Marshall for the Territory of Utah, through his deputies, R. T. Burton, Esqr. and Theodore McKean Esqr. for a military force to act as a posse comitatus in the service of certain writs issued from the Third Judicial District Court of said Territory, for the arrest of Joseph Morris and others residing in the northern part of Davis County, in said district.

It appears that said Joseph Morris and his associates have organized themselves into an armed force to resist the execution of said writs, and are setting at defiance the law and its officers.

I therefore require you to furnish the said Henry W. Lawrence Esqr. or his Deputies aforesaid a sufficient military force for the arrest of the offenders, the vindication of justice and the enforcement of law.

Frank Fuller
Acting Governor and Commander-in-Chief.

Newspapers

“Fatal Resistance to the Laws,” June 18, 1862

Deseret News, p. 404
What it is
The Salt Lake paper’s first account of the siege, written the day after the prisoners reached the city. It traces the writs from Enoch M. King’s complaint of May 22, served on the 24th by J. L. Stoddard, to the affidavits of H. O. Hanson and Philo Allen on June 10, and names Morris, Cook, Banks, Klemgaard and John Parsons as the men the writs were against. It reports two young men of the posse killed, “Smith and Wahler” (see Questions), and Cook, Parsons, Klemgaard and some ninety others brought in as prisoners on June 17.

“The Davis County Bandits,” June 25, 1862

Deseret News, p. 412
What it is
The result of the hearing before Judge Kinney in chambers, June 18–19, 1862. Richard Cook was fined fifty dollars and jailed thirty days for contempt and held to bail; Peter Klemgaard and Christian Neilson were remanded to prison; the other ninety-five men were held to bail of $1,500 each for the March 1863 term of the Third District Court, and stood surety for one another. It is the only list found so far of the men taken at Kington Fort, and a check on the roll: 78 of the 95 match a man on the roll, 8 more probably do, and 9 are not on it (marked * below).
On the roll
Every man on this list who matches a name on the 1861–62 roll of membership is tagged 1862 prisoner there: the 78 held to bail, with Richard Cook, Peter Klemgaard and Christian Neilson. Hover over or tap a tag to see the name as the paper printed it and what happened to him. The 8 uncertain matches are not tagged. The men not on the roll are an open question (see Questions); for the trials of March 1863, see the next entry.

“Third District Territorial Court,” April 1, 1863

Deseret News, p. 4
What it is
The court’s own report of the March 1863 term at Salt Lake City, Chief Justice Kinney presiding, in which the Morrisites taken at Kington Fort were tried. It names the seven convicted of murder, whom the Morrisites’ own histories name differently, and the eighty-five men tried for resisting an officer.
The murder trial
In People v. Peter Klemgard and others, for the deaths of the two posse men, the prosecutor entered a nolle prosequi for Richard Cook. Robert T. Burton, Judson L. Stoddart, Theodore McKean, David J. Ross and James Hales testified for the prosecution; Margaret Cook, Jens Christian Sorensen, Mrs. Dove, James Bowman, Joseph Marsh, James Mather, Mrs. Banks, David Thomas, Mrs. Gray, John O. Mather, Mrs. Garrett, Philip Hewit, Peter Swenson and Lebrecht Bar for the defence. On Friday, March 27, the jury found Peter Klemgard, Christian Neilsen, Abraham Taylor, Kadrup Neilsen, Jens Christiansen, Andrew Lee and Andrew M. Mason guilty of murder in the second degree, and John Parsons and John E. Jones not guilty. The sentences were fifteen years at hard labor for Klemgard, twelve for Neilsen and ten for each of the others. On Saturday Richard Cook, John Parsons and John E. Jones pleaded guilty to resisting an officer and were fined $100 each, and Cook’s $50 fine for contempt in June 1862 was remitted.
Two lists of the seven
The Spirit Prevails (p. 7) and Eardley’s Gems (p. 31) name the seven as Peter C. Klemgard, John Nielson, Abraham Taylor, George Lee, Christian Nelsen, Jens Christiansen and John E. Jones. The court report agrees on four, but has Kadrup Neilsen, Andrew Lee and Andrew M. Mason where they have John Nielson, George Lee and John E. Jones, and it records John E. Jones as acquitted. The report was printed within days of the verdict, and its sentences (fifteen, twelve and five of ten years) agree with Stenhouse; the Morrisite accounts were written decades later.
Resisting an officer
On Thursday, March 26, in the case of Peter L. McCue and others, the jury found the defendants guilty of resisting an officer and fined each $100. Chief Justice Kinney told them the court had been disposed to look with mercy on men who had acted under the direction of leaders they trusted. Of the eighty-five names called, sixty-eight match a man on the 1861–62 roll, nine more probably do, and eight are not on it (marked * below). The court found several defendants absent, so not all need have been fined; Stenhouse and Bancroft give sixty-six fined.

Joseph Morris’s letters and revelations

Church History Library, CR 1234 1
What it is
Morris’s letters to Brigham Young and manuscript revelations, 1858–1862, in the folders “Revelations of Joseph Morris” in Brigham Young’s office files. Kirk Watson’s edition, The Morrisites: Writings and Revelations (2026), transcribes twelve of them, from October 11, 1858, to November 26, 1860, in its section “Joseph Morris, Letters to Brigham Young,” and gives the catalog link for each. Watson notes that the revelation headed February 18, [1859], is No. 6 of The Spirit Prevails, dated there September 6, 1860.

George Morris reminiscences, circa 1867–1886

Church History Library, MS 1614
What it is
The reminiscences of Joseph Morris’s brother George Morris (1816–1897): his life in England, conversion, Nauvoo, emigration to Utah in 1848 and life in Salt Lake City, with incidents about his brother Joseph, a family genealogy and a photograph. The bound manuscript (321 images) opens “A Short sketch of the Geneoligy and History of George Morris the Son of Joseph and Elisabeth Morris”; George was born on August 23, 1816, at Handley, Cheshire, one of six children, among them Joseph. Brought to this guide’s attention by Kirk Watson, who finds in it valuable evidence of Morris’s personality, theology and life.

Two revelations to the First Presidency, January 14 and 16, 1860

Church History Library, CR 1234 1
What it is
Two manuscript revelations, signed “Jesus Christ” and addressed to “the first presidency of the Church of Jesus Christ of Later day saints and all so to the twelve apostles.” Both demand that the leaders receive “my servant Joseph.” They are filed as anonymous letters among Brigham Young’s incoming correspondence for 1860, outside the folders of Morris’s revelations, and were identified by Kirk Watson in November 2026, after his edition of Morris’s letters was published, so they are not in it.
Why they matter
They are among the earliest dated revelations of Morris to survive in manuscript. The Spirit Prevails has only No. 2 (“Winter of 1859”) before them; its No. 3 is dated March 1860. Neither is printed there, but both are early forms of later texts. The January 14 revelation opens “when you whas small in your own eyes i took you and i rose you to the apostle ship,” the same opening as No. 18, “Revelation to President Brigham Young” (October 2, 1860). Like No. 18, it charges Young with turning the Lord’s servant away from his door and speaks of “the throne of isreal.” But it is addressed to the Twelve as well as to Young, and its warnings to them, which name Jeroboam, promise “fresh apostles” in their place and threaten to cut them off root and branch, are not in No. 18, which is addressed to Young alone. The January 16 revelation requires anyone with “gould and silver and houses and lands and whives” to “lay them at the feet of my servant Joseph,” the test Morris set out again in No. 185 (December 28, 1861).
How they were received
The office docket on the January 14 letter reads “Jan 14 1860 / Balderdash / About revelations / Rec Jan 20 1860,” and the same clerk wrote “Balderdash” above the heading of each revelation. The letter is addressed “To president Brigham Young grait Sault Lake City.”
The hand
Kirk Watson, who has transcribed many of Morris’s writings, identifies the hand and spelling as Morris’s. He compared the January 14 revelation with a revelation “to president young” dated October 11, 1858, catalogued as a “Joseph Morris letter” in the folder “Revelations of Joseph Morris, 1858–1860” of the same office files. The two share the same dating formula (“October the 11 1858,” “January the 14 1860”), the same letter forms and the same phonetic spelling (“speckelating,” “whas”).
