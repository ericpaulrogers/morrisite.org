Open questions

Questions for research

Questions the guide has not been able to settle, for students, local historians, and other researchers. Questions of record can be advanced with records that are digitized or held in Utah, Idaho, Montana, or California. Questions of interpretation ask what the movement means for the history of religion; they suit theses and articles.

If you take one up, or can answer one, write to contact@morrisite.org. Answers will be credited here. The edition of Forscutt’s “Sketch of Joseph Morris” is in progress by the editor.

Questions of record

Where is Joseph Morris buried?

Open
Known
The Salt Lake City Cemetery sexton’s record lists Morris under June 15, 1862, as no. 1469; the entry just before his, no. 1468, is Peter Wahler of the posse, written “Walden” (see “Did one posse member die, or two?” below). Morris lies in the pauper ground, in an unmarked grave. The city’s register of deaths enters “Walden,” “Morris Joseph” and “Banks John” together under June 15, with the cause “Resisting Federal Warrant” and burial in the strangers’ ground; two lines above them is Jared Smith of the 19th Ward, son of Arthur P. Smith, “killed at Weber” on June 13 (Salt Lake City “Record of the Dead,” 1862, p. 52, nos. 1469 and 1472–1474; the register’s numbers are not the sexton’s). New A tradition holds that he was buried beneath a street so that he would be trodden underfoot; no published source for it has been found.
Where to look
Salt Lake City Cemetery office (plat maps of the pauper sections); sexton’s records on FamilySearch; Salt Lake newspapers for June 1862 in Utah Digital Newspapers; where the tradition first appears in print or family accounts.

Where were the dead of Kington Fort buried?

Open
Known

The men are accounted for; most of the women are not.

  • In the Salt Lake City Cemetery: Jared Smith of the posse (sexton’s record no. 1465, June 13, “Killed at Weber”); Peter Wahler of the posse (no. 1468, written “Walden”); Joseph Morris (no. 1469) and John Banks (no. 1470), in the pauper or strangers’ ground, unmarked (see Where is Joseph Morris buried?).
  • Not in the Salt Lake City record: none of the Morrisite women killed at the fort appears in it between June 13 and mid-August 1862.
  • Mrs. Bear and her baby, killed by a cannon ball on June 13: John L. Bear wrote that they were buried together that night, without saying where (autobiography, MS 10240, Church History Library, pp. 25–26).
  • Isabella Bowman, killed at the surrender: South Weber City’s cemetery records place a “Bella Bowman” outside the fence near the northeast corner of the Pioneer Cemetery, because residents did not want Morrisites inside; very probably she.
  • Unknown: the two women killed by the first cannon shot on June 13 (one probably Mrs. Marsh, not yet checked against family records) and the Danish woman remembered as “Mrs. O’Hagg” or “Haag,” killed at the surrender.
  • A local memory: in 1908 Henry Firth of South Weber showed John Ray Banks fifteen unmarked graves in the village cemetery that he said were those of Morrisites killed in the war (Banks, “A Document History of the Morrisites in Utah,” 1909, p. 2). That is more than the known dead; the Morrisite letters of 1891 count eleven Morrisites killed.

Bendt Nielsen, buried in the Pioneer Cemetery after his death on July 15, 1862, was a Latter-day Saint settler, not a casualty. For the names and dates of the dead, see Who died at Kington Fort?; for the cemetery, the Kington Fort page.

Where to look
Davis County death and burial records for 1862; South Weber Ward and Weber Stake records; Thomas Peek’s South Weber diary (owned in 1969 by Richard O. Tucker); family records of the Bear, Bowman, Hegg and Marsh families; the Deseret News for June 1862; testimony at Robert T. Burton’s trial in 1879. If you know where any of the dead were laid, please write.

Did Brigham Young ever answer Morris?

Open
Known

About a dozen of Morris’s letters survive in Young’s office files (CR 1234 1). Anderson found no reply, and reports a penciled note on the first letter, “He’s weak minded.” Two more Morris texts, revelations dated January 14 and 16, 1860, were found in 2026 filed as anonymous letters; a clerk docketed them “Balderdash” and received them January 20, 1860, and the first charges Young with turning the Lord’s servant away from his door (Documents). That docket is so far the only recorded reaction from the office. Kirk Watson’s edition transcribes twelve of the letters, October 11, 1858, to November 26, 1860, with catalog links (dated list on the Documents page); others may be catalogued as anonymous.

A story that Young did answer Morris face to face was going round among missionaries by July 1862: Jacob G. Bigler, writing from Liverpool on news brought by Chauncey W. West, who left Salt Lake City in mid-May, says Morris offered to make Young his counselor and Young dismissed him with an obscenity, which “Ended the interview.” The same letter has Morris’s horsemen to be met by “2,0000 of heavenly messengers to wipe out the Brighamites.” It is hearsay, with no date, place or witness, and it was written before news of the siege could reach England (Bigler to Joseph F. Smith, July 5, 1862, Joseph F. Smith papers, MS 1325, Church History Library, General correspondence, “A–Cl, 1862,” images 56–57). New

Where to look
Young’s letterpress copybooks and office journals, 1857–1861, in the same collection; the Journal of Wilford Woodruff for February 1861; the anonymous letters in the same files.

What did Joseph F. Smith write to Niels Anderson?

Open
Known
In 1903 Niels Anderson asked the church to help his wife, Mary Christoffersen Anderson, whose jaw was shot away in 1862. His letters survive (CR 1 191, digitized), and they answer a letter from Smith dated February 25, 1903, which is not in the file. Whether any help followed is unknown.
Where to look
Joseph F. Smith and First Presidency letterpress copybooks for 1903; Bannock Stake and Soda Springs ward records.

Who were the Morrisites?

In progress
Known
The roll of membership printed in 1886 lists 430 people baptized at South Weber in 1861–62. Since November 2026 this guide has linked 183 of them to FamilySearch Family Tree, marked 81 men named among the prisoners of June 1862, recorded the later path of 59, grouped families, and begun pages on individual members (for example John Parson and Erik Eliasson). Counts of sex, origin, age and birthplace are on Who they were. Most members are still known only by name, and the identifications marked probable need checking. For what they believed, see Teachings.
Where to look
The roll; Slaterville and other ward records; the 1860 and 1870 censuses; Soda Springs, Carson Valley, and Deer Lodge records; Black, Early Members of the Reorganized Church; descendants’ family records. Findings are added to the roll and person pages with their sources; living people are excluded.

What became of the California congregation?

Open
Known
George Dove, who with his father James had followed George Williams and then William W. Davies, began receiving communications in 1873 and organized a Church of the Firstborn of about thirty people (Kirk Watson, ed., The Morrisites: Writings and Revelations, 2026, pp. 198–99). Their own pamphlet of 1879 dates the founding to July 2, 1876, in San Francisco, with five baptized (George S. and James Dove, A Voice from the West, p. 22; see Chronology). The Doves led the church in San Francisco after 1882 and published its books into the 1890s. A second body existed in California by 1874: in August that year S. P. Guhl, writing from Council Bluffs, reported that “Joseph Morris’s Apostles have organized themselves in California,” with John Parson “in his place as President” and R. J. Livingston and Alexander Dow as his counselors (S. P. Guhl to Andrew Hendrikson, Council Bluffs, August 11, 1874, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to Andrew Hendrickson, 1872–1878; translation from the Danish generated by Claude). The report is at second hand, and how this group related to the Doves’ is not known. How and when the congregation ended is not known.
Where to look
San Francisco directories and newspapers in the California Digital Newspaper Collection; probate and cemetery records for the Dove family; the imprints of later Morrisite publications.

Where are the records of the 1863 trials?

In progress
Known

At the March 1863 term, ten Morrisites were indicted for killing the two posse men: seven were convicted of second-degree murder, two were acquitted, and one case was dropped (nolle prosequi). One man was sentenced to fifteen years, one to twelve, and five to ten. Sixty-six others were fined $100 for resisting the posse (Fanny Stenhouse, An Englishwoman in Utah, 1880; Bancroft, History of Utah, ch. 22). Governor Harding pardoned them all a few days later; Anderson (Revisited, p. 145) says the proclamation named the convicted men, and Eardley says John Parson carried the pardon to the penitentiary (Gems, p. 31). The Deseret News of June 25, 1862, names everyone brought before Judge Kinney after the siege: Richard Cook and Peter Klemgaard, Christian Neilson, and ninety-five others held to bail (Documents; the men are marked on the roll). The court’s own report, in the Deseret News of April 1, 1863, names them (Documents): Peter Klemgard (fifteen years), Christian Neilsen (twelve), and Abraham Taylor, Kadrup Neilsen, Jens Christiansen, Andrew Lee and Andrew M. Mason (ten each); John Parsons and John E. Jones were acquitted, and Richard Cook’s case was dropped.

The Spirit Prevails and Gems give a different seven, with John Nielson, George Lee and John E. Jones in place of Kadrup Neilsen, Andrew Lee and Andrew M. Mason. The same report names eighty-five men tried for resisting an officer. Harding’s two pardons of March 31, 1863, are printed in full in Tullidge’s History of Salt Lake City (1886): the same seven for murder, and sixty-nine for resisting an officer, which fits the sixty-six fined with Cook, Parson and Jones (Documents). Still open: who “Kadrup Neilsen” was (he is not on the roll, though the pardon names him too), why the Morrisite accounts differ, and the court’s own case files.

Where to look
The Gordon M. Howard papers at the Utah State Historical Society (Mss B 279), box 2: People v. Peter Klingard, 1863 (folder 5), and a register of arrests of Morrisites, 1863 (folder 6). Utah territorial court case files at the Utah State Archives; the original pardons, in the territorial executive records (Utah State Archives).

Was Lot Smith at the siege?

In progress
The question
Writing from Loughborough on April 12, 1873, George Williams warned the Montana church against spirits “more wicked than Burton and Lot Smith (the murderers at Weber)” (Church History Library, MS 31876). Robert T. Burton led the posse. Lot Smith (1830–1892), sheriff of Davis County and the militia officer famous among Latter-day Saints for burning the army’s supply trains in the Utah War of 1857, is not named in the accounts of the siege used by this guide. In 1857, the Utah History Encyclopedia says, he “outshone” Porter Rockwell and Burton himself in leading the militia against the army’s trains and livestock; Anderson calls him a “well-known guerrilla fighter and at times Young’s personal bodyguard,” and the most notable of the Mormons accused of harassing the Morrisites before the war (Revisited, pp. 227, 232). He later led Latter-day Saint settlers to northern Arizona, where he died in 1892.
Probably not
On April 28, 1862, the War Department asked Brigham Young for a cavalry company to guard the overland mail and telegraph; on April 30 Young told “Captain Lot Smith and Company” that they had been mustered into United States service for ninety days “to be employed in protecting the property of the Telegraph and Overland Mail companies in or about Independence Rock” (Brigham Young to Lot Smith and Company, April 30, 1862, Lot Smith papers, J. Willard Marriott Library, University of Utah). The company left Salt Lake City on May 1, spent June in camp at Independence Rock on the Sweetwater, in what is now Wyoming, returned on August 15 and was discharged on August 16 (Kenneth L. Alford and Joseph R. Stuart, “The Lot Smith Cavalry Company: Utah Goes to War,” in Civil War Saints, ed. Alford (2012), 127–41). The siege was June 13–15. Alford and Stuart say Smith sent Brigham Young several telegrams in June but not from where, and record no trip home that month; so he was very probably hundreds of miles away, which would also explain his absence from McKean’s posse returns. Anderson does not raise the question; he notes the call of April 28 (p. 110) but does not connect it with Smith.
Sheriff in 1861
The Davis County Sheriff’s Office and Wikipedia list Smith as sheriff from 1854 to 1859, succeeded by Phelemon C. Merrill. Carmen R. Smith and Talana S. Hooper’s biography, working from the county court minutes, has Merrill in office only briefly in 1859, Smith reappointed that March and serving as sheriff under Judge Samuel Richards on March 11, 1861, and again after his cavalry service, in October and November 1862 and March 1863 (Lot Smith: Mormon Pioneer and American Frontiersman (2018), notes to the early chapters). So the Morrisite and posse-side accounts are right to call him sheriff in 1861–62. Anderson also has him acting as sheriff on December 8, 1861 (p. 82).
Known

Williams’s charge, made eleven years after the siege. The Morrisite letters printed in the Salt Lake Tribune in 1891 give Lot Smith a part before the siege, not in it: they say he came with about twenty armed men when Taylor and Woodruff met the South Weber branch on February 11, 1861, and that as sheriff at Farmington he collected the fines laid on the Morrisites for missing militia drill and attached their property, until he was “ashamed to take more” (letters 7–9 of “The Elder,” July 12, 19 and 26, 1891, p. 3; see Newspapers). Richard W. Young, writing in 1890 from the posse’s side, also says that “Lot Smith, sheriff of Davis County, while attempting to levy an execution, was met by armed men and ordered away from the place” before the siege, but does not name him among the posse (The Contributor 11, no. 9 (July 1890): 348–49).

That may be why Williams named him with Burton. John L. Bear, in the fort, remembered Lot Smith trying to take the Parks family’s horse to cover the $25 drill fine (“And you want to take a $75.00 horse for $25.00”), again before the siege (autobiography, circa 1908, MS 10240, Church History Library, p. 24). He was a witness for the prosecution at the trial of the Morrisites for resisting process in March 1863, but the report does not record what he said (Deseret News, March 25, 1863, p. 5). Lot Smith does not appear on the posse returns kept by Deputy Marshal Theodore McKean (MS 33041, Church History Library; see the posse chart), though the returns may not be complete. The horse incident is in the Chronology (Dec 1861), with Eardley’s fuller account.

To settle it
Smith’s June 1862 telegrams to Brigham Young (Brigham Young office files, Church History Library, CR 1234 1), which should say where he was; the Lot Smith papers at the University of Utah (MS 0004); the company’s muster rolls; and Burton’s report of June 1862.

Did one posse member die, or two?

Answered
Answer

Two. Jared Smith, aged nineteen, was shot on June 13, 1862, the first day of the siege, “killed by a rifle ball from the fort” (Deseret Evening News, March 7, 1879, p. 2). Peter Wahler, about twenty-four, was shot through the head on June 15, the day of the surrender. Joseph J. Romney, captain of fifty, who went with the volunteers under James Lewis to take a house on the west side about half an hour before the surrender, testified at Burton’s trial: “as we entered one man was shot by a ball coming from the northwest part of the fort. He was a Danishman and fell and blocked the door” (Salt Lake Herald, March 1, 1879, p. 3).

Wilford Woodruff’s journal for June 15 names him as “Peter Wahlen,” and the Deseret News of June 18 reports the deaths of “Smith and Wahler.” According to the transcription in his FamilySearch record, Robert T. Burton’s June 1862 report to Brigham Young (Brigham Young office files, Church History Library, CR 1234 1) sent home “the remains of Peter Wahler, 24 years old,” of the 1st Battalion, 3rd Regiment of Infantry, and said Burton could find no relatives of his in the territory. He was buried in the Salt Lake City Cemetery, where the sexton recorded the name as “Walden” (FamilySearch GBWC-GY9). In March 1863, ten Morrisites were indicted for killing the two men (Fanny Stenhouse, An Englishwoman in Utah, 1880).

Why it was missed
Smith was a local young man whose funeral Brigham Young preached, and most later accounts, including Wikipedia, remember only him. Anderson does name the second man, as John Peter Wahlin, killed as a party under Lieutenant James Lewis broke into a cabin on the west side of the fort (Revisited, p. 136), but did not connect him with Wahler. Wahler apparently had no family in Utah, and his name was spelled several ways. The “John Wahlin” of one later local history is probably the same man.
Still open
Where Wahler came from (Romney took him for a Dane, though the name looks German or Swiss), and anything else about his life.

Who were the men held after the siege who are not on the roll?

In progress
Known

Of the ninety-eight men the Deseret News of June 25, 1862, names as brought before Judge Kinney, eighty-one match names on the 1861–62 roll, eight are uncertain matches, and nine are not on the roll at all: L. Parr, S. Williamsen, J. P. Sorensen, H. Okensen, S. P. Rasmusson, J. E. Reese, J. C. Edwards, Saml. Hulse, and Peter Moffat. Three names first counted here turned out to be on the roll under other spellings, found through the emigrant company rosters of the Church History Biographical Database: J. G. Lousley is Loasle, John C. (Hans Kasper Loosli), John Trehan is Trahern, John, and Wm. Rogers is one of the roll’s two William Rodgers. Family Tree searches in November 2026 found no Morrisite tie for the other nine. They may have joined after the roll was drawn up, been in the fort without joining, or be misprinted.

All nine are on the list of prisoners taken at the surrender on June 15, 1862, and brought to Salt Lake City, which gives each man’s birthplace and age (“Morrisite prisoners, 1862 June,” Historian’s Office collected historical documents, CR 100 397, Church History Library; names as read from the manuscript): Labroch Par (Switzerland, 23), Soren Willison (Denmark, 46), Hans Okenson (Sweden, 23), Sorn P. Rasmerson (Denmark, 25), John E. Reese (South Wales, 44), John C. Edwards (South Wales, 38), Saml. Hulse (England, 30) and Peter Moffatt (Scotland, 22). In place of J. P. Sorensen the list has John Peter Lorenson (Denmark, 46); the roll’s John Peter Lorensen was born in Denmark in 1817, so “Sorensen” is very probably a misprint for him. Five of the nine were tried in March 1863: John Peter Sorenson, Soren Peter Rasmussen, John E. Reese, John C. Edwards and Peter Moffat are among the men found guilty of resisting an officer and fined $100 (Documents), and Harding’s pardon names all of them but Moffat, with Soren Willissen besides (Documents). Par, Okenson and Hulse appear in neither. So these men were taken in the fort and dealt with as Morrisites, which makes it likely that they joined after the roll was drawn up; who they were, and what became of them, is still open.

Where to look
The register of arrests of Morrisites, 1863, in the Gordon M. Howard papers (Mss B 279, box 2, folder 6), Utah State Historical Society; the 1860 census for Weber and Davis counties; ward records for South Weber and Slaterville; Val Holley, “Slouching Towards Slaterville” (2008), which names Slaterville and Marriott men among the prisoners.

Are the January 1860 revelations in Morris’s hand?

Answered (probably)
Known
The two revelations of January 14 and 16, 1860, are unsigned except as from “Jesus Christ,” name “my servant Joseph,” and share wording with Nos. 18 and 185 of The Spirit Prevails (Documents). Kirk Watson has compared the hand with a revelation of October 11, 1858, filed among the “Revelations of Joseph Morris, 1858–1860” in Brigham Young’s office files, and identifies it as Morris’s; the dating formula, letter forms and spelling agree (Documents). A comparison with a letter Morris signed in his own name would settle it.
Where to look
The folder “Revelations of Joseph Morris, 1858–1860” and Morris’s signed letters in the same files (CR 1234 1), viewable in the Church History Library catalog; other anonymous letters of 1858–61 in the same series.

What happened at the Farmington court-martial of 1861?

Open
Known
Forscutt’s “Sketch of Joseph Morris” says members of Morris’s First Council, among them John Parson, John and Richard Cook, and Forscutt himself, were summoned before a militia court-martial at Farmington in 1861 for refusing militia training, and that Forscutt was fined $56.80 (Forscutt, Sketch, MS pp. 28–29, 34–35, 51). The 1861 territorial militia records checked so far (Utah State Archives, series 2210) contain no trace of it. A Morrisite letter of 1891 says that in September 1861 the square of the fort was named as a drill ground for the neighboring settlements, that the Morrisites stayed indoors, and that the fine for missing drill, three dollars at first, rose to thirty, forty, fifty or sixty dollars, collected by Lot Smith as sheriff, beginning with Morris (“The Morrisite Tragedy,” letter 8 of “The Elder,” Salt Lake Tribune, July 19, 1891, p. 3). Forscutt’s diary, kept at the time, confirms the collector: on December 7, 1861, “Lot Smith came and read to the brethren” the fines, now $56.80, which they refused to pay (Mark H. Forscutt, diary, 1860–62 (“Pettitt’s Pocket Diary for 1860”), Mark Hill Forscutt Collection, Vault MSS 811, L. Tom Perry Special Collections, Harold B. Lee Library, Brigham Young University).
Where to look
Records of the Davis Military District of the Nauvoo Legion; Davis County court records; Woodruff’s journal and the Journal History for 1861; any later reference in the 1862 writs or the 1863 trials.

When was the photograph of Morris taken, and who is with him?

Open
Known
Anderson (Revisited, 118) dates the only known photograph of Morris to about 1841, in England, and identifies the man beside him as his older brother William, who came to America only after 1862. Commercial photography reached England only in 1841, and the second man looks much older than Morris, while family trees make William about two years his senior. Others date the picture about 1860. William, “next older then Joseph,” first came to Utah with his family in the fall of 1862, after Joseph’s death (George Morris reminiscences, MS 1614, p. 122), so a picture of the two together would have been taken in England, before Joseph emigrated.
Where to look
The original image and its owner; Anderson’s research files at USU (Caine MSS 10); Morris family descendants in England and Utah; the photographic process and studio, if a mount or case survives.

Who led the Montana church after George Williams?

Answered
Answer
From the church’s minutes, as Anderson gives them (Revisited) and as the Montana minutebook for 1881–1890 records them (Church History Library, MS 32226): William James, the local president under Williams, sustained on April 25, 1883, with Andrew Hendrikson and John R. Eardley as counselors; George Thompson of London, elected president of the church on April 25, 1886, probably the last general president; Andrew Hendrikson, president of the Montana Morrisites from 1886 until his death on July 3, 1921; and George Johnson, who presided until shortly before his death on April 29, 1954, with which, Anderson writes, the church came to an end (Revisited, pp. 212, 214); USU’s finding aid gives 1969 for its formal disbanding. Eardley, often named as the Montana leader, was a counselor and stake president who left Montana about 1900. The minutebook places both elections at the yearly general assembly on April 25, the anniversary of Williams’s death; the dates this answer gave before, May 1882 and May 10, 1886, are those of council meetings. The Chronology and the lineage chart now follow this account. Thompson’s standing was already plain in March 1882, when, writing for the dying Williams from Walthamstow, he promised to “confirm” the Montana church’s choice of officers (George Thompson to the Montana brethren, March 23, 1882, Church History Library, MS 31876).
Still open
How far Thompson’s authority reached beyond Montana and the dates of Eardley’s offices (in January 1882 Hendrikson called him “first Counselor to Cainan in England,” with no calling in Montana; by April 1887 he was President of Stake No. 1: Church History Library, MS 31876, Minutes, 1882, translation from the Danish generated by Claude, and Minutes, 1886–1887); for the years after 1954, see How and when did the Montana church end? Court and Council minutes and the Hendrikson papers in the Morrisite collection at USU (Caine MSS 10) are the place to check.

How and when did the Montana church end?

Open
Known

George Johnson presided at the Lord’s House until shortly before his death on April 29, 1954. Anderson calls him the last official Morrisite leader and dates the end to his death: by the 1940s fewer than a dozen members were active, and when Johnson died the church came to an end and the Lord’s House was boarded up (Revisited, pp. 212, 214). Surviving Morrisites, all of the second or third generation, still gathered at the Lord’s House on August 9, 1969, the anniversary of its dedication and one of the church’s two feast days, though by then the gatherings were more social than religious (p. 218). Anderson’s sources included his own interviews with Henry Hendrikson (1969) and Robert Johnson (1968). The finding aid for the Morrisite collection at Utah State University (Caine MSS 10) says the church officially disbanded in 1969, and the description of Anderson’s papers says that meetings were held irregularly until 1969, when it formally disbanded.

In July 1899 the church was still meeting: a Latter-day Saint missionary held a meeting at Race Track, lodged with “Mr Hendrickson,” and was shown “the Eternal Rounds” by a Morrisite family, the Petersons, near Warm Springs (John Welch, missionary journal, July 15–18, 1899, MS 29881, Church History Library). Don Cornelius, interviewing in the valley in 1962, found a woman of one of the founding families acting as the “nominal leader” of the Morrisites’ descendants (“Mormonism in Montana,” 1962, p. 65). The local paper, at the time, supports Anderson’s date: Johnson’s obituary calls the church “almost extinct these days” (Silver State Post, May 7, 1954, p. 7), and the Butte paper’s says the small church his group built “still stands but has not been used for many years” (Montana Standard, April 30, 1954, p. 1); the 1958 obituary of Andrew Hendrickson’s daughter Mary Ann Goldie calls the “House of the Lord Church” no longer active (March 28, 1958, p. 12); and in 1962 the new valley historical society spoke of “the Morrisite church which was located at one time” in the south valley (July 27, 1962, p. 7). See Newspapers. Earlier, the church nearly broke up: after a division in 1891–92, Eardley in April 1901 called the members together “to prevent if possible the threatened collapse of God’s little church” (Eardley to Hendrikson, April 22, 1901, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to Andrew Hendrickson, 1893–1901).

What conflicts
Small details differ even in the papers: a 1954 feature has Johnson reaching Montana “early in 1874,” his obituary and a 1956 article 1873. Henry Hendrickson, who had left the valley in 1908, told Cornelius in 1962 that the church faded about 1919–20 (interview, April 19, 1962, transcript pp. 8–10, 18; see Repositories), and Cornelius has the movement dying with Andrew Hendrikson and George Johnson, though he misdates Johnson’s death to the early 1930s. The two dates may describe different things: Anderson’s 1954 marks the end of the church as a led and worshipping body, while the 1969 of the USU descriptions may rest on the last of the feast-day gatherings, or on some formal act not yet found. No record made at the time has yet been seen for this guide: who, if anyone, presided after 1954, how often the survivors met, and what, if anything, formally ended the church are all unknown.
Where to look
The Morrisite collection at USU (Caine MSS 10), whose dates run to 1975, and Anderson’s papers there, including his 1968–69 interviews; the Deer Lodge Silver State Post (online for 1893–1963 through Newspapers.com), where Johnson’s obituary has now been found, for any report of meetings between 1954 and 1963; the Butte Montana Standard; local papers around August 1969, on microfilm at the Montana Historical Society; Powell County deed records for the Lord’s House land; and family memories, which descendants are invited to share.

What does Forscutt’s shorthand diary say about the spring of 1862?

In progress
Known
Mark H. Forscutt, clerk of Morris’s First Council, kept a pocket diary at Kington Fort from December 1861. Its longhand entries run to April 1862 (see the Chronology); from April 24, 1862, he wrote in shorthand, and about twenty pages of the diary, covering the weeks before and during the siege, have never been transcribed. The shorthand is not Pitman’s or any other published system that has been identified, but an alphabetic system of his own, and Forscutt wrote out its alphabet on the inside back cover of his 1867 journal. Alan Bernthal-Hooker, an English mathematics teacher, worked out the system in 2026, beginning from shorthand passages beside longhand names, and the shorthand pages of the 1860–62 diary have been photographed for the editor’s edition of Forscutt’s writings. They may hold an eyewitness record of May and June 1862: the drill, the Foreshadowing, the writs and the siege itself.
Where to look
The original, in the Mark Hill Forscutt Collection (Vault MSS 811), L. Tom Perry Special Collections, Harold B. Lee Library, Brigham Young University; a microfilm of the 1860–62 notebook at the Church History Library (MS 17393, “Journal, 1860–1862”). Help with the transcription is welcome; write to contact@morrisite.org.

Questions of interpretation

Where did the Morrisite teaching of reincarnation come from?

Open
Why it matters

Morris, Williams, Davies and the Doves all taught that spirits return to the earth in new bodies. The Latter-day Saint church never adopted the doctrine, but it was not unheard of among Latter-day Saints in the 1850s: Kirk Watson notes that the apostle Orson Hyde taught a form of it from 1846 to 1850, that Brigham Young and Wilford Woodruff later blamed Hyde when a belief called “baby resurrection” spread among church members in the 1850s, and that it was discussed in church meetings at Manti, in Sanpete County, in 1858–59 (there Bishop Warren S. Snow called the doctrine of “various ‘probations’” “not impossible but also not desirable” on December 5, 1858, and on July 3, 1859 said he “did not know how many Probations men had passed”; Manti Ward general minutes, Historical record, 1857–1859, Church History Library, LR 5253 11, images 130 and 222; passages located by Watson), and that it is arguably implicit in the Nauvoo teaching that people may become gods.

A Swedish copy of an article attributed to Morris teaches that the resurrection “takes place by birth,” the spirits being “brought forth by immortal parents” (Record of epistles from Cainan, 1868–1872, p. 62, Church History Library, MS 31876; translation from the Swedish generated by Claude), which is close to what Young rejected; see Teachings. Morris applied it to himself: in “The Order of Sevens” he taught that Seth had filled a second mission as Moses and was filling his third as Joseph Morris, and that Enos had returned as Elijah and then as Joseph Smith (The Spirit Prevails, p. 645; also pp. 665–66, and Morris to Brigham Young, November 21, 1860, Church History Library, CR 1234 1, in Watson, p. 47; references supplied by Kirk Watson). Where the Morrisites got it bears on how early Mormon ideas were carried and reshaped by dissenters.

What has been proposed

C. LeRoy Anderson suggested that Morris took the idea from Charles B. Thompson, leader of an earlier Latter Day Saint offshoot, reasoning that it had “no counterpart in orthodox Mormon theology.” Kirk Watson disputes this: the idea was circulating among Latter-day Saints in the 1850s; Anderson assumes Morris held it from the early 1850s, while the written record, including Joseph Dove’s understanding, says Morris “received it by revelation in 1859”; the two men’s theologies differ widely, Morris building on Nauvoo teaching about God that Thompson opposed, though both dwell on the patriarchs of Genesis; and no contact between them is known, as Anderson himself acknowledged, nor does either mention the other (Watson, correspondence with the editor, November 2026; he made the same case publicly in Watson, Scriptural Mormonism, Ep. 113, November 14, 2026, at 22:31–23:12). More generally, Watson finds that belief in reincarnation in Mormon history seldom passed from one person or book to another; far more often it came to someone by revelation and was then fitted into an existing Mormon theology (Watson, Ep. 113, at 35:36–36:06). Where Morris was when the idea reached him is still open. The records place him at Provo in 1857, where his ward disciplined him during the Reformation and where his first revelation is dated (see Chronology); Watson has found no record of him in the Sanpete minutes before he came forward as a prophet (correspondence with the editor, November 2026).

In Davies’s community
Watson summarizes Davies’s teaching as the incarnation of God the Father and Jesus Christ in two of Davies’s children, with Davies himself identified with the Holy Ghost (Watson, Ep. 113, at 24:21–24:38). Sworn testimony in the 1880 suit against William W. Davies shows how fully his branch held the doctrine. Witnesses on both sides described it: V. D. Lambert said Davies’s missionaries in San Francisco were teaching “the reincarnation of the souls” by 1868, that Davies was presented as Adam, Abraham, Noah and David returned, and that members who died would come back in new bodies and remain in the Kingdom; Christian Miller, still a member, said “the Spirit leaves the body at death & returns and is reincarnated”; and S. W. Wood listed reincarnation among the community’s principles. Davies himself testified that “the doctrine of reincarnation is taught and believed,” and five more members on his side, among them Broer Sorenson of the 1861–62 roll, described the same belief: that the soul lives on after death and returns to the earth in another body. Peter C. Klemgard, who had been at Kington Fort, told the court that the doctrine taught at the Davies camp was “not like the Morrisite religion” (Cilley, Evans and Courtney v. Davies, 1880–81, Washington State Archives; see Repositories).
Other claimants
Similar ideas appear in groups with no known Morrisite tie. James Brighouse seems to have taught reincarnation only after he left Missouri and returned to Utah (see the chronology under 1884). Israel A. J. Dennis (born 1852), a former Methodist holiness preacher and Seventh-day Adventist from Illinois, came to believe about 1881 that he was the reincarnation of the biblical Jacob, or Israel; he was briefly a member of the RLDS Church, organized a “Church of the First Born” in Kansas in 1889, joined the Latter-day Saints in Utah in 1890, and refounded his Church of the First Born in 1895. Watson finds several possible sources for that name, among them an Illinois sect that believed in reincarnation, a faith-healing church in New Jersey, and the Morrisites, but no evidence of contact with the Morrisites (Watson, “Newsie Messiah,” JWHA, 2024, and correspondence).
In the later church
In George Williams’s founding vision he learned, as Watson tells it, that he had been Cainan and then Melchizedek before he was George Williams: in his account of his second interview with celestial beings, Seth and Enos lay their hands on him and declare him “Cainan, then Melchisedec, now George Williams” (“A Description of Interviews with Celestial Beings,” in Watson, pp. 86–89, at 89; Watson, Ep. 113, at 23:54–24:11). Williams’s St. Ann’s Hill records describe the incarnations and reincarnations of divine beings (Watson, pp. 96–110), and in 1869 he told converts at Omaha and Council Bluffs who they had been in earlier lives (see Daybell, above). Yet Gems of Inspiration (1899), the later church’s own selection of Morris’s and Williams’s writings, never uses the word. James Dove’s Treatise on Re-Incarnation or Re-Embodiment (San Francisco, 1892), reprinted by Watson (pp. 208–64), is in Watson’s judgment the earliest book on the subject in the wider Mormon tradition (p. 199). See Teachings.
In the Reorganized Church’s paper

The Reorganized Church answered the teaching in print within four years of the siege. On June 1, 1866, Joseph Smith III listed “the followers of Joseph Morris, transmigrationists or regenerationists” with Sidney Rigdon, Granville Hedrick and the Strangites among the groups grown up “as an outgrowth of Mormonism” (Saints’ Herald 9, June 1, 1866, p. 162). His commas leave it open whether the second phrase describes Morris’s followers or names another group.

“Regeneration” was the word most tied to Charles B. Thompson’s Baneemyites, whose commune at Preparation, Iowa, broke up in the late 1850s and many of whose members went into the Reorganized Church; RLDS missionaries there in 1859–60 found Thompson’s teachings on “regeneration” and the “transmigration of the soul” the main obstacle (Junia Silsby Braby, “An Abode in the Wilderness: Charles B. Thompson’s Communal Society in Western Iowa,” John Whitmer Historical Association Journal 35, no. 1 (2015): 84–103, at p. 100). At Little Sioux, Iowa, in February 1864, the Reorganized Church heard Rowland Cobb, once one of Thompson’s leaders, defend for nearly two hours his teaching that the soul passes through many mortal bodies before it reaches celestial glory; answered that man has “but one earthly probation,” he promised to stop preaching it (Frederick B. Blair, comp., The Memoirs of President W. W. Blair (Lamoni, Iowa: Herald Publishing House, 1908), pp. 98–99). So Smith may have been naming the Morrisites alongside a belief he knew from the Baneemyites, or setting the two groups side by side. Kirk Watson pointed the editor to both sources.

Two months later the paper began an unsigned article in two parts, “Transmigration, or Sexual Resurrection,” against “some people who have been Latter Day Saints” who taught that the dead rise by being born again of women; that children who die in infancy must be “born again of women, and afterward be baptized into Christ”; and that “the heathen and all who knew no law in this life, will have another probation, by being born into this world again” (Saints’ Herald 10, August 15, 1866, pp. 50–53; 11, January 1, 1867, pp. 1–5). It names no group. What it describes is close to Morris’s resurrection “by birth,” and Alexander H. Smith had just met Morrisites at Austin, Nevada (see the Chronology), but it may as well be answering the 1850s “baby resurrection” belief described above.

In 1878 the apostle Zenas H. Gurley Jr., writing from Malad City, Idaho, put the Walla Walla child down to the same belief. The Utah Mormons, he wrote, “believed largely in transmigration, and this accounts for the young ‘Jesus’ that one faction of them, (the original organization having gone out of existence), claims to have among them in the valley of Walla Walla, and is called Davisites” (“Knowledge of God,” dated October 2, 1878, Saints’ Herald 26, January 15, 1879, p. 23). All three passages are checked against the page images on LatterDayTruth.org.

Where to start
The Spirit Prevails; Kirk Watson’s edition, including Davies’s 1882 interview and the Doves’ Treatise on Re-Incarnation (1892); the Scriptural Mormonism episode of November 14, 2026; Watson and Robert Beckstead’s Sunstone talk “Reincarnation in Mormonism” (2006); for background, other Sunstone talks on reincarnation among Latter-day Saints, by Virginia Bourgeous (1997), Doe Daughtrey (2011) and a 2016 panel, and Alex Criddle’s draft with Beckstead on Joseph Smith and reincarnation (2024) (see Bibliography); reports of reincarnation teaching among Latter-day Saints in Nauvoo and 1850s Utah; Spiritualism in Britain and America in the 1850s and 1860s.

Is there a line from Morrisite teaching to Chad Daybell’s?

Open
Why it matters
Chad Daybell, an Idaho author of Latter-day Saint end-times novels, was convicted in 2024 of three murders committed in 2019 and sentenced to death. Prosecutors said he used apocalyptic prophecy and stories of possession by evil spirits to justify the killings (Associated Press, June 1, 2024). Some of what he taught resembles Morrisite teaching, and the two are now compared in print and online. Whether the likeness means descent or only a shared background bears on how ideas travel along the margins of the Latter-day Saint tradition.
Likenesses
Earlier lives revealed by a seer. In 1869 Williams told converts at Council Bluffs and Omaha who they had been. Carlton Fellows had been Jonas, father of Simon Peter, and Maren Fellows had been Abigail, who “after Nabal’s death was married to King David” (record book, 1869, Church of the Firstborn (Morrisites) records, MS 31876, Church History Library; translation from the Danish generated by Claude). In January 2019 Daybell sent a document listing past lives for Lori Vallow, among them the wife of the prophet Moroni, and saying that he had been James the Just, married to her (FOX 10 Phoenix). Marriage across lives. A friend of Vallow’s testified that the two told her they had been married in a previous life (NPR, May 30, 2024). The name “Church of the Firstborn.” The Montana records are catalogued under it, and it was used in Daybell’s circle. Both took it from Latter-day Saint scripture (Doctrine and Covenants 76:54, 76:94, 93:22), and Israel A. J. Dennis’s Church of the First Born, above, shows how readily the name could arise on its own. An end close at hand. The Morrisites at Kington Fort awaited deliverance, and Sven Olson Hagg wrote in 1866 that their three and a half years of waiting were ending (see the chronology under 1865–66); Daybell, too, foretold an imminent end.
Differences
Daybell rated people on a scale of light and dark and taught that a person overcome by a dark spirit became a “zombie” whose body had to die (NPR, May 30, 2024). Christopher Blythe, who studies Latter-day Saint apocalypticism, has said he never saw that idea promoted in any Mormon group, nor “an LDS account that claimed someone’s identity was permanently taken over” (interview with Jana Riess, Religion News Service, August 18, 2020). Nothing of the kind appears in the Morrisite records read for this guide. In 1882 George Thompson, writing for the dying Williams, warned the Montana Saints not to “pray too much or they will invite foul spirits around them” (MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to William James and Montana followers, 1879–1882), and the Montana councils met defiance with trials, exclusion and shunning (see the chronology under 1873). The violence in Morrisite history was done to the Morrisites, at Kington Fort.
What has been proposed
No source known to the editor shows Daybell drawing on Morris, Williams or Davies. Blythe traces his ideas to a modern prophecy subculture on the margins of the church, which grew out of nineteenth-century visionary culture and drew on visionaries such as Julie Rowe, Michael Stroud and Douglas Mendenhall (Blythe, “The Daybells and Latter-day Saint Apocalypticism,” Sightings, February 27, 2020, and the Riess interview). The phrase “multiple mortal probations,” used for past lives in Daybell’s circle, is current among some Mormon fundamentalist groups (Cristina Rosetti, quoted by FOX 10 Phoenix). Any common ground with the Morrisites probably lies further back, in the reincarnation ideas that circulated among Latter-day Saints in the 1840s and 1850s (see the question above). From there the paths divide: Williams’s teaching about earlier lives stayed doctrinal, while Daybell’s merged with a demonology of possession.
Where to start
Christopher James Blythe, Terrible Revolution: Latter-day Saints and the American Apocalypse (Oxford University Press, 2020); the 1869 record book in MS 31876 (see Repositories); reporting of the 2023 and 2024 trials in Boise; Rosetti’s work on Mormon fundamentalist theology.

Did the Prophet Cainan read birthrights by phrenology?

Open
The page
A typescript fragment in the Morrisite collection at Utah State University (Caine Coll. MSS 10, box 3, folder 7, p. 29) is filed after a general invitation dated at Soda Springs, August 12, 1863, to “the Scattered Sheep of Joseph to Come to Meet the Prophet of the Lord.” A note in the margin says it “does not follow logically” from that page, and its writer, date and recipient are lost. Kirk Watson prints both pages in The Morrisites: Writings and Revelations (2026), pp. 90–91, and the quotations here follow his text. In substance, the fragment says that the Prophet, staying incognito at Cottonwood, told two visitors, a Brother Smith and Niel Beck, their priesthood by laying a hand on their heads (he “felt it Phrenologically”); that birthrights, known for now by inspiration, would in future be something “all will know by science”; that no descendant of Cain would be admitted to the Church; and that about twenty years of preaching would come before Moses and Christ. Kirk Watson brought the page to the guide’s attention.
Who was the Prophet?
The page does not name him. Watson identifies him as George Williams, the Prophet Cainan: the page was filed with the invitation, which USU’s indexes call “Cainan to Scattered Sheep of Joseph”; “Joseph” and the coming of Moses, whom the Morrisites identified with Morris, fit Cainan’s teaching rather than Joseph Smith’s; Cainan called himself a prophet; and Anderson describes him as living in secret. Cainan stayed in the Salt Lake area until 1869 (Anderson, Revisited, p. 168). Watson also notes a vision Cainan is said to have had in Little Cottonwood Canyon, which may or may not bear on the page’s “Cottonwood” (Watson, correspondence with the editor, November 2026).
Who was meant by Cain?
In Brigham Young’s Utah, “seed of Cain” usually meant people of African descent, and Williams had been a Latter-day Saint. But Morris taught that Cain was the son of the serpent, “Lucifer, the devil,” and a fallen angel, that a third of Adam’s children belong to Cain, and that Cain “claims the right to enter into the Church” (The Spirit Prevails, pp. 12–13, 448, 660–61, 664). The page’s promise reads like an answer to that claim, and its appeal to phrenology and “science” fits Morris’s meaning: if spirits return in new bodies, a birthright could be read from the body. Watson favors this reading. The Spirit Prevails also links the seed of Cain with “Gentile blood” (pp. 651–52), so a racial sense in how the teaching was heard cannot be ruled out.
Why it matters
Phrenology was widely popular in the 1840s, and Latter-day Saint leaders were divided over it, but Cainan may be the only Latter Day Saint leader known to have made it part of his religion. If Niel Beck is Niels R. Beck of Deer Lodge, the page is also the earliest known link between him and the Morrisite leadership, years before he sent money to William W. Davies, who testified in 1881 that “Mr. Beck is not a member.”
Where to start
The page and its neighbors in Caine MSS 10, box 3, folder 7 (the fragment and the 1863 invitation filed with it are printed in Kirk Watson’s edition, pp. 90–91), with the collection’s item indexes, which are available from USU Special Collections; Cainan’s letters and revelations in the same collection; Anderson’s chapters on Williams; The Spirit Prevails on Cain; the literature on phrenology among Latter Day Saints (a starting point is Wikipedia’s overview).
Williams’s own words
A letter in Williams’s hand supports the fragment on one point. On October 18, 1876, he wrote to William James that the “common Salvation” had “taken in negroes, Indians and every vile spirit that transgressed the righteous laws in their first estate,” that these “must be ruled over in this & every estate,” and that “our merciful God gave keys of detection, that if possible they may not press into our borders” (Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1876–1877). The letter does not say what the keys were. It does not mention phrenology. A letter of Williams’s of August 28, 1878, also supports the fragment’s setting: it recalls that Beck visited him “at Cottonwood” and left intending to follow him with “two wagons & much cattle” (Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1878–1881). The fragment says that “Niel Beck” visited the Prophet at Cottonwood, which agrees with Watson’s identification of the Prophet as Williams.

How did believers survive failed prophecy?

Open
Why it matters
Morris set days for the Second Coming that passed; he was killed in the siege; Williams told the Montana Saints that the City of the great King would come down in their region, “the Sides of the North”; Davies proclaimed his infant son the Messiah. Yet the movement lasted more than ninety years. Who stayed, who left, and how they explained what happened are open questions.
First findings

The revelations show how the first disappointment was explained. On December 30, 1861, Christ promised to come “to-morrow morning” if those present consecrated all their property (The Spirit Prevails, Nos. 188–189, pp. 338–39). When the morning passed, a revelation of January 6, 1862, blamed those who had consecrated unwillingly; one of January 11 refused to make any further promise about the time; and in February deliverance was promised after the people had taken an oath (Nos. 195, 199 and 224, pp. 350, 359, 419). Three revelations of January 1, 5 and 6, 1862, left out of The Spirit Prevails but kept in manuscript copies, go further: the dated promises “have failed, because they were intended to fail,” for “if I had told them the naked truth, I could not have led them on at all”; Morris himself complains that the people will “deny everything,” and is answered with a threat that many thousands in the territory will die within days (Joseph Morris Papers, P11-1 f1, pp. 250–55, and f2, Nos. 177–178, Community of Christ Library Archives; see the Chronology, 1–6 Jan 1862).

Josiah D. Price dates the consecration to December 30, 1861, and the swearing of the oath to February 12, 1862 (Josiah D. Price journal, 1858–1873, photocopy, P26-22 f1, Community of Christ Library Archives). In June 1862 an angel speaking in tongues told the camp that on his own earth the faithful had also set dates and been disappointed, and that the error lay not in setting them but in refusing to give them up (Watson, pp. 76–77). Williams’s letter of January 6, 1879, set no date at all, but in Montana William James looked in 1880 for the coming of Christ in 1891, “11 years more” (minutes, May 10, 1880, Church History Library, MS 31876, Minutes, 1880).

When 1891 came, President Andrew Hendrikson told the church’s court that they had long been led to expect “that in the present year the ‘winding up scene’ would take place,” and that he still trusted Williams’s promise that he would live to see the Second Coming; John R. Eardley looked to a coming war of the nations and to a new prophet, Mahalaleel, and compared the waiting Saints to the wise and foolish virgins (court minutes, November 10, 1891, Church History Library, MS 31876, Minutes, 1891). See Teachings.

Where to start
The revelations and testimonies of 1862 in Kirk Watson’s edition; Williams’s letters at USU; the Davies material; the literature that began with Festinger, When Prophecy Fails; changes in membership over time (cited, not published as lists).

How did a prophet’s movement become a church run by vote?

Open
Why it matters
Authority passed from Morris’s revelations to Williams’s letters from afar, then to presidents elected at yearly general assemblies (1883, 1886) and to Danish lay leaders who had never been Latter-day Saints. Few movements of the period document that change so closely.
Where to start
Court and Council minutes and the Cainan letters in the Morrisite collection at USU (Caine MSS 10); the Montana minutebook, 1881–1890, and the Cainan letters at the Church History Library (MS 32226 and MS 31876, both online); Anderson’s chapters on the Montana church; the sociology of schism and of the routinization of charisma.
First findings
Williams himself began to spread authority. His letter of April 2, 1877, created “the Court of the Lord’s House,” three officers meeting every May 10, and later also every November 10, “that a portion of the weight of this infant Kingdom may not rest upon one,” with a report to go to him “for approval”; yet the same year he wrote that the next servant “will be chosen of God, not by man” (Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to William James and Montana followers, 1876–1878, and Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1876–1877). After his death the court and the yearly assemblies chose the leaders; in 1887 Hendrikson reminded the church that it was “a trial to some” when Williams wrote that “the People should meet on the anniversary day and elect their President” (Minutes, 1886–1887). In July 1881 the dying Williams appointed George Thompson to act “in his stead,” but Thompson sent the appointment to Montana “for their approval or disapproval” (Thompson to the Montana brethren, August 21, 1881, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1878–1881).

Why were so many Morrisites Scandinavian?

Open
Why it matters
Danish speakers were prominent from the start: Morris’s revelations circulated in Danish, the Omaha and Council Bluffs congregations kept Danish-language records in 1869, and the last Montana leaders were Danish immigrants. Judged by name alone, 168 of the 430 people on the roll have Scandinavian names (Who they were), and in 1889 Joseph Smith III found the old Morrisites at Soda Springs to be mainly Swedes, Norwegians, and Danes (Joseph Smith III and the Restoration, 1952, p. 437). Disappointment after emigration, language, poverty, and patterns of recruitment are all possible explanations.
Where to start
The 1886 roll of membership compared with Scandinavian Mission emigration records; the Danish-language material at USU; emigrant letters and Danish-language newspapers.

What became of the spiritual gifts?

Open
Why it matters
Speaking in tongues, interpreted tongues, and messages from angels were central at Kington Fort, as in the “3rd Captain of the Lord’s Host” testimony of June 8, 1862, given in a tongue through Morris and interpreted by David Richards, and the tongue of March 9, 1862, interpreted by Margaret Cook (The Spirit Prevails, pp. 634–35, 638–42). An interpretation of tongues of June 7, 1862, teaches that the gifts cease only at the resurrection and charges that a council in Brigham Young’s house decided to put them down (Watson, pp. 70–72). How Morrisite practice compares with the Latter-day Saint church in the 1850s has not been studied closely.
First findings
In Montana the gifts lasted at least into the 1880s, but they were brought under the council’s control. On March 26, 1881, the Race Track council examined a member who said that “spirits” influenced him and who had told his dreams in meeting. Rasmus Rasmussen cited Williams’s answer that the Evil One could give an influence that seemed good but was false, and a “key” of Joseph Morris’s for testing dreams. President Andrew Hendrikson ruled that anyone who would speak in tongues in a public meeting was led by the wrong influence, because God’s spirit would assist only in what the meeting was appointed for and would never lead anyone to overstep the order of the church (minutes, March 26, 1881, Church History Library, MS 31876, Minutes, 1881; translation from the Danish generated by Claude). Whether the gifts survived later in the century is not known.
Where to start
Kirk Watson’s edition; The Spirit Prevails; studies of spiritual gifts and the Reformation of 1856–57 in Utah.

Where did the rituals come from?

Open
Why it matters
The scepters, Morris’s robe and crown, the ceremony at Kington Fort in May 1862 that Anderson calls the Foreshadowing Ceremony, the holding of goods in common there, and later communal experiments at Walla Walla and in the Order of Enoch. Their relation to Latter-day Saint temple ritual and to the church’s own communal programs is unexplored.
Where to start
The scepters and their inscriptions (see Repositories); Anderson, Revisited, 116–17; The Spirit Prevails; Davies’s 1881 deposition in Kirk Watson’s edition.
First findings

Josiah D. Price’s journal describes the rites of May 1862 at Kington Fort: the four horsemen ridden by Morris, Cook, Banks and Parson on May 1, the soldier’s oath sworn by the companies on May 26, and the order of the Foreshadowing procession of May 30, with William Harris in the bishop’s robe; it also copies the inscription of Morris’s scepter and an oath sworn by members, who kissed “the written word of the Lord” (Josiah D. Price journal, 1858–1873, photocopy, P26-22 f1, Community of Christ Library Archives; see the Chronology). An onlooker’s letter of June 2, 1862, describes the procession of May 30 (Morris’s seven-crowned hat and seven-braided horse, Cook’s sword, Banks’s balances, Parson’s book); a form for ordaining an “elect lady” with consecrated oil survives from 1861; and copies made on June 19, 1862, of the inscriptions on three rods show them numbered five, six and seven, for Hugh Park, John Eames and Daniel Smith, beside Morris’s own (Brigham Young office files, CR 1234 1, and Historian’s Office collected historical documents, CR 100 397, Church History Library; see Teachings).

Kirk Watson has pointed out a parallel in another dissenter of the period. Francis Gladden Bishop planned a “Great Solemn Assembly,” a minutely ordered ceremony in which he and others would stand in for divine beings, much like the Latter-day Saint idea of the council at Adam-ondi-Ahman; he set it out in his Address to the Sons and Daughters of Zion (Kirtland, 1851) and in The Ensign (Kanesville, 1852). Morris’s camp likewise acted out on earth what his revelations said of heaven: a revelation of March 5, 1862, teaches that earthly things in the priesthood are a pattern of heavenly ones, taking the twenty-four elders of Revelation 4 as its example (The Spirit Prevails, No. 234; a Welsh translation is in Price’s journal), and the four horsemen and the Foreshadowing followed in May. No direct link is known. Watson checked the Morrisite roll against Richard L. Saunders’s lists of Gladdenites and found no names in common, and Saunders’s Salt Lake City lists end in 1854, soon after Morris reached Utah in the fall of 1853. A shared root in the Kirtland solemn assembly and the teaching on Adam-ondi-Ahman seems more likely than borrowing (Kirk Watson, correspondence with the editor, November 2026; Richard L. Saunders, “Francis Gladden Bishop and Gladdenism: A Study in the Culture of a Mormon Dissenter and His Movement,” MA thesis, Utah State University, 1989, appendixes B–C, pp. 245ff.).

In Montana the robe was prescribed by letter. Williams told William James in February 1874 to wear “a linen robe large and broad, reaching down to the feet,” saying that Morris had wanted his ministers so clothed, and in April 1877 sent a pattern for a fuller white robe with an embroidered sun, “in semblance to the Celestial attiring of the Celestial Priesthood.” James wore it to administer the sacrament in 1880 and to preside at the court in 1881. Williams also set out the dedication rite for the Lord’s House (1878) and the blessing of children (1879) (Church History Library, MS 31876, letters to Montana, 1874–1879; see Teachings).

What did Morris teach about marriage, and what did women do in the movement?

Open
Why it matters
Later writers often describe Morris as an opponent of plural marriage; Kirk Watson’s introduction argues that he accepted it as a celestial principle. Women’s part in the gifts, in testimony, and in the community has hardly been studied.
First findings

Morris’s own article on celestial marriage, dated June 4, 1861, falls between the two readings: the biblical law of many wives and concubines was given for this life, “not being a celestial law,” and kings and prophets who had many wives “can have but one sealed to them each for eternity” (The Spirit Prevails, pp. 651–52). An angel’s testimony through Morris in June 1862 calls polygamy true “in its proper time and place” (Watson, p. 78), and Morris did not authorize it at Kington Fort, treating it as belonging to a future time (Watson, p. 8 n. 8). Some members came into the movement with plural households formed as Latter-day Saints: Moses Byrne, one of the six baptized on April 6, 1861, though soon “cast off,” joined with both his wives, Catherine Cardon (married 1854) and Anne Beus (married 1857), who are on the roll as Catherine and Ann Bryne and kept neighbouring households in the 1860 census; and South Weber tradition calls James Laird “a polygamist and Morrisite.” No source yet shows how Morris treated such households.

Morris’s successor George Williams departed from his rule of one wife for eternity: while saying he had “never taught the doctrine or counselled the practice,” he kept the household he had formed as a Latter-day Saint, with Susannah and Louisa, and in 1871 taught that a high priest takes “one Daughter of each God” in the eternities (see George Williams). By 1899 the movement’s own history in Gems of Inspiration condemned polygamy outright, citing the Book of Mormon and the 1890 Manifesto, which may be where the later tradition began. Morris’s marriage rite of August 23, 1861, distinguishes two covenants. Ordinary couples, before a high priest, each give “my hand and heart in a marriage covenant for time”; the bride alone vows to keep herself “from all others,” while the groom pledges to be “a true and faithful husband” (The Spirit Prevails, No. 89, pp. 129–30; a manuscript copy of the bride’s part is in the Community of Christ Library Archives, P11-1 f6). The prophet and prophetess alone were sealed “for time and all eternity” (No. 33, January 7, 1861, pp. 44–45). See Teachings.

In Montana, Williams had a woman ordained to the priesthood. In January 1877 he wrote that “a Sister be ordained special for your Stake as High Priestess” to “minister to the Sisterhood in all things for their good, temporal & spiritual”; Sister Rasmussen was to take “a vow” and be ordained by Hendrikson and his counselor “in the pattern shown,” and Williams planned “a quorum of three” sisters (Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1876–1877, and Letters from Cainan to William James and Montana followers, 1876–1878). She had died by March 1881, when the Race Track council noted that “no one was appointed in her place” and Hendrikson said he had had no answer from Williams about it (Church History Library, MS 31876, Minutes, 1881; translation from the Danish generated by Claude). In 1882 Thompson asked the church to fill “the place of Sister Rasmuson”; women and girls voted in the election of 1886 (Minutes, 1886–1887).

Where to start
The Spirit Prevails; Kirk Watson’s introduction and sources; Emma Thompson Just’s letters in Agnes Just Reid, Letters of Long Ago; the Anderson plaque and other family accounts.

Did the Morrisites ordain women?

Open
Short answer
Yes. Joseph Morris had a form for ordaining an “elect lady” in 1861, and the churches that followed George Williams ordained women as priestesses and High Priestesses from 1869 and ordained two girls “to the lesser priesthood” in Montana in 1890. What the women’s offices allowed them to do beyond leading the sisters is not yet clear.
Why it matters
Women’s offices in Morris’s and Williams’s churches have hardly been studied, and the records that show them, most of them in the Church History Library and some in Danish, have only lately been read. They show a small church on the frontier giving women titles, ordinations and duties of their own over thirty years.
First findings

Morris, 1861. A form “for ordaining an elect lady,” signed by Morris and reported to Brigham Young’s office in September 1861, anoints the woman with consecrated oil “that thou mayest be an heiress of the Priesthood of Melchisedec” (report by Judson Stoddard, “Morrisite papers, 1861–1862,” Brigham Young office files, CR 1234 1, Church History Library). The title comes from Joseph Smith’s revelation to Emma Smith, “thou art an elect lady” (Doctrine and Covenants 25:3). After Morris’s death Williams held the subject back at first. In April 1866 he told John R. Eardley that an account written for the sisters on “their position in the Priesthood — their throne of authority — their equal privileges — their voice in their own assemblies” was to wait, for “a positive word cometh, ‘not yet’” (letter copy book, circa 1866–1877, MS 32226), and in August that “the female Priesthood likewise is in advance of this present time” (MS 31876).

Council Bluffs and Omaha, 1869–71. At Council Bluffs in 1869 the new congregation ordained women with the men. Petronella Borgström, baptized on May 28, was confirmed and ordained a priestess “after Abel’s order,” and Nels Damander and his wife, baptized on July 11, were ordained to Abel’s order together (Member records, circa 1869–1890, MS 31876; translation from the Danish generated by Claude). “Abel’s Priesthood” was Williams’s name for the lesser priesthood. At Omaha, Marie Mortensen’s written ordination, in English and Danish, sets her apart with the birthright of Esther “to preside over the Sisters in Nebraska to administer in holy things.” In 1870 Williams called her “High Priestess” and told her that if she could not attend she should lay her hands on another sister and appoint her “to officiate as High Priestess in her absence” (Letters from Cainan to Andrew Henrikson, 1870, MS 31876). When she joined the Reorganized Church in May 1871, Williams told the council to let “her place & office be given to another” (see the RLDS question).

England and Montana, 1872–82. On September 22, 1872, when the Montana Saints met at William P. Thomas’s house on Warm Spring Creek to organize their church, Margaret Thomas was reordained “as the Lady elect to preside over the Female Order” (minutebook, September 1872–January 1874, MS 32226). In England, Theodora, who wrote letters for Williams, signed herself “High Priestess in England” in 1874, and that year Williams wrote of celestial names given to women as well as men in an “order of Birthright.” In January 1877 he asked that “a Sister be ordained special for your Stake as High Priestess” to “minister to the Sisterhood in all things for their good, temporal & spiritual.” Sister Rasmussen was to take “a vow” and be ordained by Andrew Hendrikson and his counselor, and Williams planned “a quorum of three” sisters (Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1876–1877, MS 31876). She had died by March 1881, when the council noted that no one had been appointed in her place, and in 1882 George Thompson asked the church to fill her place (Minutes, 1881; see marriage and women).

The second generation, 1886–90. Women and girls voted in the Montana church’s election of 1886 (Minutes, 1886–1887, MS 31876). On September 6, 1890, after baptisms on August 30, Eardley and Hendrikson confirmed a group of the members’ children, and the record notes that two girls, Anna Johnson and Mary Ann L. Beck, were ordained “to the lesser priesthood” (Member records, circa 1869–1890, MS 31876). Williams had told the Montana church in 1876 to baptize new members “to the lesser priesthood”; the 1890 record does not say whether the other children, boys among them, were ordained too.

Still open
What a priestess or High Priestess could do outside the sisters’ meetings; whether the quorum of three sisters was ever formed; whether the women’s offices lasted after 1890; where Morris’s form came from, and whether it had any link to the Nauvoo Relief Society; and whether women held offices in William W. Davies’s community or the Doves’ congregation in California.
Where to start
The glossary entry; the Teachings page on Williams; the Chronology for 1869 and 1890; CR 1234 1; the member records, minutes and letters from Cainan in MS 31876; and the minutebook and letter copy book in MS 32226 (Church History Library; see Repositories).

How has the Morrisite War been remembered?

Open
Why it matters
The descendants’ bronze plaque at Soda Springs, the 2006 monument at South Weber, folk traditions such as the street burial, and the church’s 1903 reply to the Andersons, which blamed the Morrisites, each tell a different story of the same three days.
The 2006 monument
The inscription on the Kington Fort–Morrisite War Site monument (Sons of Utah Pioneers marker no. 128) is the version most visitors read. It calls the fort “a 645 foot by 645 foot enclosure,” puts the population at over 200, says that a cannon ball “killed two women and seriously wounded a teenage girl,” that “as the army assaulted the fort and breached the gates, two militiamen were killed,” and that “in the ensuing confusion, Morris, his second in command, John Banks and two more women were killed. In all, eleven people died” (inscription as photographed in December 2008, MO-IMG-001). The eyewitness accounts on the Morrisite War page tell it differently: the two posse men died during the siege, and Morris was shot after the surrender, not in confusion during an assault. The monument’s eleven includes the two posse men; the Morrisite letters of 1891 count eleven Morrisites. The 645-foot figure has not been found in an earlier source. New

How has the Morrisite story been passed down, and where do the common errors come from?

Open
Why it matters
The same errors turn up again and again (see Common errors in print), and tracing them shows how the story has moved. Some begin in the press of 1862, when one telegraphic dispatch was copied across the country and changed as it went. Some come from the partisan retellings of the 1880s and 1890s, on both sides. Some come from family memory, which is often right about people and places and less sure about dates: Eliza Eardley Hardin remembered Willow Glen’s founding in 1881, the newspapers first mention it in 1882. And some come from scholarship itself, as later works summarized earlier ones without returning to the records; even the federal gazetteer places the Willow Glen post office about a mile from where the 1882 paper put it. Today the story also travels by online video, where short pieces often borrow one another’s descriptions; their errors are those listed in Common errors, above all the claim that Morris broke away over polygamy. Who told the story, to whom, and why are questions for the history of memory as well as of the movement.
Where to start
The Newspapers page, which follows the 1862 dispatch through its reprints; the 1891 Salt Lake Tribune letters and Richard W. Young’s “The Morrisite War” in the Contributor (1889–90); the theses and their sources (Bibliography); oral histories such as the 1962 Hendrickson interview (Repositories); the Hardin reminiscence (SC 788, Montana Historical Society Library & Archives; see Repositories); C. LeRoy Anderson’s notes at USU, which show what he had in hand.

How did Latter-day Saints, the Reorganized Church and the Morrisites live alongside one another in the Deer Lodge Valley?

Open
Why it matters
For nearly a century three churches that traced themselves to Joseph Smith shared one valley. Latter-day Saint missionaries knew the Morrisite leaders well enough in 1896 to call John R. Eardley “Apostle Eardly” (Wright, 2004, drawing on the Butte District manuscript history). The Reorganized Church was active in Montana from 1868, and some Morrisites joined it, among them Lena Hanson at Deer Lodge in 1895. Hymnals and a 1931 Reorganized Church lesson quarterly were still in the Lord’s House in 2020 (see Images). Whether the three groups competed for members, shared buildings, preachers or burial grounds, married into one another’s families, or simply kept apart, and how that changed as the Morrisite church dwindled toward 1969, has not been studied.
First findings

The Montana council’s minutes for 1881–1890 (Church History Library, MS 32226) give some early answers. In November 1886 an “Elder Clapp of the Reorganized Church” invited the Morrisites to use the Reorganized Church’s meeting house in Deer Lodge City while he was away preaching in Utah, and the council agreed to accept and to announce the meeting “among the Josephites and Morrisites.” This was the church the New North-West had reported in September 1886 as going up under Clapp’s direction on the Cottonwood road east of town (see Newspapers). The Reorganized Church branch remembered that building as its first church, “on Cottonwood Creek,” built under John Eliasson of a Morrisite family and later used as his blacksmith shop; its second church, begun about 1892 with Andrew Christofferson, another Morrisite son, on the building committee, was dedicated in 1901 and still stands (Places).

A year earlier the council had placed a notice in the same paper disowning George and James Dove of the California congregation as “pretenders” (November 13, 1885). In 1888 and 1889 the council discussed John Parson, the former Morrisite apostle, who still held Reorganized Church membership while following a self-styled prophet in Oakland, California, and took it as a sign of waning confidence in “Young Joseph’s prophetic office.” Speakers cited Brigham Young both as a cautionary example and as an authority on running a branch, and in 1889 said Williams’s predictions about “the Utah people” were being fulfilled.

The council also kept the sacrament open to non-members who came as “friends for the gospel’s sake.” Among Williams’s own followers, the earliest case found so far is fifteen years older and comes from the Omaha congregation: in May 1871 Sister Marie Mortensen, who had led its sisters’ meetings and whom Williams had called “High Priestess” in 1870, “had joined another church, the Josephites, and let herself be baptized by them.” She told the elders she was afraid “young Joseph was the man they said he was,” and chose to risk rejecting Cainan rather than rejecting young Joseph; she gave back her written appointment and was excluded (Omaha minutebook, May 7, 1871, MS 31876; translation from the Danish generated by Claude). Williams approved: “you have done quite right, particularly in obtaining the papers; let her place & office be given to another” (Williams to Hendrikson, June 2, 1871, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Andrew Henrikson, 1871).

George Thompson, the newly elected president, wrote from England in December 1886 that Joseph Smith [III] “may be called through our Joseph,” and that John Parson “may be instrumental” in telling him of “the mission of the fullness,” but that he had “not yet called him” to be a prophet (Church History Library, MS 31876). In 1887–89 Eardley and Emmons were also in touch with David Whitmer and his followers, and read their papers, the Expositor and The Return (Eardley to Hendrikson, 1887–89, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to Andrew Hendrickson, 1880–1889). In March 1890 John R. Eardley wrote that Brother Emmons of the Montana church had been baptized into the Reorganized Church and that Mrs. Emmons meant to follow at Elder Clapp’s next visit; “it was only a matter of time,” he wrote, and “our numbers are growing less” (Eardley to Hendrikson, March 20, 1890, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to Andrew Hendrickson, 1890).

Where to start
The Butte District manuscript history of the Northwestern States Mission (Church History Library); Cornelius, “Mormonism in Montana” (1962), ch. 6, and his interviews; Wright, “Mormon Movement to Montana” (2004); RLDS branch records and Saints’ Herald reports from Montana (Community of Christ Library Archives); the New North-West and Silver State Post; the 1962 Hendrickson interview (Repositories); burials at Hillcrest Cemetery, Deer Lodge; and family memories, which descendants are invited to share.

Where did the Copper Village organ come from?

Open
Why it matters

The Copper Village Museum and Art Center in Anaconda holds a Mason & Hamlin reed (pump) organ said to have come from the Morrisites, and by local tradition from the Lord’s House at Racetrack. If the tradition holds, it is one of the few furnishings of Morrisite worship to survive outside the church. Jim Furshong, a volunteer at the museum, brought the organ to this guide’s attention and showed it to the editor on November 18, 2026 (MO-IMG-102 to 104). It is a walnut parlor organ with a mirrored, spindled top, thirteen stops, two knee levers (a knee swell and a “full organ” lever) and two carpeted pedals. The nameboard prints Mason & Hamlin’s exhibition medals, the latest of them London, 1885, so the organ was made in 1885 or later; the knee lever marked “Patented Full Organ, June 25, 1878” gives the date of that device’s patent, not the year the organ was built.

That fits a visiting organist’s description of it, when he played it at the museum on August 3, 2013, as a Mason & Hamlin parlor organ built in Boston about 1892 (“BIRD: Auf dem Lande, on Mason & Hamlin parlor organ,” YouTube, posted August 9, 2013). Either way, the organ did not come from Utah and was not bought when the Lord’s House was built and dedicated in 1878–79; it could still have been bought for the church, or by a Morrisite family, later, while the congregation met there into the 1950s. Museum volunteers say an accession sheet was probably made when the organ was donated but has not been found: for years donations to the museum and archives went undocumented, and volunteers are only now putting the collections in order.

Where to start
The museum’s accession files (Marcus Daly Historical Society Museum and Archives, through the director of the Copper Village Museum and Art Center; see Repositories); the organ’s serial number, which can date it from Mason & Hamlin’s production records; the Montana council’s minutes (Church History Library, MS 32226) and later church records, for any purchase or gift of an organ; and family memories, which descendants are invited to share.

Was Gwendale named for Gwenllian Evans?

Open
Why it matters
Gwendale was the post office of the upper Deer Lodge Valley, near Warm Springs and Lost Creek, where Morrisite settlers such as W. P. Thomas lived; in 1878 it was renamed Morristown because, the New North-West said, many of its people were Morrisites (see Deer Lodge Valley). The Evans family called its homestead Gwendale Farm (In the Shadow of Mount Haggin, p. 134). The Montana Historical Society’s Women’s History Matters calls Gwenllian Evans, Morgan Evans’s mother, Montana’s first woman homesteader and “one of the territory’s first post mistresses” (“A Farm of Her Own,” May 20, 2014). A family account names the farm for her. In a 1982 interview, her great-grandson George P. Wellcome Jr., who drew on family notes kept by his mother, said that Morgan Evans built an estate near the family homestead in 1875 and named it Gwendale Farms “in honor of his wife’s mother.” The article makes Gwenllian the mother of Morgan’s wife, Ann, but she was Morgan’s own mother, so on this account the farm was named for her (Ed Kemmick, “George Wellcome Has ‘Copper Royalty’ in His Past,” Montana Standard, February 1, 1982, p. 5; clipping shared by Jim Furshong, who noticed the slip). The same article says that Granville Stuart’s Forty Years on the Frontier (1925) credits her as the first woman in the state to file a homestead claim; that passage has not yet been checked. No record seen so far says that the post office took its name from the farm, or that she kept the Gwendale office.
Where to start
The Post Office Department’s Record of Appointment of Postmasters, 1832–1971 (National Archives, Record Group 28; microfilm M841), for Deer Lodge County; the department’s site reports for Gwendale and Morristown; the New North-West; and the sources behind the Women’s History Matters essay.

Were the Evans and Thomas families of Gwendale connected to the Morrisites?

Open
Why it matters

Morgan Evans and William P. Thomas were Gwendale election judges together in 1876, and in 1880 their homestead claims were among the four held up by the land office (see the roll note for Thomas, W. P., probably the Morrisite of that name). The Evanses were Welsh, like William James, the Morrisites’ local president. The family account has them in Utah for about eight years before they came to the valley in 1865 (In the Shadow of Mount Haggin, p. 134), but Women’s History Matters says Gwenllian Evans emigrated to the United States in 1868, and her obituary, a clipping shared by Jim Furshong whose newspaper and date are not yet recorded here, says she came to Montana in 1869. Neither family history calls the Evanses Morrisites. The same obituary says “the late Mrs. E. P. Thomas” was her daughter. A family chart shared by a reader names her as Gwenllian Evans (1847–1891), born at Penderyn, Breconshire, Morgan Evans’s sister, and her husband as Evan Price Thomas (1845–1891), born in Carmarthenshire, a rancher on Lost Creek; the two were killed by lightning in July 1891 (Find a Grave memorial 106925533).

The chart and Find a Grave both make him the son of William Price Thomas, and a 1945 account of the valley’s first settlers says that William Price Thomas was already settled on Warm Springs Creek when Morgan Evans’s party came in 1865 (Montana Standard, September 23, 1945). This is very probably the Morrisite W. P. Thomas, who was also born in Carmarthenshire (patriarchal blessing, MS 31876, Church History Library; see his note on the roll). The chart records a second marriage between the families: Morgan Evans’s daughter Rachel (1864–1951) married Joseph Henry Thomas (1863–1894), also a son of William Price Thomas, at Anaconda on September 18, 1890. Two of W. P. Thomas’s sons thus married into the Evans family. That ties the families closely, but it does not show that the Evanses were Morrisites.

Where to start
The Evans and Thomas homestead case files (National Archives, Record Group 49); the 1870 and 1880 censuses of Deer Lodge County; the Montana church’s Danish minutes and council minutes (Church History Library, MS 31876 and MS 32226), for the names Evans and Thomas; Latter-day Saint records of the Evanses in Utah before 1865; and family records, which descendants are invited to share.

Who was “Brother Warner”?

Answered
Why it matters

In January 1875 George Thompson wrote from Walthamstow to William P. Thomas in Montana of a “Brother Warner” who claimed “to have seen Bro Joseph” [Morris] and to be sent to warn the brethren to gather to Weber, for “the Shepherds of Utah will be cut off in 1875 & the Saints be gathered to Weber in 1876”; Thompson was “looking for him & at Weber.” Williams had already warned the Race Track church in February 1875 against “one who is going round the Weber House saying he is commissioned by Seth to warn that people to gather back to Weber this year … I have not sent him” (Williams to Andrew Hendrikson, February 5, 1875, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Montana followers, 1874–1875), and he rejected the prophecy in May 1875, and in June 1876 Thompson wrote that “some Jew or fanatic was bidding for your loyalty” (Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters from Cainan to Margaret and William P. Thomas, 1866–1882). He would be a claimant who called the scattered Morrisites back to the site of the camp a dozen years after the siege. He was almost certainly Joseph Warner of South Weber.

In November 1875 John R. Eardley wrote from Hollister, California, that “Bro. Warner—the German brother, has been through this country warning the Weber Saints to prepare for the coming of Bro. Joseph next June,” claiming “the Presidency of the Apostleship” and to be “President of Germany”; Eardley thought these “the aspirations of a diseased mind” and said Warner had “disgusted all whom I have conversed with” (John R. Eardley to William James, Hollister, California, November 9, 1875, Church History Library, MS 31876, Letters to William James, 1874–1875). The roll lists a Joseph Warner and an Ingray Werner. A Joseph Warner, born in Germany, aged 45, was among the men discharged at the fort on June 16, 1862 (“Morrisite prisoners, 1862 June,” Historian’s Office collected historical documents, CR 100 397, Church History Library); Anderson names a Joseph Warner among those who gained wider prominence in the scattered congregations (pp. 157, 181); Family Tree has a Joseph Warner, born in Hanover in 1816, in Cache County in 1860 (FamilySearch Family Tree, GK43-2XF); and in 1880 a Joseph Warner, sixty-two, born in Germany, widowed, a laborer, was living at South Weber (1880 U.S. census, South Weber, Davis County, ED 16).

Testifying at Burton’s trial in February 1879, Joseph Warner said: “I was born in Germany; came here in ’56; came to Salt Lake first; I was in Providence and there I had a vision; Joseph Smith appeared to me; for which I was cut off of the Mormon church,” and “I joined Joseph Morris in May, 1861, at Weber.” He read the court a sermon “half in German and half in broken English” and recited a revelation in full, until, the reporter wrote, it proved “utterly impossible to obtain intelligent responses” and he was allowed to stop (Ogden Junction, February 22, 1879, p. 4).

He died at South Weber in 1893, probably in September and before October 2 (the county court’s session of September 11 does not mention him), “an indigent person” who “left no relatives”; P. P. Prophet “and others” buried him at county expense and sold his effects, but “the manuscript he had not disposed of,” and on October 30 he was told to dispose of it “if he can” (Davis County Court minutes, October 2 and October 30, 1893, pp. 462, 470 (County court minutes, 1852–1893, FamilySearch film 484601, item 2, DGS 7579269); Davis County Clipper, November 2, 1893, p. 1). What became of the manuscript is not known. The marker in the South Weber Pioneer Cemetery reads “Joseph Werner, ‘Morrisite,’ about 1870” (MO-IMG-021); the marker was probably set long after the burial (an Army Reserve unit restored the cemetery in 1973–75, according to the USGenWeb Archives page for it), and its date is an estimate; Find a Grave’s “1870” comes from a Utah State Historical Society listing of 2000 that most likely copied the marker (memorial 120420). A German-born Morrisite at Weber who had claimed a vision of Joseph Smith, still reading his own sermons and revelations in 1879, fits Thompson’s and Eardley’s words closely; what remains open is the fate of the manuscript he left in 1893.

Where to start
The Montana council minutes of 1874–76 and Williams’s letters of 1875 to William James and Andrew Hendrickson (Church History Library, MS 31876), for the name; Utah newspapers of 1874–76, for a prophet at Weber; the Davis County Court minutes from November 1893 on (County court minutes, 1893–1900, FamilySearch film 484602), for a later report on Warner’s manuscript, which may hold his revelations; who P. P. Prophet was, and his family’s papers; Thomas Peek’s manuscript “Early Settlement of South Weber” and his diary, owned in 1969 by Richard O. Tucker, which may mention him (the city’s cemetery records, read in November 2026, give no date for him; see Kington Fort); his excommunication at Providence, Cache County, in the ward records; and the 1870 census of Davis and Weber counties.

When was Arthur Davies first called the “Walla Walla Jesus”?

Answered
Answer
In his lifetime, by his neighbours. When the boy died of diphtheria in February 1880, the Walla Walla Watchman wrote: “This boy, a little over twelve years of age, was no other than the Walla Walla Jesus Christ, so called, and in whom the camp of Davies, at the foot of the Blue Mountains, had implicit faith. Great wonders were expected of this little Messiah, but alas! man proposes and God disposes” (Daily Intelligencer (Seattle), March 4, 1880, p. 3, reprinting the Walla Walla Watchman; Washington Digital Newspapers; checked against the page image). “So called” shows the name was already current. The Watchman itself, which is not digitized, may have used it earlier.
Earlier mentions, without the name

J. H. Beadle in 1870, of “another prophet named Davis” whose followers kept a child “dressed in white” as their future Christ (Life in Utah, pp. 427–28); the Carson Valley News in 1876 and 1877 (see Newspapers); the New York Sun of November 14, 1878, p. 1 (“Mr. Wood of Walla-Walla”; Chronicling America; checked against the page image), in which Davies’s missionary S. W. Wood called Davies “the Comforter” and his society the “Redemptionists,” and said the Messiah, “the Father’s Son,” “was born in 1868 in Walla Walla … He is now 10 years old” (consistent with his birth on February 11, 1868, given by Gilbert, and with the Watchman’s “a little over twelve” in February 1880; summarized in the Puget Sound Argus, December 12, 1878, p. 2); and the Watchman’s exposé of August 1879, as summarized in the Argus (August 28, 1879, p. 4: “a child that is the Savior of the world”) and the Spokan Times (September 4, 1879: “the father of Christ … the Savior of the world has made his second advent in the person of his twelve-year old son”). Davies himself called the boy “Messiah Son of David” (Watson, p. 192), as did Frank T. Gilbert’s county history of 1882 (Historic Sketches of Walla Walla, pp. 374–75), whose plate facing p. 369 shows Davies and the two boys above a view of the house, captioned “Birth and Burial Place of the Messiah” (MO-IMG-114).

Zenas H. Gurley Jr., an apostle of the Reorganized Church, writing from Malad City, Idaho, on October 2, 1878, six weeks before Wood’s visit to the Sun, spoke of “the young ‘Jesus’” of the “Davisites” in the valley of Walla Walla and put him down to belief in transmigration (Saints’ Herald 26, January 15, 1879, p. 23; checked against the page image; see the reincarnation question).
