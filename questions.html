Open questions

Questions for research

Questions the guide has not been able to settle, for students, local historians, and other researchers. Questions of record can be advanced with records that are digitized or held in Utah, Idaho, Montana, or California. Questions of interpretation ask what the movement means for the history of religion; they suit theses and articles.

If you take one up, or can answer one, write to contact@morrisite.org. Answers will be credited here. The edition of Forscutt’s “Sketch of Joseph Morris” is in progress by the editor.

Questions of record

Where is Joseph Morris buried?

Open
Known
The Salt Lake City Cemetery sexton’s record lists Morris under June 15, 1862, as no. 1469; the entry just before his, no. 1468, is Peter Wahler of the posse, written “Walden” (see “Did one posse member die, or two?” below). Morris lies in the pauper ground, in an unmarked grave. A tradition holds that he was buried beneath a street so that he would be trodden underfoot; no published source for it has been found.
Where to look
Salt Lake City Cemetery office (plat maps of the pauper sections); sexton’s records on FamilySearch; Salt Lake newspapers for June 1862 in Utah Digital Newspapers; where the tradition first appears in print or family accounts.

Did Brigham Young ever answer Morris?

Open
Known
About a dozen of Morris’s letters survive in Young’s office files (CR 1234 1). Anderson found no reply, and reports a penciled note on the first letter, “He’s weak minded.” Two more Morris texts, revelations dated January 14 and 16, 1860, were found in 2026 filed as anonymous letters; a clerk docketed them “Balderdash” and received them January 20, 1860, and the first charges Young with turning the Lord’s servant away from his door (Documents). That docket is so far the only recorded reaction from the office. Kirk Watson’s edition transcribes twelve of the letters, October 11, 1858, to November 26, 1860, with catalog links (dated list on the Documents page); others may be catalogued as anonymous.
Where to look
Young’s letterpress copybooks and office journals, 1857–1861, in the same collection; the Journal of Wilford Woodruff for February 1861; the anonymous letters in the same files.

What did Joseph F. Smith write to Niels Anderson?

Open
Known
In 1903 Niels Anderson asked the church to help his wife, Mary Christoffersen Anderson, whose jaw was shot away in 1862. His letters survive (CR 1 191, digitized), and they answer a letter from Smith dated February 25, 1903, which is not in the file. Whether any help followed is unknown.
Where to look
Joseph F. Smith and First Presidency letterpress copybooks for 1903; Bannock Stake and Soda Springs ward records.

Who were the Morrisites?

In progress
Known
The roll of membership printed in 1886 lists 430 people baptized at South Weber in 1861–62. Since November 2026 this guide has linked 178 of them to FamilySearch Family Tree, marked 81 men named among the prisoners of June 1862, recorded the later path of 59, grouped families, and begun pages on individual members (for example John Parson and Erik Eliasson). Counts of sex, origin, age and birthplace are on Who they were. Most members are still known only by name, and the identifications marked probable need checking. For what they believed, see Teachings.
Where to look
The roll; Slaterville and other ward records; the 1860 and 1870 censuses; Soda Springs, Carson Valley, and Deer Lodge records; Black, Early Members of the Reorganized Church; descendants’ family records. Findings are added to the roll and person pages with their sources; living people are excluded.

Who died at Kington Fort?

Open
Known
Accounts differ on the number of dead and on some of their names and dates. The two posse deaths are now settled (Jared Smith and Peter Wahler; see below). On the Morrisite side, Morris was killed at the surrender and John Banks died in custody that evening; Forscutt saw two women dead from the first cannon shot on June 13, and the Anderson gravestone plaque says that Mrs. J. L. Bear (Anne Bar on the roll) and her baby were killed by another cannon ball. Anderson, however, says that two women were killed while holding an infant and that in neither case was the child hurt (Revisited, p. 169 n. 6), so whether the Bears’ baby died is not settled. Isabella Bowman, wife of James Bowman, was one of two Morrisite women killed during the surrender (The Spirit Prevails names the other “Mrs. O’Hagg,” p. 8, and Dow’s affidavit calls her a Danish woman; she may be the roll’s Ann Hegg, wife of Svend Hegg, whom Family Tree gives as dying in 1862, but that is not checked); Robert T. Burton was indicted for her death in 1870 and again in 1877, tried in 1879 and acquitted (Anderson, Revisited, pp. 148–49). The names of the other dead, and the date of each death, have not been checked against one another. No list checked against all the sources exists.
Where to look
Contemporary newspapers; Woodruff’s journal; the Journal History; Forscutt’s account; the Anderson gravestone; the Salt Lake City Cemetery sexton’s record, which has Morris and Wahler as nos. 1469 and 1468; Weber County burial records; the Deseret News of June 18, 1862 (Documents); testimony from the 1879 trial of Robert T. Burton.

What became of the California congregation?

Open
Known
George Dove, who with his father James had followed George Williams and then William W. Davies, began receiving communications in 1873 and organized a Church of the Firstborn of about thirty people (Kirk Watson, ed., The Morrisites: Writings and Revelations, 2026, pp. 198–99). The Doves led the church in San Francisco after 1882 and published its books into the 1890s. How and when the congregation ended is not known.
Where to look
San Francisco directories and newspapers in the California Digital Newspaper Collection; probate and cemetery records for the Dove family; the imprints of later Morrisite publications.

What do the Danish revelations say?

Open
Known
The USU collection holds a bound volume of Morris’s revelations in Danish that does not seem to exist anywhere else. It has not been translated or compared with The Spirit Prevails.
Where to look
USU Special Collections, Caine MSS 10. A reader of nineteenth-century Danish handwriting is needed.

Where are the records of the 1863 trials?

In progress
Known
At the March 1863 term, ten Morrisites were indicted for killing the two posse men: seven were convicted of second-degree murder, two were acquitted, and one case was dropped (nolle prosequi). One man was sentenced to fifteen years, one to twelve, and five to ten. Sixty-six others were fined $100 for resisting the posse (Fanny Stenhouse, An Englishwoman in Utah, 1880; Bancroft, History of Utah, ch. 22). Governor Harding pardoned them all a few days later; Anderson (Revisited, p. 145) says the proclamation named the convicted men, and Eardley says John Parson carried the pardon to the penitentiary (Gems, p. 31). The Deseret News of June 25, 1862, names everyone brought before Judge Kinney after the siege: Richard Cook and Peter Klemgaard, Christian Neilson, and ninety-five others held to bail (Documents; the men are marked on the roll). The court’s own report, in the Deseret News of April 1, 1863, names them (Documents): Peter Klemgard (fifteen years), Christian Neilsen (twelve), and Abraham Taylor, Kadrup Neilsen, Jens Christiansen, Andrew Lee and Andrew M. Mason (ten each); John Parsons and John E. Jones were acquitted, and Richard Cook’s case was dropped. The Spirit Prevails and Gems give a different seven, with John Nielson, George Lee and John E. Jones in place of Kadrup Neilsen, Andrew Lee and Andrew M. Mason. The same report names eighty-five men tried for resisting an officer. Still open: who “Kadrup Neilsen” was (he is not on the roll), why the Morrisite accounts differ, and the court’s own case files.
Where to look
The Gordon M. Howard papers at the Utah State Historical Society (Mss B 279), box 2: People v. Peter Klingard, 1863 (folder 5), and a register of arrests of Morrisites, 1863 (folder 6). Utah territorial court case files at the Utah State Archives; Harding’s proclamation and pardon records, in the territorial executive records or the Deseret News of early April 1863.

Was Lot Smith at the siege?

Open
The question
Writing from Loughborough on April 12, 1873, George Williams warned the Montana church against spirits “more wicked than Burton and Lot Smith (the murderers at Weber)” (Church History Library, MS 31876). Robert T. Burton led the posse. Lot Smith, the Utah militia officer known for his raids on the army’s supply trains in 1857, is not named in the accounts of the siege used by this guide.
Known
Only Williams’s charge, made eleven years after the siege. It has not been checked against the posse rolls, Burton’s report or Lot Smith’s own papers.
Where to start
The militia records and Burton’s report of June 1862 (Brigham Young office files, Church History Library, CR 1234 1); the territorial militia papers at the Utah State Archives; biographies and papers of Lot Smith; Morrisite accounts that name members of the posse.

Did one posse member die, or two?

Answered
Answer
Two. Jared Smith, aged nineteen, was shot on June 13, 1862, the first day of the siege. Peter Wahler, about twenty-four, was shot through the head on June 15, the day of the surrender. Wilford Woodruff’s journal for June 15 names him as “Peter Wahlen,” and the Deseret News of June 18 reports the deaths of “Smith and Wahler.” According to the transcription in his FamilySearch record, Robert T. Burton’s June 1862 report to Brigham Young (Brigham Young office files, Church History Library, CR 1234 1) sent home “the remains of Peter Wahler, 24 years old,” of the 1st Battalion, 3rd Regiment of Infantry, and said Burton could find no relatives of his in the territory. He was buried in the Salt Lake City Cemetery, where the sexton recorded the name as “Walden” (FamilySearch GBWC-GY9). In March 1863, ten Morrisites were indicted for killing the two men (Fanny Stenhouse, An Englishwoman in Utah, 1880).
Why it was missed
Smith was a local young man whose funeral Brigham Young preached, and most later accounts, including Wikipedia, remember only him. Anderson does name the second man, as John Peter Wahlin, killed as a party under Lieutenant James Lewis broke into a cabin on the west side of the fort (Revisited, p. 136), but did not connect him with Wahler. Wahler apparently had no family in Utah, and his name was spelled several ways. The “John Wahlin” of one later local history is probably the same man.
Still open
Where Wahler came from, and anything else about his life.

Who were the men held after the siege who are not on the roll?

Open
Known
Of the ninety-eight men the Deseret News of June 25, 1862, names as brought before Judge Kinney, eighty-one match names on the 1861–62 roll, eight are uncertain matches, and nine are not on the roll at all: L. Parr, S. Williamsen, J. P. Sorensen, H. Okensen, S. P. Rasmusson, J. E. Reese, J. C. Edwards, Saml. Hulse, and Peter Moffat. Three names first counted here turned out to be on the roll under other spellings, found through the emigrant company rosters of the Church History Biographical Database: J. G. Lousley is Loasle, John C. (Hans Kasper Loosli), John Trehan is Trahern, John, and Wm. Rogers is one of the roll’s two William Rodgers. Family Tree searches in November 2026 found no Morrisite tie for the other nine. They may have joined after the roll was drawn up, been in the fort without joining, or be misprinted.
Where to look
The register of arrests of Morrisites, 1863, in the Gordon M. Howard papers (Mss B 279, box 2, folder 6), Utah State Historical Society; the 1860 census for Weber and Davis counties; ward records for South Weber and Slaterville; Val Holley, “Slouching Towards Slaterville” (2008), which names Slaterville and Marriott men among the prisoners.

Are the January 1860 revelations in Morris’s hand?

Answered (probably)
Known
The two revelations of January 14 and 16, 1860, are unsigned except as from “Jesus Christ,” name “my servant Joseph,” and share wording with Nos. 18 and 185 of The Spirit Prevails (Documents). Kirk Watson has compared the hand with a revelation of October 11, 1858, filed among the “Revelations of Joseph Morris, 1858–1860” in Brigham Young’s office files, and identifies it as Morris’s; the dating formula, letter forms and spelling agree (Documents). A comparison with a letter Morris signed in his own name would settle it.
Where to look
The folder “Revelations of Joseph Morris, 1858–1860” and Morris’s signed letters in the same files (CR 1234 1), viewable in the Church History Library catalog; other anonymous letters of 1858–61 in the same series.

What happened at the Farmington court-martial of 1861?

Open
Known
Forscutt’s “Sketch of Joseph Morris” says members of Morris’s First Council, among them John Parson, John and Richard Cook, and Forscutt himself, were summoned before a militia court-martial at Farmington in 1861 for refusing militia training, and that Forscutt was fined $56.80 (Forscutt, Sketch, MS pp. 28, 34, 51). The 1861 territorial militia records checked so far (Utah State Archives, series 2210) contain no trace of it.
Where to look
Records of the Davis Military District of the Nauvoo Legion; Davis County court records; Woodruff’s journal and the Journal History for 1861; any later reference in the 1862 writs or the 1863 trials.

When was the photograph of Morris taken, and who is with him?

Open
Known
Anderson (Revisited, 118) dates the only known photograph of Morris to about 1841, in England, and identifies the man beside him as his older brother William, who came to America only after 1862. Commercial photography reached England only in 1841, and the second man looks much older than Morris, while family trees make William about two years his senior. Others date the picture about 1860.
Where to look
The original image and its owner; Anderson’s research files at USU (Caine MSS 10); Morris family descendants in England and Utah; the photographic process and studio, if a mount or case survives.

Who led the Montana church after George Williams?

Answered
Answer
From the church’s minutes, as Anderson gives them (Revisited) and as the Montana minutebook for 1881–1890 records them (Church History Library, MS 32226): William James, the local president under Williams, sustained on April 25, 1883, with Andrew Hendrikson and John R. Eardley as counselors; George Thompson of London, elected president of the church on April 25, 1886, probably the last general president; Andrew Hendrikson, president of the Montana Morrisites from 1886 until his death on July 3, 1921; and George Johnson, who presided until shortly before his death on April 29, 1954, with which, Anderson writes, the church came to an end (Revisited, pp. 212, 214); USU’s finding aid gives 1969 for its formal disbanding. Eardley, often named as the Montana leader, was a counselor and stake president who left Montana about 1900. The minutebook places both elections at the yearly general assembly on April 25, the anniversary of Williams’s death; the dates this answer gave before, May 1882 and May 10, 1886, are those of council meetings. The Chronology and the lineage chart now follow this account. Thompson’s standing was already plain in March 1882, when, writing for the dying Williams from Walthamstow, he promised to “confirm” the Montana church’s choice of officers (George Thompson to the Montana brethren, March 23, 1882, Church History Library, MS 31876).
Still open
How far Thompson’s authority reached beyond Montana and the dates of Eardley’s offices; for the years after 1954, see How and when did the Montana church end? Court and Council minutes and the Hendrikson papers in the Morrisite collection at USU (Caine MSS 10) are the place to check.

How and when did the Montana church end?

Open
Known
George Johnson presided at the Lord’s House until shortly before his death on April 29, 1954. Anderson calls him the last official Morrisite leader and dates the end to his death: by the 1940s fewer than a dozen members were active, and when Johnson died the church came to an end and the Lord’s House was boarded up (Revisited, pp. 212, 214). Surviving Morrisites, all of the second or third generation, still gathered at the Lord’s House on August 9, 1969, the anniversary of its dedication and one of the church’s two feast days, though by then the gatherings were more social than religious (p. 218). Anderson’s sources included his own interviews with Henry Hendrikson (1969) and Robert Johnson (1968). The finding aid for the Morrisite collection at Utah State University (Caine MSS 10) says the church officially disbanded in 1969, and the description of Anderson’s papers says that meetings were held irregularly until 1969, when it formally disbanded. Don Cornelius, interviewing in the valley in 1962, found a woman of one of the founding families acting as the “nominal leader” of the Morrisites’ descendants (“Mormonism in Montana,” 1962, p. 65). The local paper, at the time, supports Anderson’s date: Johnson’s obituary calls the church “almost extinct these days” (Silver State Post, May 7, 1954, p. 7); the 1958 obituary of Andrew Hendrickson’s daughter Mary Ann Goldie calls the “House of the Lord Church” no longer active (March 28, 1958, p. 12); and in 1962 the new valley historical society spoke of “the Morrisite church which was located at one time” in the south valley (July 27, 1962, p. 7). See Newspapers.
What conflicts
Small details differ even in the papers: a 1954 feature has Johnson reaching Montana “early in 1874,” his obituary and a 1956 article 1873. Henry Hendrickson, who had left the valley in 1908, told Cornelius in 1962 that the church faded about 1919–20 (interview, April 19, 1962, transcript pp. 8–10, 18; see Repositories), and Cornelius has the movement dying with Andrew Hendrikson and George Johnson, though he misdates Johnson’s death to the early 1930s. The two dates may describe different things: Anderson’s 1954 marks the end of the church as a led and worshipping body, while the 1969 of the USU descriptions may rest on the last of the feast-day gatherings, or on some formal act not yet found. No record made at the time has yet been seen for this guide: who, if anyone, presided after 1954, how often the survivors met, and what, if anything, formally ended the church are all unknown.
Where to look
The Morrisite collection at USU (Caine MSS 10), whose dates run to 1975, and Anderson’s papers there, including his 1968–69 interviews; the Deer Lodge Silver State Post (online for 1893–1963 through Newspapers.com), where Johnson’s obituary has now been found, for any report of meetings between 1954 and 1963; the Butte Montana Standard; local papers around August 1969, on microfilm at the Montana Historical Society; Powell County deed records for the Lord’s House land; and family memories, which descendants are invited to share.

Questions of interpretation

Where did the Morrisite teaching of reincarnation come from?

Open
Why it matters
Morris, Williams, Davies and the Doves all taught that spirits return to the earth in new bodies. The Latter-day Saint church never adopted the doctrine, but it was not unheard of among Latter-day Saints in the 1850s: Kirk Watson notes that the apostle Orson Hyde taught a form of it from 1846 to 1850, that Brigham Young and Wilford Woodruff later blamed Hyde when a belief called “baby resurrection” spread among church members in the 1850s, and that it was discussed in Sanpete stake meetings, where Morris may have met it, and that it is arguably implicit in the Nauvoo teaching that people may become gods. Where the Morrisites got it bears on how early Mormon ideas were carried and reshaped by dissenters.
What has been proposed
C. LeRoy Anderson suggested that Morris took the idea from Charles B. Thompson, leader of an earlier Latter Day Saint offshoot, reasoning that it had “no counterpart in orthodox Mormon theology.” Kirk Watson disputes this: the idea was circulating among Latter-day Saints in the 1850s; Anderson assumes Morris held it from the early 1850s, while the written record, including Joseph Dove’s understanding, says Morris “received it by revelation in 1859”; the two men’s theologies differ widely, Morris building on Nauvoo teaching about God that Thompson opposed, though both dwell on the patriarchs of Genesis; and no contact between them is known, as Anderson himself acknowledged, nor does either mention the other (Watson, correspondence with the editor, November 2026).
In Davies’s community
Sworn testimony in the 1880 suit against William W. Davies shows how fully his branch held the doctrine. Witnesses on both sides described it: V. D. Lambert said Davies’s missionaries in San Francisco were teaching “the reincarnation of the souls” by 1868, that Davies was presented as Adam, Abraham, Noah and David returned, and that members who died would come back in new bodies and remain in the Kingdom; Christian Miller, still a member, said “the Spirit leaves the body at death & returns and is reincarnated”; and S. W. Wood listed reincarnation among the community’s principles. Davies himself testified that “the doctrine of reincarnation is taught and believed,” and five more members on his side, among them Broer Sorenson of the 1861–62 roll, described the same belief: that the soul lives on after death and returns to the earth in another body. Peter C. Klemgard, who had been at Kington Fort, told the court that the doctrine taught at the Davies camp was “not like the Morrisite religion” (Cilley, Evans and Courtney v. Davies, 1880–81, Washington State Archives; see Repositories).
Other claimants
Similar ideas appear in groups with no known Morrisite tie. James Brighouse seems to have taught reincarnation only after he left Missouri and returned to Utah (see the chronology under 1884). Israel A. J. Dennis (born 1852), a former Methodist holiness preacher and Seventh-day Adventist from Illinois, came to believe about 1881 that he was the reincarnation of the biblical Jacob, or Israel; he was briefly a member of the RLDS Church, organized a “Church of the First Born” in Kansas in 1889, joined the Latter-day Saints in Utah in 1890, and refounded his Church of the First Born in 1895. Watson finds several possible sources for that name, among them an Illinois sect that believed in reincarnation, a faith-healing church in New Jersey, and the Morrisites, but no evidence of contact with the Morrisites (Watson, “Newsie Messiah,” JWHA, 2024, and correspondence).
In the later church
George Williams’s St. Ann’s Hill records describe the incarnations and reincarnations of divine beings (Watson, pp. 96–110), and in 1869 he told converts at Omaha and Council Bluffs who they had been in earlier lives (see Daybell, above). Yet Gems of Inspiration (1899), the later church’s own selection of Morris’s and Williams’s writings, never uses the word. James Dove’s Treatise on Re-Incarnation or Re-Embodiment (San Francisco, 1892), reprinted by Watson (pp. 208–64), is in Watson’s judgment the earliest book on the subject in the wider Mormon tradition (p. 199). See Teachings.
Where to start
The Spirit Prevails; Kirk Watson’s edition, including Davies’s 1882 interview and the Doves’ Treatise on Re-Incarnation (1892); the Scriptural Mormonism episode of November 14, 2026; reports of reincarnation teaching among Latter-day Saints in Nauvoo and 1850s Utah; Spiritualism in Britain and America in the 1850s and 1860s.

Is there a line from Morrisite teaching to Chad Daybell’s?

Open
Why it matters
Chad Daybell, an Idaho author of Latter-day Saint end-times novels, was convicted in 2024 of three murders committed in 2019 and sentenced to death. Prosecutors said he used apocalyptic prophecy and stories of possession by evil spirits to justify the killings (Associated Press, June 1, 2024). Some of what he taught resembles Morrisite teaching, and the two are now compared in print and online. Whether the likeness means descent or only a shared background bears on how ideas travel along the margins of the Latter-day Saint tradition.
Likenesses
Earlier lives revealed by a seer. In 1869 Williams told converts at Council Bluffs and Omaha who they had been. Carlton Fellows had been Jonas, father of Simon Peter, and Maren Fellows had been Abigail, who “after Nabal’s death was married to King David” (record book, 1869, Church of the Firstborn (Morrisites) records, MS 31876, Church History Library; translation from the Danish generated by Claude). In January 2019 Daybell sent a document listing past lives for Lori Vallow, among them the wife of the prophet Moroni, and saying that he had been James the Just, married to her (FOX 10 Phoenix). Marriage across lives. A friend of Vallow’s testified that the two told her they had been married in a previous life (NPR, May 30, 2024). The name “Church of the Firstborn.” The Montana records are catalogued under it, and it was used in Daybell’s circle. Both took it from Latter-day Saint scripture (Doctrine and Covenants 76:54, 76:94, 93:22), and Israel A. J. Dennis’s Church of the First Born, above, shows how readily the name could arise on its own. An end close at hand. The Morrisites at Kington Fort awaited deliverance, and Sven Olson Hagg wrote in 1866 that their three and a half years of waiting were ending (see the chronology under 1865–66); Daybell, too, foretold an imminent end.
Differences
Daybell rated people on a scale of light and dark and taught that a person overcome by a dark spirit became a “zombie” whose body had to die (NPR, May 30, 2024). Christopher Blythe, who studies Latter-day Saint apocalypticism, has said he never saw that idea promoted in any Mormon group, nor “an LDS account that claimed someone’s identity was permanently taken over” (interview with Jana Riess, Religion News Service, August 18, 2020). Nothing of the kind appears in the Morrisite records read for this guide. In 1882 George Thompson, writing for the dying Williams, warned the Montana Saints not to “pray too much or they will invite foul spirits around them” (MS 31876, folder 9), and the Montana councils met defiance with trials, exclusion and shunning (see the chronology under 1873). The violence in Morrisite history was done to the Morrisites, at Kington Fort.
What has been proposed
No source known to the editor shows Daybell drawing on Morris, Williams or Davies. Blythe traces his ideas to a modern prophecy subculture on the margins of the church, which grew out of nineteenth-century visionary culture and drew on visionaries such as Julie Rowe, Michael Stroud and Douglas Mendenhall (Blythe, “The Daybells and Latter-day Saint Apocalypticism,” Sightings, February 27, 2020, and the Riess interview). The phrase “multiple mortal probations,” used for past lives in Daybell’s circle, is current among some Mormon fundamentalist groups (Cristina Rosetti, quoted by FOX 10 Phoenix). Any common ground with the Morrisites probably lies further back, in the reincarnation ideas that circulated among Latter-day Saints in the 1840s and 1850s (see the question above). From there the paths divide: Williams’s teaching about earlier lives stayed doctrinal, while Daybell’s merged with a demonology of possession.
Where to start
Christopher James Blythe, Terrible Revolution: Latter-day Saints and the American Apocalypse (Oxford University Press, 2020); the 1869 record book in MS 31876 (see Repositories); reporting of the 2023 and 2024 trials in Boise; Rosetti’s work on Mormon fundamentalist theology.

Did the Prophet Cainan read birthrights by phrenology?

Open
The page
A one-page typescript transcription in the Morrisite collection at Utah State University (Caine Coll. MSS 10, box 3, folder 7, p. 29) is a fragment of a letter whose writer, date and recipient are lost. A note written on it says it “does not follow logically” from the page before, a general invitation dated Soda Springs, August 12, 1863, to “the scattered sheep of Joseph to come to meet the Prophet of the Lord.” The fragment says that the Prophet was staying at Cottonwood, incognito, until the next spring. A Brother Smith visited him there; the Prophet put his hand on Smith’s head, “felt it Phrenologically,” and told him he was a High Priest. Niel Beck later visited him the same way and was told he was an Aaronic Priest. The page says that knowledge of men’s birthrights now came by inspiration, but that in future “all will know by science,” and that no descendant of Cain would be allowed into the Church. It looks ahead to about twenty years of preaching before the coming of Moses and of Christ. Kirk Watson brought the page to the guide’s attention.
Who was the Prophet?
The page does not name him. Watson identifies him as George Williams, the Prophet Cainan: the page was filed with the invitation, which USU’s indexes call “Cainan to Scattered Sheep of Joseph”; “Joseph” and the coming of Moses, whom the Morrisites identified with Morris, fit Cainan’s teaching rather than Joseph Smith’s; Cainan called himself a prophet; and Anderson describes him as living in secret. Cainan stayed in the Salt Lake area until 1869 (Anderson, Revisited, p. 168). Watson also notes a vision Cainan is said to have had in Little Cottonwood Canyon, which may or may not bear on the page’s “Cottonwood” (Watson, correspondence with the editor, November 2026).
Who was meant by Cain?
In Brigham Young’s Utah, “seed of Cain” usually meant people of African descent, and Williams had been a Latter-day Saint. But Morris taught that Cain was the son of the serpent, “Lucifer, the devil,” and a fallen angel, that a third of Adam’s children belong to Cain, and that Cain “claims the right to enter into the Church” (The Spirit Prevails, pp. 12–13, 448, 660–61, 664). The page’s promise reads like an answer to that claim, and its appeal to phrenology and “science” fits Morris’s meaning: if spirits return in new bodies, a birthright could be read from the body. Watson favors this reading. The Spirit Prevails also links the seed of Cain with “Gentile blood” (pp. 651–52), so a racial sense in how the teaching was heard cannot be ruled out.
Why it matters
Phrenology was widely popular in the 1840s, and Latter-day Saint leaders were divided over it, but Cainan may be the only Latter Day Saint leader known to have made it part of his religion. If Niel Beck is Niels R. Beck of Deer Lodge, the page is also the earliest known link between him and the Morrisite leadership, years before he sent money to William W. Davies, who testified in 1881 that “Mr. Beck is not a member.”
Where to start
The page and its neighbors in Caine MSS 10, box 3, folder 7 (the fragment and the 1863 invitation filed with it are printed in Kirk Watson’s edition, pp. 90–91), with the collection’s item indexes, which are available from USU Special Collections; Cainan’s letters and revelations in the same collection; Anderson’s chapters on Williams; The Spirit Prevails on Cain; the literature on phrenology among Latter Day Saints (a starting point is Wikipedia’s overview).

How did believers survive failed prophecy?

Open
Why it matters
Morris set days for the Second Coming that passed; he was killed in the siege; Williams told the Montana Saints that the City of the great King would come down in their region, “the Sides of the North”; Davies proclaimed his infant son the Messiah. Yet the movement lasted more than ninety years. Who stayed, who left, and how they explained what happened are open questions.
First findings
The revelations show how the first disappointment was explained. On December 30, 1861, Christ promised to come “to-morrow morning” if those present consecrated all their property (The Spirit Prevails, Nos. 188–189, pp. 338–39). When the morning passed, a revelation of January 6, 1862, blamed those who had consecrated unwillingly; one of January 11 refused to make any further promise about the time; and in February deliverance was promised after the people had taken an oath (Nos. 195, 199 and 224, pp. 350, 359, 419). In June 1862 an angel speaking in tongues told the camp that on his own earth the faithful had also set dates and been disappointed, and that the error lay not in setting them but in refusing to give them up (Watson, pp. 76–77). Williams’s letter of January 6, 1879, set no date at all. See Teachings.
Where to start
The revelations and testimonies of 1862 in Kirk Watson’s edition; Williams’s letters at USU; the Davies material; the literature that began with Festinger, When Prophecy Fails; changes in membership over time (cited, not published as lists).

How did a prophet’s movement become a church run by vote?

Open
Why it matters
Authority passed from Morris’s revelations to Williams’s letters from afar, then to presidents elected at yearly general assemblies (1883, 1886) and to Danish lay leaders who had never been Latter-day Saints. Few movements of the period document that change so closely.
Where to start
Court and Council minutes and the Cainan letters in the Morrisite collection at USU (Caine MSS 10); the Montana minutebook, 1881–1890, and the Cainan letters at the Church History Library (MS 32226 and MS 31876, both online); Anderson’s chapters on the Montana church; the sociology of schism and of the routinization of charisma.

Why were so many Morrisites Scandinavian?

Open
Why it matters
Danish speakers were prominent from the start: Morris’s revelations circulated in Danish, the Omaha and Council Bluffs congregations kept Danish-language records in 1869, and the last Montana leaders were Danish immigrants. Judged by name alone, 168 of the 430 people on the roll have Scandinavian names (Who they were), and in 1889 Joseph Smith III found the old Morrisites at Soda Springs to be mainly Swedes, Norwegians, and Danes (Joseph Smith III and the Restoration, 1952, p. 437). Disappointment after emigration, language, poverty, and patterns of recruitment are all possible explanations.
Where to start
The 1886 roll of membership compared with Scandinavian Mission emigration records; the Danish-language material at USU; emigrant letters and Danish-language newspapers.

What became of the spiritual gifts?

Open
Why it matters
Speaking in tongues, interpreted tongues, and messages from angels were central at Kington Fort, as in the “3rd Captain of the Lord’s Host” testimony of June 1862. An interpretation of tongues of June 7, 1862, teaches that the gifts cease only at the resurrection and charges that a council in Brigham Young’s house decided to put them down (Watson, pp. 70–72). How Morrisite practice compares with the Latter-day Saint church in the 1850s has not been studied closely.
Where to start
Kirk Watson’s edition; The Spirit Prevails; studies of spiritual gifts and the Reformation of 1856–57 in Utah.

Where did the rituals come from?

Open
Why it matters
The scepters, Morris’s robe and crown, the ceremony at Kington Fort in May 1862 that Anderson calls the Foreshadowing Ceremony, the holding of goods in common there, and later communal experiments at Walla Walla and in the Order of Enoch. Their relation to Latter-day Saint temple ritual and to the church’s own communal programs is unexplored.
Where to start
The scepters and their inscriptions (see Repositories); Anderson, Revisited, 116–17; The Spirit Prevails; Davies’s 1881 deposition in Kirk Watson’s edition.

What did Morris teach about marriage, and what did women do in the movement?

Open
Why it matters
Later writers often describe Morris as an opponent of plural marriage; Kirk Watson’s introduction argues that he accepted it as a celestial principle. Women’s part in the gifts, in testimony, and in the community has hardly been studied.
First findings
Morris’s own article on celestial marriage, dated June 4, 1861, falls between the two readings: the biblical law of many wives and concubines was given for this life, “not being a celestial law,” and kings and prophets who had many wives “can have but one sealed to them each for eternity” (The Spirit Prevails, pp. 651–52). An angel’s testimony through Morris in June 1862 calls polygamy true “in its proper time and place” (Watson, p. 78), and it was not practiced at Kington Fort (Watson, p. 8). By 1899 the movement’s own history in Gems of Inspiration condemned polygamy outright, citing the Book of Mormon and the 1890 Manifesto, which may be where the later tradition began. See Teachings.
Where to start
The Spirit Prevails; Kirk Watson’s introduction and sources; Emma Thompson Just’s letters in Agnes Just Reid, Letters of Long Ago; the Anderson plaque and other family accounts.

How has the Morrisite War been remembered?

Open
Why it matters
The descendants’ bronze plaque at Soda Springs, the 2006 monument at South Weber, folk traditions such as the street burial, and the church’s 1903 reply to the Andersons, which blamed the Morrisites, each tell a different story of the same three days.
Where to start
The plaque transcription (Images); the monument text; the 1903 file (CR 1 191); the Newspapers page, whose coverage chart shows when the press returned to the story: the 1877 national stories at Burton’s arrest, the 1891 Salt Lake Tribune letters retelling the siege from the Morrisite side, and the 1907 exchange in the Brisbane Truth; Daniel H. Wells’s 1870 letter in the militia dispute, which printed Acting Governor Fuller’s order for the posse (Documents); oral histories at USU and Weber State University.

How has the Morrisite story been passed down, and where do the common errors come from?

Open
Why it matters
The same errors turn up again and again (see Common errors in print), and tracing them shows how the story has moved. Some begin in the press of 1862, when one telegraphic dispatch was copied across the country and changed as it went. Some come from the partisan retellings of the 1880s and 1890s, on both sides. Some come from family memory, which is often right about people and places and less sure about dates: Eliza Eardley Hardin remembered Willow Glen’s founding in 1881, the newspapers first mention it in 1882. And some come from scholarship itself, as later works summarized earlier ones without returning to the records; even the federal gazetteer places the Willow Glen post office about a mile from where the 1882 paper put it. Today the story also travels by online video, where short pieces often borrow one another’s descriptions; their errors are those listed in Common errors, above all the claim that Morris broke away over polygamy. Who told the story, to whom, and why are questions for the history of memory as well as of the movement.
Where to start
The Newspapers page, which follows the 1862 dispatch through its reprints; the 1891 Salt Lake Tribune letters and Richard W. Young’s “The Morrisite War” in the Contributor (1889–90); the theses and their sources (Bibliography); oral histories such as the 1962 Hendrickson interview (Repositories); the Hardin reminiscence (SC 788, Montana Historical Society Library & Archives; see Repositories); C. LeRoy Anderson’s notes at USU, which show what he had in hand.

Why did some Morrisites join the RLDS Church?

Open
Why it matters
Mark H. Forscutt joined the Reorganization in Salt Lake City in 1865, and John L. Bear also joined it; John Parson, the president of the Morrisite apostles, was later baptized at Oakland by Joseph Smith III himself, over the protests of some of Smith’s brethren (Joseph Smith III and the Restoration, 1952, p. 432), while other Morrisites returned to the Latter-day Saint church. Others joined later: Robert E. Farley in Nebraska in 1870, and, after years in the Morrisite groups, Erick and Caroline Eliason at Soda Springs in 1870, Margaretta Cook in Nevada in 1873 and Lena Hanson at Deer Lodge in 1895. The Eliasons’ joint baptism at Soda Springs, a Morrisite settlement, suggests the RLDS mission reached the community there directly. Several people on the roll were baptized at Malad, Idaho, in 1865. Smith himself visited the camp site with Parson and stayed with Morrisite families at Soda Springs in 1889. What the Reorganization offered them, and whether its missionaries sought them out, is not well understood.
Where to start
Forscutt’s journals; Bear’s autobiography in the Journal of History; records of the RLDS mission to Utah in the 1860s; Black, Early Members of the Reorganized Church (1993), which cites RLDS branch records and Saints’ Herald obituaries; the later-path dots on the roll; Rogers, “Mark Hill Forscutt” (2001); Joseph Smith III and the Restoration (1952), pp. 432–37.

How did Latter-day Saints, the Reorganized Church and the Morrisites live alongside one another in the Deer Lodge Valley?

Open
Why it matters
For nearly a century three churches that traced themselves to Joseph Smith shared one valley. Latter-day Saint missionaries knew the Morrisite leaders well enough in 1896 to call John R. Eardley “Apostle Eardly” (Wright, 2004, drawing on the Butte District manuscript history). The Reorganized Church was active in Montana from 1868, and some Morrisites joined it, among them Lena Hanson at Deer Lodge in 1895. Hymnals and a 1931 Reorganized Church lesson quarterly were still in the Lord’s House in 2020 (see Images). Whether the three groups competed for members, shared buildings, preachers or burial grounds, married into one another’s families, or simply kept apart, and how that changed as the Morrisite church dwindled toward 1969, has not been studied.
First findings
The Montana council’s minutes for 1881–1890 (Church History Library, MS 32226) give some early answers. In November 1886 an “Elder Clapp of the Reorganized Church” invited the Morrisites to use the Reorganized Church’s meeting house in Deer Lodge City while he was away preaching in Utah, and the council agreed to accept and to announce the meeting “among the Josephites and Morrisites.” This was the church the New North-West had reported in September 1886 as going up under Clapp’s direction on the Cottonwood road east of town (see Newspapers). A year earlier the council had placed a notice in the same paper disowning George and James Dove of the California congregation as “pretenders” (November 13, 1885). In 1888 and 1889 the council discussed John Parson, the former Morrisite apostle, who still held Reorganized Church membership while following a self-styled prophet in Oakland, California, and took it as a sign of waning confidence in “Young Joseph’s prophetic office.” Speakers cited Brigham Young both as a cautionary example and as an authority on running a branch, and in 1889 said Williams’s predictions about “the Utah people” were being fulfilled. The council also kept the sacrament open to non-members who came as “friends for the gospel’s sake.” Among Williams’s own followers, the earliest case found so far is fifteen years older and comes from the Omaha congregation: in May 1871 Sister Marie Mortensen, who had led its sisters’ meetings, “had joined another church, the Josephites, and let herself be baptized by them.” She told the elders she was afraid “young Joseph was the man they said he was,” and chose to risk rejecting Cainan rather than rejecting young Joseph; she gave back her written appointment and was excluded (Omaha minutebook, May 7, 1871, MS 31876; translation from the Danish generated by Claude). George Thompson, the newly elected president, wrote from England in December 1886 that Joseph Smith [III] “may be called through our Joseph,” and that John Parson “may be instrumental” in telling him of “the mission of the fullness,” but that he had “not yet called him” to be a prophet (Church History Library, MS 31876).
Where to start
The Butte District manuscript history of the Northwestern States Mission (Church History Library); Cornelius, “Mormonism in Montana” (1962), ch. 6, and his interviews; Wright, “Mormon Movement to Montana” (2004); RLDS branch records and Saints’ Herald reports from Montana (Community of Christ Archives); the New North-West and Silver State Post; the 1962 Hendrickson interview (Repositories); burials at Hillcrest Cemetery, Deer Lodge; and family memories, which descendants are invited to share.

Where did the Copper Village organ come from?

Open
Why it matters
The Copper Village Museum and Art Center in Anaconda holds a Mason & Hamlin reed (pump) organ said to have come from the Morrisites. If the tradition holds, it is one of the few furnishings of Morrisite worship to survive outside the Lord’s House. Museum volunteers say an accession sheet was probably made when the organ was donated but has not been found: for years donations to the museum and archives went undocumented, and volunteers are only now putting the collections in order. A visiting organist who played it at the museum on August 3, 2013, describes it as a Mason & Hamlin parlor organ built in Boston about 1892 (“BIRD: Auf dem Lande, on Mason & Hamlin parlor organ,” YouTube, posted August 9, 2013). If that date is right, the organ did not come from Utah and was not bought when the Lord’s House was built in 1878–79. It could still have belonged to the church or to a Morrisite family later.
Where to start
The museum’s accession files (Marcus Daly Museum and Archives, through the director of the Copper Village Museum and Art Center; see Repositories); the organ’s serial number, which can date it from Mason & Hamlin’s production records; the Montana council’s minutes (Church History Library, MS 32226) and later church records, for any purchase or gift of an organ; and family memories, which descendants are invited to share.

Was Gwendale named for Gwenllian Evans?

Open
Why it matters
Gwendale was the post office of the upper Deer Lodge Valley, near Warm Springs and Lost Creek, where Morrisite settlers such as W. P. Thomas lived; in 1878 it was renamed Morristown because, the New North-West said, many of its people were Morrisites (see Deer Lodge Valley). The Evans family called its homestead Gwendale Farm (In the Shadow of Mount Haggin, p. 134). The Montana Historical Society’s Women’s History Matters calls Gwenllian Evans, Morgan Evans’s mother, Montana’s first woman homesteader and “one of the territory’s first post mistresses” (“A Farm of Her Own,” May 20, 2014). The name looks like hers, and she may have kept the Gwendale office, but no record seen so far says either.
Where to start
The Post Office Department’s Record of Appointment of Postmasters, 1832–1971 (National Archives, Record Group 28; microfilm M841), for Deer Lodge County; the department’s site reports for Gwendale and Morristown; the New North-West; and the sources behind the Women’s History Matters essay.

Were the Evans and Thomas families of Gwendale connected to the Morrisites?

Open
Why it matters
Morgan Evans and William P. Thomas were Gwendale election judges together in 1876, and in 1880 their homestead claims were among the four held up by the land office (see the roll note for Thomas, W. P., probably the Morrisite of that name). The Evanses were Welsh, like William James, the Morrisites’ local president. The family account has them in Utah for about eight years before they came to the valley in 1865 (In the Shadow of Mount Haggin, p. 134), but Women’s History Matters says Gwenllian Evans emigrated to the United States in 1868, and her obituary, a clipping shared by a reader whose newspaper and date are not yet identified, says she came to Montana in 1869. Neither family history calls the Evanses Morrisites. The same obituary says “the late Mrs. E. P. Thomas” was her daughter. Evan Price Thomas (1845–1891), a rancher on Lost Creek, and his wife were killed by lightning in 1891 (Find a Grave memorial 106925533). Whether he was related to W. P. Thomas is not known.
Where to start
The Evans and Thomas homestead case files (National Archives, Record Group 49); the 1870 and 1880 censuses of Deer Lodge County; the Montana church’s Danish minutes and council minutes (Church History Library, MS 31876 and MS 32226), for the names Evans and Thomas; Latter-day Saint records of the Evanses in Utah before 1865; and family records, which descendants are invited to share.

When was Arthur Davies first called the “Walla Walla Jesus”?

Open
Why it matters
“Walla Walla Jesus” is the name most readers know the Davies story by, and the guide uses it (see the glossary). But the sources seen so far that use it are late: the Washington State Archives’ index to case 2373, Zbaraschuk’s 1989 paper and the Fort Walla Walla Museum’s collection description. Davies himself called the boy “Messiah Son of David” (Watson, p. 192), and the Carson Valley News in 1877 spoke of a child at Walla Walla who was to be the Savior, without the nickname. Whether neighbors used the name while the community lived, or whether it was coined later, changes how it should be presented: as a period name, or as a later label.
Where to start
Walla Walla newspapers of 1868–1882, especially the reports of the boys’ deaths in February 1880 and of the 1880–81 lawsuit; the depositions in case 2373 (Washington State Archives, Eastern Region Branch); the reprints of the 1877 Nevada story in other papers; Frank T. Gilbert’s Historic Sketches of Walla Walla (1882); and the clippings in the William W. Davies collection at the Fort Walla Walla Museum (see Repositories).
