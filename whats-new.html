New find. Kirk found two revelations of Morris, dated January 14 and 16, 1860, filed among anonymous letters in Brigham Young’s office papers, where a clerk docketed one “Balderdash.” He identified the hand as Morris’s and shared his transcription. Archivist · Washington State Archives

Lee Pierce and the Walla Walla court files

For decades the story in Walla Walla was that the court records of the lawsuits against William W. Davies had gone missing. A researcher who asked for them at the county courthouse was told they had been checked out, and he never found them. They were safe all along in the Washington State Archives, whose Eastern Region Branch in Cheney holds the territorial court records for most of the state’s eleven eastern counties.

Lee Pierce, an archivist at the branch, answered the editor’s request the next morning with page counts and costs for both files, and the same afternoon he scanned and sent the whole 1885 bank suit and most of the 292-page 1880 case. He also supplied the archives’ preferred citation, so the files can be cited properly. They hold sworn testimony from men and women who lived in Davies’s community, printed tracts filed as exhibits, the lists of what members gave, the 1881 judgment and the sheriff’s sales, much of it new to this guide. See Repositories.

When the editor found a gap in the page numbers, Lee checked again: the whole file had been scanned and sent, and pages 133–157 seem to have been missing before it ever reached the archives. He suspects they went the way of pages 158–195, which turned up at Whitman College and were returned, and wonders whether this is what lay behind the clerk’s report that the records had been “checked out.” As he points out, territorial court files are permanent records that should never have left the clerk’s custody, so the missing pages may yet be found.

Work like this rarely gets public credit, and guides like this one depend on it. Thank you, Lee.

From the families

How a descendant filled in the Eliason story

Keith Chappelow, a descendant of Erik and Karin Eliasson, wrote to the editor with what his family knew. He sent the family’s account of the Eliasons at Soda Springs and Racetrack, pages from Joseph Smith III and the Restoration (1952), a family group sheet, and pages from a 1989 history of Powell County, Montana.

That led to Family Tree links for Erik and Karin; their 1870 baptisms into the RLDS Church; their daughter Anna, whom an early county history calls the first white child born at Soda Springs; Erik’s brother Elias, who came to Soda Springs in 1863 but is not on the roll; and Joseph Smith III’s 1889 visit to the old camp at Weber with John Parson. See the pages on Erik and Karin.

From the community · local history groups

What local history groups added

In November the editor shared this guide in local history groups on Facebook in places where the Morrisites settled, beginning with Deer Lodge, Montana, and Walla Walla, Washington. Within a day the replies were changing the guide.

Deer Lodge. A reader who once worked for a Soren Beck near Avon pointed the way to a 1902 biography of Soren R. Beck, who came to work on Niels R. Beck’s ranch in 1884 and was probably his nephew. A great-grandson of George Johnson, the last official leader of the Montana Morrisites, who preached at the Lord’s House near Racetrack until 1954, joined the conversation. So did descendants of families who built the church and worshiped there, neighbors who want to see it preserved, and a volunteer at the Deer Lodge County museum, which holds a pump organ said to have come from the Morrisites.

Walla Walla. A reader asked whether the editor had checked Whitman College and recalled that the court records of the lawsuit against William W. Davies had gone missing from the county courthouse. Following up led to the case file in the Washington State Archives and to Whitman’s copies of depositions and an 1882 photograph of the Davies family and home. See Repositories.

Since then. A reader in Deer Lodge contributed ten photographs of the Lord’s House, inside and out, taken in 2020 (see Images), and readers in Salmon, Idaho, told the story of Lars Christian Geertsen’s log house, a National Register landmark, damaged by fire and torn down in 2025.

Thank you to everyone who has commented, shared and written. If your family or town has a Morrisite story, a photograph or an old record, share what you have. Nothing is published without your permission, and people are named only if they wish.

The translations of the Danish minutes, the 1869 record book and the Omaha minutebook quoted in this guide were generated by Claude, Anthropic’s AI model, from the Church History Library’s page images (MS 31876).     2. 10 Nov 2026Omaha in 1871. The last pages of the Omaha minutebook show the congregation giving up Latter-day Saint-style conferences in March 1871, a leader of its sisters, Marie Mortensen, joining the Reorganized Church that May (the earliest such case yet found among Williams’s followers), and the book closing on June 4, 1871, as members prepared to move west. Its companion record book of 1869 records the earlier lives Williams assigned to the men he anointed. See Omaha & Council Bluffs under Places, the RLDS question under Questions, and Reincarnation in the Glossary.
    3. 10 Nov 2026The Omaha minutebook. The Danish minutebook of the Omaha and Council Bluffs church (Church History Library, MS 31876) tells how it was organized in February 1869, while George Williams passed through Omaha on his way to England, and names the Welsh missionary who went with William P. Thomas in 1869 as Daniel James, settling the question corrected earlier today. See Omaha & Council Bluffs under Places, the Chronology, and the roll notes for Rasmus Rasmusson, S. P. Guhl and W. P. Thomas.
    4. 10 Nov 2026The Lord’s House dedicated August 9, 1879. The Danish council minutes of the Race Track stake (Church History Library, MS 31876) date the dedication of the Lord’s House: in July 1882 the council planned to celebrate August 9 as the third anniversary of its dedication. They also show the council keeping February 14 as Williams’s birthday, and the Danish members’ debate before the 1883 vote. Added to the Chronology and the Glossary.
    5. 10 Nov 2026A correction: which James went to England. Earlier today the Chronology said that William James went with William P. Thomas to join Williams in England in 1869–70. George Thompson’s letter of July 29, 1869, addresses the two as “W. P. and D. J.,” so the Brother James was probably Daniel James, not William M. James; the entry is corrected. Thompson’s letters to Thomas (Church History Library, MS 31876) also place Thompson with Williams at Ashwicken in 1869 and give Williams’s age and the cause of his death, now in the Chronology and the Glossary.
    6. 10 Nov 2026Williams’s letters to Andrew Hendrikson. Williams’s letters of 1869–73 to Hendrikson and the Omaha church (Church History Library, MS 31876) show that William P. Thomas and a Brother James went to England to be with him and returned to Montana in 1870, give Williams’s own account of Almerin Grow’s mission to Constantinople and Jerusalem, and trace the move from Omaha to Montana in 1871–72. Added to the Chronology and to the roll notes for Rasmus Rasmusson, John Merrick and S. P. Guhl; John Merrick’s identification is raised from “Check” to “Probable.”
    7. 10 Nov 2026Williams in England, 1869–73. Williams’s letters of 1870–73 in the Church History Library (MS 31876) show that he left America about January 1869, leaving his family in Salt Lake City, and that his “Ashby” was Ashby by Brigg, Lincolnshire; they also name his daughter Adelaide. In 1873 he called “Burton and Lot Smith” “the murderers at Weber”; whether Lot Smith was at the siege is a new open question under Questions.
    8. 10 Nov 2026A correction: Montana by 1868. The Glossary said the Montana church regathered in the Deer Lodge Valley in the 1870s. A letter of George Williams dated December 14, 1868, is addressed to “Bro. James & Saints of the Most High in Montana,” so William James’s group was there, under that name, by 1868 (Church History Library, MS 31876). The Glossary is corrected and the Chronology has a new 1868 entry, which also notes that Williams was living in George Thompson’s house at Walthamstow by 1876. The roll note for Charles Higham adds his visit to Thompson in England about 1877.
    9. 10 Nov 2026Thompson at Williams’s side. In March 1882, as George Williams lay dying at Walthamstow, George Thompson answered the Montana church’s questions for him, explaining that Williams “was not really Cainan the son of Enos” but represented him, and promising to confirm the church’s choice of officers (Church History Library, MS 31876). Added to the Chronology, the Glossary and the Questions.
    10. 10 Nov 2026Thompson in England. George Thompson, elected president of the church in Montana in April 1886, was writing to the members that December from Thornwood Common, Epping, Essex, open to Joseph Smith III being “called through our Joseph” but not yet calling him a prophet. The letter is in the Church History Library’s Cainan letters (MS 31876) and is now in the Chronology and the Questions. The same letters show John R. Eardley planning, in February 1880, to open a store in Montana (see the roll note).
    11. 10 Nov 2026Williams’s last instructions. The Church History Library’s Cainan letters (MS 31876) hold George Williams’s own note of January 29, 1882, directing that William James be put before the church a year after his death, and an envelope to James inscribed “I leave you in charge after my decease,” the words James later relied on in claiming the presidency for life. Both are now in the Chronology. The same letters add to the roll notes for S. P. Guhl, who disputed James’s authority from Omaha in 1874, and for John Parson, William White and John Merrick, whom John R. Eardley reported on from California in 1875.
    12. 10 Nov 2026A correction: the Montana elections. This guide said, following Anderson, that William James was sustained as president in Montana in May 1882 and that George Thompson was elected president of the church on May 10, 1886. The Montana minutebook at the Church History Library records both elections at the yearly general assembly on April 25, the anniversary of George Williams’s death: James in 1883, by 19 votes to 10, and Thompson in 1886. The May dates are those of council meetings. The Chronology and Questions are corrected.
    13. 10 Nov 2026The Montana church’s own records. The Church History Library’s Church of the Firstborn records, now on the Repositories page and readable online, include the membership record kept from 1874 and the council minutes for 1881–1890. They give the date of Williams’s death in England (April 25, 1882), the two stakes on Warm Spring Creek and at Race Track, the meeting house at Willow Glen, the schisms of 1883 and 1886, the council’s notice in the New North-West of November 13, 1885, disowning the Doves of the California congregation (now on the Newspapers page), and an invitation in 1886 to use the Reorganized Church’s new meeting house in Deer Lodge City, which settles who built the church a newspaper had called Morrisite, now a first finding on one of the open questions. The membership record also strengthens the notes on several names on the roll: Ole Olsen, W. P. Thomas and Erick Eliason, and new notes for William James, Christian Anderson and Rasmus Rasmusson.

Its acknowledgments thank his thesis advisor at Walla Walla College, and the Church History Library catalogs it the same way. The Bibliography is corrected.
