Kirk Watson

New book. The Morrisites: Writings and Revelations (2026), edited by Kirk Watson, collects writings of Joseph Morris, George Williams, William W. Davies, George S. Dove and James Dove, with his introduction. It is the most convenient way to read the movement’s own writings beyond The Spirit Prevails, and buying a copy supports this kind of independent work. Buy it on Amazon (paperback and Kindle).

Upcoming, November 14, 2026, 10:00 a.m. Mountain. “Episode 113: Kirk Watson on Reincarnation in Early Mormonism,” on Robert Boylan’s Scriptural Mormonism podcast, premieres live on YouTube; the conversation includes the Morrisites. Watch on YouTube

New find. Kirk found two revelations of Morris, dated January 14 and 16, 1860, filed among anonymous letters in Brigham Young’s office papers, where a clerk docketed one “Balderdash.” He identified the hand as Morris’s and shared his transcription. They are early forms of revelations later printed in The Spirit Prevails. See Documents.

Archivist · Washington State Archives

Lee Pierce and the Walla Walla court files

For decades the story in Walla Walla was that the court records of the lawsuits against William W. Davies had gone missing. A researcher who asked for them at the county courthouse was told they had been checked out, and he never found them. They were safe all along in the Washington State Archives, whose Eastern Region Branch in Cheney holds the territorial court records for most of the state’s eleven eastern counties.

Lee Pierce, an archivist at the branch, answered the editor’s request the next morning with page counts and costs for both files, and the same afternoon he scanned and sent the whole 1885 bank suit and most of the 292-page 1880 case. He also supplied the archives’ preferred citation, so the files can be cited properly. They hold sworn testimony from men and women who lived in Davies’s community, printed tracts filed as exhibits, the lists of what members gave, the 1881 judgment and the sheriff’s sales, much of it new to this guide. See Repositories.

When the editor found a gap in the page numbers, Lee checked again: the whole file had been scanned and sent, and pages 133–157 seem to have been missing before it ever reached the archives. He suspects they went the way of pages 158–195, which turned up at Whitman College and were returned, and wonders whether this is what lay behind the clerk’s report that the records had been “checked out.” As he points out, territorial court files are permanent records that should never have left the clerk’s custody, so the missing pages may yet be found.

Work like this rarely gets public credit, and guides like this one depend on it. Thank you, Lee.

From the families

How a descendant filled in the Eliason story

Keith Chappelow, a descendant of Erik and Karin Eliasson, wrote to the editor with what his family knew. He sent the family’s account of the Eliasons at Soda Springs and Racetrack, pages from Joseph Smith III and the Restoration (1952), a family group sheet, and pages from a 1989 history of Powell County, Montana.

That led to Family Tree links for Erik and Karin; their 1870 baptisms into the RLDS Church; their daughter Anna, whom an early county history calls the first white child born at Soda Springs; Erik’s brother Elias, who came to Soda Springs in 1863 but is not on the roll; and Joseph Smith III’s 1889 visit to the old camp at Weber with John Parson. See the pages on Erik and Karin.

Is there a Morrisite in your family? Family accounts, letters, photographs, group sheets and local histories can answer questions no archive can. Share what you have; nothing is published without your permission.

From the community · local history groups

What local history groups added

In November the editor shared this guide in local history groups on Facebook in places where the Morrisites settled, beginning with Deer Lodge, Montana, and Walla Walla, Washington. Within a day the replies were changing the guide.

Deer Lodge. A reader who once worked for a Soren Beck near Avon pointed the way to a 1902 biography of Soren R. Beck, who came to work on Niels R. Beck’s ranch in 1884 and was probably his nephew. A great-grandson of George Johnson, the last official leader of the Montana Morrisites, who preached at the Lord’s House near Racetrack until 1954, joined the conversation. So did descendants of families who built the church and worshiped there, neighbors who want to see it preserved, and a volunteer at the Deer Lodge County museum, which holds a pump organ said to have come from the Morrisites.

Walla Walla. A reader asked whether the editor had checked Whitman College and recalled that the court records of the lawsuit against William W. Davies had gone missing from the county courthouse. Following up led to the case file in the Washington State Archives and to Whitman’s copies of depositions and an 1882 photograph of the Davies family and home. See Repositories.

Since then. A reader in Deer Lodge contributed ten photographs of the Lord’s House, inside and out, taken in 2020 (see Images), and readers in Salmon, Idaho, told the story of Lars Christian Geertsen’s log house, a National Register landmark, damaged by fire and torn down in 2025.

Thank you to everyone who has commented, shared and written. If your family or town has a Morrisite story, a photograph or an old record, share what you have. Nothing is published without your permission, and people are named only if they wish.

Kirk Watson, who found it, identifies the prophet as the Prophet Cainan. The page also names a Niel Beck. See the new question of interpretation.
  54. 9 Nov 2026The Racetrack church in 2020. Ten photographs taken inside and outside the Lord’s House on August 16, 2020, contributed by a reader, extend the condition record between 2018 and the videos of 2026. They show the pulpit, benches and table still in place, and the hymnals and a 1931 Reorganized Church lesson quarterly left in the building. See Images. The Bibliography adds In the Shadow of Mount Haggin (1975), on the Evans family at Gwendale, and an Anaconda Standard article of 1935 that Patrick F. Morris cites.
  55. 9 Nov 2026The missing pages of the Davies case. Pages 133–157 of the 1880 case against Davies are not in the file at the Washington State Archives: archivist Lee Pierce confirms the whole file was scanned and that those pages were missing before it reached the archives. They may have left the courthouse with pages 158–195, which Whitman College later found and returned. See Repositories.
  56. 8 Nov 2026Corrections. A check of the guide against its sources led to several fixes. The chronology no longer gives a single death toll for the siege, and now says that John Banks died in custody on the evening of the surrender. The Glossary explains that “Church of the Firstborn” was not the name of Morris’s church in 1861: his revelations call it “my Church,” and Anderson gives the Church of Jesus Christ of Saints of the Most High. The roll no longer lists Joseph Morris’s wives as Mary and Karen: his wife Mary Olsen is probably the roll’s Mary Morris, and Karen Morris is not identified. Notes on Anne Bar now record that the sources disagree on whether her baby died. Citations of The Spirit Prevails have been checked against the 1886 text; the account of the surrender, the trials and the pardons is on its p. 8, not p. 7, and it names the second woman shot at the surrender “Mrs. O’Hagg” (see Who died at Kington Fort?). The counts on Who they were now include all 178 people linked to Family Tree.
  57. 7 Nov 2026Reading the sources. The Bibliography now opens with a note on using the secondary literature and a list of common errors in print, from the siege placed at “Manti” to the street burial, with what the records show. A new question on the Questions page asks how the story has been passed down and where those errors come from.
  58. 7 Nov 2026Two Montana sources. Henry Hendrickson’s 1962 interview on the Deer Lodge Valley Morrisites, now online from the University of Montana, is listed under Repositories, and Julie A. Wright’s 2004 thesis “Mormon Movement to Montana” under Bibliography.
  59. 7 Nov 2026Where Gwendale was. Notices in the New North-West place Gwendale, the Montana settlement renamed Morristown in 1878, in the upper Deer Lodge valley near Warm Springs and Lost Creek; by 1883 its school district was called Willow Glen. Gwendale is now marked on the valley map, with roll notes for W. P. Thomas and William W. Thomas. The four Warm Springs homestead claims named in a March 1880 land-office notice (Morgan Evans, Peter Jessen, Daniel James and W. P. Thomas) were all patented in 1880–81 and are now drawn on the map. The paper also settles the link between the names: in March 1882 it reported John R. Eardley’s appointment as postmaster “at Willow Glen on Warm Spring creek, where the Gwendale postoffice formerly was”; his store stood at “Daniel James’ corner.” See Deer Lodge Valley and Newspapers.
  60. 7 Nov 2026The Deer Lodge paper. Seven items from the New North-West of Deer Lodge (1877–86), free on Chronicling America, are now on the Newspapers page. Among them: a Montana post office renamed Morristown in 1878 because many of its people were Morrisites, a description of Davies’s colony in 1877, and the Doves’ visit to preach at the Deer Lodge courthouse in 1885.
  61. 7 Nov 2026The groups over time. A new timeline in the History chronology shows the Morrisite groups side by side on one time scale, from Morris’s church through Soda Springs, Nevada, Cainan’s claim, Omaha, the Montana church, Davies’s Kingdom of Heaven, Parson’s kingdom and the Doves’ Church of the Firstborn, with their leaders and the movements of members between them.
  62. 7 Nov 2026Witnesses at Burton’s trial. Former Morrisites who testified at Burton’s 1879 trial are now noted on the roll: Mary Christoffersen Anderson and Caroline (Karen) Eliason of Soda Springs, Philip Hewitt of Salt Lake City, Emma Thompson Just of the Snake River valley, and, less certainly, Abraham Taylor and Thomas Williams. Mary Anderson, Emma Just and Philip Hewitt now have an 1879 stop on Where they went.
  63. 7 Nov 2026Fuller’s order for the posse. The Documents page now has the Wells–Shaffer correspondence of October 1870, with the text of Acting Governor Frank Fuller’s order of June 11, 1862, requiring Daniel H. Wells to furnish a military posse for the arrest of Joseph Morris.
  64. 7 Nov 2026Isabella and James Bowman. Isabella Bowman, killed at the surrender and the woman Burton was tried for killing in 1879, is now listed among the others named in the sources, with her husband James, who testified at the trial. James was earlier taken to be the John Bowman of the roll; the sources show they were different men. The timeline now gives both of Burton’s indictments: 1870, voided in 1872, and 1877.
  65. 7 Nov 2026Newspapers. A new Newspapers page lists the press coverage by event, from the Sacramento dispatch of June 16, 1862, and its reprints as far as England and Australia, through the 1870 dispute over who commanded at Kington Fort, Burton’s arrest in 1877 and the Salt Lake Tribune letters of 1891, to recent retellings. It also has tips for searching the newspapers. A first pass through Utah Digital Newspapers added the Salt Lake Herald’s daily reports of Burton’s 1879 trial, where former Morrisites testified and gave their homes at the time, and a 1907 interview with Presto Burrell of Connor’s command, who helped escort the colony to Soda Springs.
  66. 6 Nov 2026Where they went. A new animated map follows the people on the 1861–62 roll from Kington Fort to Soda Springs, the Carson Valley, the Deer Lodge Valley, Walla Walla and beyond, year by year, as censuses, land patents, testimony and death records place them. Choose a family to follow it, or tap a dot for the person’s sources.
  67. 6 Nov 2026Zbaraschuk on Davies, and two more court cases. Tony Zbaraschuk’s 1989 Walla Walla College paper on William W. Davies is now in the Bibliography. Its notes point to two more suits against the Davieses (1882 and 1883), now listed with the Davies case files on Repositories.
  68. 6 Nov 2026A map of Morristown. The Morristown page now maps the 1863 settlement at Soda Springs: the Lower Town (mostly under Alexander Reservoir), the probable site of Camp Connor, the markers, the Andersons’ graves in Fairview Cemetery, and the land patents of the families who stayed. Hover over the markers on the Places map to preview each place’s site map.
  69. 6 Nov 2026Maps of Kington Fort and the Kingdom of Heaven. The Kington Fort page now maps the fort and the siege of June 1862 as G. Q. Jones’s plan and Anderson place them: the monument, the fort’s outline, the bowery, the west gate, the slough by the south gate and the posse’s bluffs. The Kingdom of Heaven page maps William W. Davies’s eighty acres, placed from the legal description in the 1881 sheriff’s sale, and Swen O. Hagg’s homestead. Each site is marked as located, approximate or not yet located.
  70. 6 Nov 2026Places in the Deer Lodge Valley. The Deer Lodge Valley page now has a map and a list of the Morrisite sites in the valley, each marked as located, approximate or not yet located: the Lord’s House at Racetrack, the land entries of Morrisite families between Racetrack and Deer Lodge, William Price Thomas’s 1863 settlement on Warm Springs Creek, and Willow Glen, where a reminiscence in the Montana Historical Society places the post office of 1881 beside “an old log church” and the home of William James. Corrections are welcome.
  71. 6 Nov 2026Anaconda and Willow Glen. Volunteers at the Marcus Daly Museum and Archives in Anaconda shared the museum’s Willow Glen photographs, including the Rasmus Rasmussen family in 1915, and pages from Patrick F. Morris’s history of Anaconda on the first Morrisite settlers in the upper Deer Lodge Valley and the wagon train of 1871. See History under the 1870s, Repositories and the Bibliography.
  72. 6 Nov 2026The “seventh angel” before Morris. Kirk Watson points out that the title Morris claimed was already in Latter-day Saint use: W. W. Phelps’s 1844 funeral sermon for Joseph Smith alludes to him as the seventh angel of Revelation 16, a connection first made by Christopher Blythe, and Doctrine and Covenants 88:112 names “Michael, the seventh angel, even the archangel.” See History under 1884 and the Glossary.
  73. 5 Nov 2026The Walla Walla court files. The Washington State Archives scanned the two lawsuits against William W. Davies for this guide. The 1880 case brought by three former followers ended in a judgment of $3,225 and the sheriff’s sale of the community’s livestock and its eighty acres in 1881. Its depositions add to the record of several people on the 1861–62 roll: Peter C. Klemgard, who lived at Davies’s camp from 1870 to 1872; Swen O. Hagg, who came to Walla Walla with the first five families in 1867; Niels R. Beck, probably the “Neil A. [or R.?] Beck of Montana” who sent money toward the land; and R. J. Livingstone, who left the Carson Valley for Walla Walla in 1877, as the Carson Valley News reported. Witnesses also describe the community’s teaching of reincarnation. Thanks to Lee Pierce of the archives’ Eastern Region Branch. See Repositories, the roll notes and the reincarnation question.
  74. 5 Nov 2026The man who went for help. Lars Christian Geertsen, one of the men the Morrisites held in their log-house jail in 1862, escaped and carried word to Salt Lake City. A Church History Department profile now confirms that he is the same man who settled at Salmon, Idaho, where he ranched at Geertson Creek and built a log house, later listed on the National Register of Historic Places, whose upstairs dance hall served the community into the 1920s. Readers in Salmon pointed the way. See Lars Christian Geertsen.
  75. 5 Nov 2026A 1982 newspaper story. The Montana Standard of June 19, 1982, ran “Deer Lodge Once Thought to Be Site of Second Coming,” with photographs of the Racetrack church; Kris Pozega, a great-great-niece of George Johnson, brought it to light. See the Bibliography.
  76. 4 Nov 2026Where did the reincarnation teaching come from? With help from Kirk Watson, the question now sets out C. LeRoy Anderson’s theory that Morris took the idea from Charles B. Thompson, and the case against it: the idea was already current among Latter-day Saints in the 1850s, and Morris dated it to a revelation of 1859. It also adds Israel A. J. Dennis, who founded a reincarnationist “Church of the First Born” with no known Morrisite tie, and corrects the chronology on James Brighouse, whose ideas have not been traced to the Morrisites. See Questions and History.
  77. 4 Nov 2026What became of them. The later paths of the people on the roll now have their own section on Who they were, beside the paths that brought them into the movement.
  78. 4 Nov 2026The 1880 census. One census page at Walla Walla shows William W. Davies’s Kingdom of Heaven in the summer it began to break up: Davies, a widower, with Sven Hagg in his house and David Park, Ole Hagg, the Sorensons and others around him, months after the deaths of his sons Arthur and David. The same census finds Erik Eliasson farming in the Deer Lodge Valley while his family stayed at Soda Springs, and settles who John Garrett’s wife was. See William W. Davies and Erik Eliasson.
  79. 4 Nov 2026The “Walla Walla Jesus” case. The 1880 lawsuit against Davies, reported missing from the county courthouse, survives in the Washington State Archives as a territorial court case file, and Whitman College holds copies of depositions and an 1882 photograph of the Davies family and home. See Repositories.
  80. 4 Nov 2026Land records. Federal land patents traced for about twenty roll members and their families, from David Park’s claim in the Carson Valley in 1864 to Walla Walla in 1890, with the Deer Lodge Valley, Soda Springs and Blackfoot between. See the notes on the roll and Repositories.
  81. 4 Nov 2026Three new pages. Niels R. Beck, John Garrett and David Park bring the pages on people of the roll to thirty-seven.
  82. 3 Nov 2026The Eliasons and the Christoffersens. A family group sheet and a 1989 Powell County history add Erik Eliasson’s brother Elias, the family’s move to the Deer Lodge Valley, and the Christoffersens’ road north to Montana. See Erik Eliasson.
  83. 3 Nov 2026The prisoners and the trials. The Deseret News named the ninety-eight men brought before Judge Kinney after the siege in June 1862, and the seven convicted of murder in March 1863, with their sentences. The posse lost two men, not one. See Documents and Questions.
  84. 3 Nov 2026Paths into the movement. Who they were now asks what brought Latter-day Saints to Morris: those who reached Utah in 1859–60 joined at about twice the rate of earlier emigrants. The roll now has 150 names linked to FamilySearch, 30 family groups, and notes on 92 people.
  85. 3 Nov 2026Pages on people of the roll. Thirty-four members now have pages of their own, with what the sources say about them and full citations; look for the page tag on the roll.
  86. Nov 2026Documents and the posse order. A new page gathers the primary documents that can be read online, including Acting Governor Fuller’s order of June 11, 1862, calling out the militia. See Documents.
  87. Nov 2026Who they were. The people on the roll counted by sex, language, age, birthplace, arrival in Utah and where they died, with 91 of them read in the 1860 census. See Who they were.
  88. Nov 2026The Racetrack church in photographs. Twenty photographs taken September 5, 1989, courtesy of Barbara B. Walden, Community of Christ Historic Sites Foundation, open a dated record of the church to 2018, with views of South Weber and the cannonballs. See Images.
