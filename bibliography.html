Using the secondary literature

Most of what has been written about the Morrisites rests on a small number of earlier works. Later histories, theses and articles often take their account of 1862 from Anderson, from the 1880s and 1890s retellings in the Salt Lake Tribune and the Contributor, or from one another, so an error made early can travel a long way. The theses listed below are valuable for what their authors saw or heard themselves, such as Don Cornelius’s 1962 interviews in Montana and Julie A. Wright’s use of mission records, and weaker where they summarize events at second hand. Where a fact matters, follow it back to a record made at the time: the court records, the cemetery sexton’s book, the 1886 roll, land and census records, and the newspapers, read in the original rather than in reprints. This guide is held to the same rule; it has its own unsettled points, which are listed on the Questions page, and corrections are welcome.

Common errors in print

Claims that recur in published and unpublished accounts, with what the records show and where to check. They are listed to help readers, not to fault the authors, most of whom were relying on what they had been told.

  • The siege at “Manti” or “Knight Fort.” The siege of June 13–15, 1862, took place at Kington Fort, the Morrisites’ settlement on the Weber River at South Weber, Weber County, Utah. “Kingston Fort” is a common spelling of the same place. See Kington Fort.
  • A single, settled death toll. Accounts differ on how many died and on some of their names and dates; no list has yet been checked against all the sources. See Who died at Kington Fort?
  • One posse man killed. The posse lost two: Jared Smith on June 13 and Peter Wahler on June 15. The sexton’s record lists Wahler, as “Walden,” next to Morris.
  • One account of how Morris died. Morris was killed at the surrender on June 15, but the Morrisite and posse accounts of the moment differ. Read them side by side rather than taking one as fact.
  • Morris buried beneath a street. The Salt Lake City Cemetery sexton’s record lists him as no. 1469, June 15, 1862, in the pauper ground; the street story has no published source yet. See Where is Joseph Morris buried?
  • Mark H. Forscutt as an RLDS apostle. Forscutt was an apostle in Morris’s church and clerk of its First Council; in the Reorganized Church he was a minister.
  • Dates and names taken from reprints. The first dispatch from Salt Lake City, of June 16, 1862, was reprinted across the country, and its wording drifted as it was copied: “the prophet Moses” became “Prophet Morris,” and “the 15th” became “the 5th.” Cite the earliest printing. See Newspapers.
  • The Montana church ending with George Williams, or with no leaders after him. It was led by William James, George Thompson (from 1886), Andrew Hendrikson (1886–1921) and George Johnson (1921–1954), and disbanded in 1969. John R. Eardley, called an apostle in 1861, was still called “Apostle Eardly” by Latter-day Saint missionaries in 1896.

Some differences are matters of memory rather than error, and are noted where they arise: the Lord’s House near Racetrack is dated 1877, 1878 and 1879 by different sources, and Willow Glen’s founding is remembered as 1881 but first appears in the papers in 1882 (see Deer Lodge Valley). Morgan Evans’s family remembered coming to the valley in 1865 (In the Shadow of Mount Haggin, p. 134); Patrick F. Morris dates his arrival to 1866 (Anaconda, Montana, p. 16). Both rest on later accounts, and no record from the time has yet been found to settle it. The family account also has Morgan Evans freighting from “Corrine, Utah, then the terminus of the Union Pacific Railroad” before the family came; Corinne was founded only in 1869, when the railroad reached Utah, so at least that detail belongs to a later time than 1865. The same family account has the Evanses living in a dugout near Gregson Springs until the spring of 1875, ten years after their arrival. That long stay is not confirmed, but it fits the land record: Evans received the patent for his homestead in 1881, after his claim had been held up in 1880 (see the Gwendale entry on the Deer Lodge Valley map).

Some differences come from changed boundaries. Racetrack lay in Deer Lodge County when the Lord’s House was built in 1877, and stayed there until 1901, when Powell County was created from Deer Lodge County. Records from before 1901 therefore place the church in Deer Lodge County, and so do this guide’s photograph captions, which keep the county the church was built in. Today it is in Powell County, as the place descriptions say. Searches for Racetrack and the lower valley should cover both counties.

  • Cornelius, D. E. “Mormonism in Montana, 1847–1898.” Master’s thesis, Montana State University (now the University of Montana), 1962. Chapter 6, “Mormon Schismatics in Montana” (pp. 56–66), treats the Reorganized Church in Montana from 1868 and the Deer Lodge Valley Morrisites, drawing on his 1962 interviews with Henry Hendrickson and Agnes Staffanson and on Reorganized Church records. It dates the Lost Creek church to 1872 and the Race Track church to 1878, and describes the Lord’s House as it stood in 1962. Its account of the Utah years is secondhand and calls Kington Fort “Knight Fort.” His interview with Henry Hendrickson is listed under Repositories. Online
  • Halford, Larry Joseph. “Mormons and Morrisites: A Study in the Sociology of Conflict.” PhD diss., University of Montana, 1972. Directed by C. LeRoy Anderson. Record (campus access only)

  • Wright, Julie A. “Mormon Movement to Montana.” Master’s thesis, University of Montana, 2004. Pages 33–38 treat the Montana Morrisites and the RLDS. Drawing on the Butte District manuscript history of the Northwestern States Mission (Church History Library), it reports Latter-day Saint missionaries’ visits to the Morrisites at Willow Glen in 1896 and at Racetrack about a year later. It also summarizes a family history of Niels R. Beck (see his page). Its brief account of 1862 places the siege at Manti; it took place at Kington Fort on the Weber River. Online
  • Zbaraschuk, Tony. “The Fall of the Standard: William W.
